Chapter 30: Concurrency: async and await in Plain Words

Section 1: What Concurrency Means in Plain Words

Concurrency is a way of describing work that can overlap in time without necessarily happening at the exact same instant. Imagine you are cooking dinner while a washing machine runs in another room. You are not stirring a pot and loading clothes with the same hands at the same moment, but both jobs move forward during the same evening. Your attention switches between them, and the overall result is ready sooner than if you waited for each job to finish completely before starting the next one. Swift concurrency works in a similar spirit. It lets your program start a slow job, such as downloading data, and do other useful work while it waits.

It is important not to confuse concurrency with doing two things at literally the same time on two different processors. That stricter idea is called parallelism, and Swift can do that too, but concurrency is broader. Concurrency is mainly about managing waiting well. Most apps spend a lot of time waiting: waiting for a network reply, waiting for a file to load, waiting for the user to tap a button, or waiting for a database to answer. Without concurrency, waiting freezes everything. With concurrency, waiting becomes a pause for one piece of work, not a stop for the whole app.

Think of a small shop with one worker. If that worker stands at the door waiting for a delivery, no customers can be served. A smarter shop lets the worker take orders, answer questions, and tidy shelves while the delivery is on its way, then handles the delivery when it arrives. Swift gives you tools to organise your code in that smarter way. You mark work that may pause, you wait for it in a clear place, and the system takes care of switching to other work in between. You do not have to manage threads by hand in most cases.

This chapter introduces the main tools step by step. You will learn the words async and await, which mark pausing work and waiting for it. You will learn Task, which represents a piece of concurrent work, and TaskGroup, which manages several pieces at once. You will learn actors, which protect shared data, and MainActor, which protects the user interface. You will also learn about data races, async sequences, testing, and the mistakes beginners make most often. By the end, concurrency should feel less like magic and more like a tidy way to organise waiting.

The key mental picture to keep is simple: concurrency is not about speed for its own sake. It is about responsiveness and good manners. A responsive app keeps answering taps, keeps scrolling smoothly, and keeps showing progress while slow work happens in the background. That is the goal of every pattern in this chapter. When you write concurrent code well, the user feels the difference even if they never learn the words async or Task. They simply notice that the app feels alive, honest about progress, and never stuck for no visible reason.

Section 2: Why Your App Freezes Without Concurrency

An app freezes when its main thread is busy doing slow work instead of responding to the user. The main thread is the thread that draws the screen, handles taps, and keeps animations moving. If you ask it to download a large file, read a huge document, or wait for a slow server, it cannot do those interface jobs at the same time. The screen stops updating, buttons feel dead, and the spinning indicator may not even spin. The user cannot tell whether the app is working hard or has crashed, and that uncertainty is frustrating.

Many beginners freeze apps by accident because slow code looks innocent. A single line that fetches data from the internet may take seconds, and on a poor connection it may take much longer. A loop that processes thousands of items may also take seconds. In a small test with fast internet and a fast device, the delay may be tiny and easy to miss. On a real device, with a real network and real data, the same code can block the interface for a painful length of time. Good programmers therefore treat any slow or unpredictable work as a candidate for concurrency, even when it feels fast in a test.

Concurrency solves freezing by moving waiting off the main thread, or at least by suspending work so the main thread can continue. When a task awaits a network reply, it pauses without blocking the thread underneath it. The system can then use that thread for other work, including drawing the interface and handling taps. When the reply arrives, the paused task resumes where it left off. From the user's point of view, the app stays alive: a loading message appears, the screen can still scroll, and a cancel button still works. That feeling of control matters as much as raw speed.

It also helps to think about what users expect. Modern apps are judged by how they behave under stress, not only by how they behave in perfect conditions. A user on a slow train connection, with an older phone and a busy server, still expects the app to respond. If your app freezes, the user may force-close it, leave a poor review, or simply stop trusting it. Concurrency is therefore not an advanced decoration. It is part of basic craftsmanship for any app that touches the network, files, or large amounts of data. Learning it early saves you from rewriting large parts of an app later.

In this chapter you will practise moving slow work into async functions and tasks. You will see how to keep the interface thread free, how to show loading states honestly, and how to cancel work the user no longer wants. Those habits protect the user experience more than any clever optimisation. An app that is a little slower but always responsive feels better than an app that is fast sometimes and frozen at other times. Responsiveness is trust, and concurrency is one of the main ways Swift helps you earn it.

    print("Loading...")

Section 3: The async Keyword Explained Simply

The word async in Swift is a marker. It tells Swift, and tells anyone reading your code, that a function may pause in the middle while it waits for something. It does not mean the function is slow, complicated, or running on a different thread by itself. It simply means the function contains at least one point where it can suspend, hand control back to the system, and resume later when the thing it was waiting for is ready. That promise is written into the function's type, so callers cannot ignore it by accident.

Think of async like a note on a recipe that says this dish needs time in the oven. The cook can start other dishes while it bakes, but the recipe itself is honest about the waiting involved. In code, you write async after the parameter list and before any throws or return type. Once you add it, every call to that function must be handled with await, or placed inside a Task, or otherwise managed by Swift's concurrency rules. The compiler will complain if you try to call an async function as if it were an ordinary function. That complaint is helpful, not annoying, because it forces you to think about waiting.

A common misunderstanding is that marking a function async automatically makes it run in the background. It does not. Async only describes the possibility of suspension. The actual scheduling is handled by Swift's cooperative system, which decides when suspended work resumes and which thread runs it. You should not assume a particular thread, and you should not rely on async alone to protect the user interface. The protection comes from how you structure the work, where you await, and whether you use actors or MainActor for sensitive parts. Async is the first step in that structure, not the whole solution.

Another useful way to see async is as a contract about time. An ordinary function starts, runs, and returns in one uninterrupted stretch, at least from the caller's point of view. An async function may start, pause, resume, and only then return. During the pause, other work can proceed. That contract makes code easier to read than older callback styles, because the steps still appear in order in your source file. You write first do this, then wait for that, then use the result, and Swift handles the pausing behind the scenes. The order in the file matches the order of the logic.

When you design your own functions, add async only when the function really needs to wait. Do not add it to every function just in case, because it spreads through your code and forces callers to become async too. Add it where there is real waiting: network calls, file access, database queries, or calls to other async functions. Keep pure, fast calculations synchronous. That discipline keeps your code honest, easier to test, and easier to understand, and it makes the async parts stand out as the places where waiting actually happens.

func fetchName() async -> String {
    return "Ada"
}

Section 4: The await Keyword Explained Simply

If async is the promise that a function may pause, await is the point where the pause actually happens. When you write await before a call, you are saying wait here until this work finishes, then continue with the result. The current task suspends, the system can run other work, and when the awaited work completes, your code resumes on the next line with the value ready to use. It looks like an ordinary function call, but it behaves like a polite pause rather than a block.

The word await also helps the reader. In a long function, every await is a visible signpost that says this line may take time. That visibility matters when you debug or review code. You can scan for awaits and immediately see where waiting happens, where errors might come from, and where cancellation might interrupt the flow. Without that marker, slow operations would hide inside innocent-looking calls, and you would have to guess which lines could pause. Swift's design deliberately makes waiting explicit, so the shape of the code matches the reality of the work.

It is worth stressing that await does not block a thread in the old sense. Blocking means holding a thread hostage while nothing useful happens on it. Suspending means freeing that thread so other work can use it, while remembering exactly where to resume. That distinction is why async and await feel smooth: many tasks can be suspended at awaits, sharing a small number of threads efficiently. You get the readability of straight-line code without the waste of blocked threads. The system does the clever scheduling; you just mark the waiting points honestly.

You must use await whenever you call an async function, and the compiler enforces that rule. If you forget it, you will get an error, not silent misbehaviour. Sometimes you will also need try alongside await, when the function can throw an error, giving you try await. The order matters, and you will practise it soon. For now, remember that await is not optional decoration. It is the mechanism that connects a caller's flow to the async function's pause, and it makes the pause part of the code's visible structure.

Beginners sometimes worry that await will make everything slow, as if each await adds a fixed delay. It does not. Await only waits as long as the work itself takes. If the work is instant, the wait is instant. If the work takes two seconds, you wait two seconds, but other tasks can proceed in the meantime. Await is therefore a tool for honesty and efficiency together: it admits that waiting is real, and it lets the system use the waiting time well instead of wasting it.

func showName() async {
    let name = await fetchName()
    print(name)
}

Section 5: Writing Your First async Function

Writing your first async function is simpler than many beginners expect, because the structure is almost the same as writing an ordinary function. You choose a clear name, decide what inputs it needs, decide what it returns, and then add async to signal that it may pause. Inside the body, you write the steps in order, using await wherever you call another async function. The result is code that reads like a story: first prepare, then wait for data, then process it, then return the answer. That readability is one of the main reasons Swift adopted this style.

Start with a small, realistic example in your mind. Suppose you need a user's display name from a server. The function might take a user identifier, await a network call, and return a string. If the network call is already async, your function becomes async simply by awaiting it. You do not need to create threads, manage queues, or write callback closures. You just describe the steps and let Swift handle suspension. That simplicity is powerful, but it also means you must be careful about what you put inside: every await is a place where the world can change, the task can be cancelled, or an error can arrive.

Good async functions are focused. They do one job well, they name their waiting clearly, and they do not hide surprising side effects. Avoid writing a giant async function that downloads data, parses it, saves it, updates the screen, and sends analytics all in one place. Break that work into smaller functions, some async and some synchronous, so each piece can be tested and reused. Small functions also make cancellation and error handling easier, because you can see exactly which step failed or was cancelled without untangling a huge body.

You should also think about errors from the start. Many async functions can throw, because network calls fail, files go missing, and servers return unexpected answers. Mark such functions with throws as well as async, and handle errors at a sensible level, not at every single line. A caller that understands the job should decide whether to retry, show a message, or fall back to cached data. Letting errors travel up to that caller keeps your small functions clean and honest about what can go wrong.

Finally, practise writing async functions that return useful values rather than just printing results. Returning a value makes the function testable: a test can call it, await the result, and check the answer. Printing inside the function hides the result and makes reuse harder. As you work through this chapter, aim for functions that take inputs, await what they need, and return a clear result or throw a clear error. That habit will serve you in every later chapter on networking, persistence, and app architecture.

func fetchGreeting(for user: String) async -> String {
    return "Hello, \(user)"
}

Section 6: Calling an async Function Correctly

You can only call an async function from a place that understands waiting. That usually means inside another async function, where you use await, or inside a Task, which provides an async context for the work it runs. If you try to call an async function from an ordinary synchronous function without a Task, the compiler will stop you. That rule may feel strict at first, but it protects you from accidentally ignoring a pause. Waiting cannot be hidden, so the code that triggers waiting must say so plainly.

The most common correct pattern is simple: an async function calls another async function with await, and the caller of that function also awaits, all the way up to a Task or an entry point that Swift allows to be async. This chain is sometimes called async propagation. It looks like extra work, but it actually makes the flow honest. Anyone reading the top of the chain can see that the whole operation may pause, and anyone testing it knows to await the result. The chain also carries errors and cancellation naturally, because each await is a place where those events can surface.

Tasks give you a bridge from synchronous code to async code. For example, a button handler is usually synchronous, but it can start a Task that runs async work. Inside the Task closure, you can await freely. The button handler returns immediately, the interface stays responsive, and the Task continues in the background. This is the standard way to start async work from user interface events. You should still think about what happens if the user taps twice, navigates away, or cancels, but the basic bridge is straightforward and you will use it constantly.

A mistake to avoid is trying to force an async result into a synchronous return by blocking or waiting with a loop. That defeats the purpose of suspension and can freeze the interface or even deadlock. Swift's concurrency model is cooperative, which means tasks are expected to suspend at awaits rather than block threads. If you block, you break that cooperation. Instead, redesign the caller to be async, or start a Task and handle the result when it arrives, perhaps by updating state or showing a message. The small redesign pays off in responsiveness.

Finally, remember that calling correctly also means handling the result correctly. Await the value, use it, and handle any error it may throw. Do not discard results silently unless you have a reason, and do not ignore errors just to make code compile. Correct calling is not only about satisfying the compiler. It is about keeping the chain of waiting, errors, and cancellation visible from the first call to the final handling, so the program behaves predictably when real-world delays and failures occur.

Task {
    let greeting = await fetchGreeting(for: "Ada")
    print(greeting)
}

Section 7: What Happens While You Await

When your code reaches an await, the current task suspends. Suspension is like bookmarking a page: Swift remembers exactly where you stopped, what values you had, and what you were waiting for, then it frees the underlying thread for other work. Other tasks can run, the interface can update, and timers can fire. Nothing about your task is lost. It is simply parked until its awaited work completes, at which point Swift resumes it at the bookmark, with the result in hand, as if no interruption had happened.

This bookmark idea explains why async code reads so naturally. You do not have to split your logic into before and after callbacks, because Swift keeps the whole context for you. Variables declared before the await are still available after it, in the same scope, with the same names. That continuity is a huge improvement over older styles, where state had to be passed through closures and it was easy to lose track of what had already happened. With await, the story stays in one place, even though the execution pauses in the middle.

While you await, the world can change. A user may tap cancel, a network request may fail, or shared data may be updated by another task. Your code after the await should therefore not assume that everything is exactly as it was before. Check cancellation if the work is long, handle errors that may have arrived, and re-read any state that another task might have changed. This careful attitude is not paranoia; it is the normal discipline of concurrent programming. Awaits are natural checkpoints where you pause and reconsider, rather than blindly continuing.

It is also important to know that suspension is cooperative. Swift does not forcibly stop a task in the middle of a calculation. A task suspends only at await points, or at other explicit suspension points. Long calculations without awaits will still occupy a thread until they finish, so you should not expect await to magically split a heavy loop. If you have long CPU work, you need to structure it differently, perhaps by breaking it into chunks with awaits between them, or by using tasks designed for that purpose. Understanding this limit prevents surprises.

In practice, thinking about what happens while you await helps you write better loading states. Show a spinner before the await, update progress if you can, and handle cancellation after it. The user sees honest feedback because your code knows it is waiting. When the await completes, hide the spinner and present the result or an error message. That simple pattern, repeated consistently, makes an app feel polished, because the interface always reflects the true state of the work behind it.

func load() async {
    let name = await fetchName()
    print("Loaded \(name)")
}

Section 8: async Functions Return Values and Throw Errors

An async function can return a value, throw an error, do both, or do neither, just like an ordinary function, with the added ability to pause. When it returns a value, the caller receives that value at the await point, after the pause completes. When it throws, the error travels back to the caller at the same point, where it can be caught and handled. This symmetry is elegant: from the caller's point of view, await is simply the moment when the result, good or bad, finally arrives. The rest of the code can then proceed with confidence.

Return values deserve careful design. Choose a return type that clearly represents success, and avoid returning vague types that force the caller to guess what happened. If a function fetches a user profile, returning a Profile type is clearer than returning a dictionary of strings. If it may legitimately have no result, consider an optional, but document when that happens. Clear return types reduce bugs, because the compiler checks that callers handle the value correctly, and readers can understand the function's job from its signature alone.

Errors deserve the same care. Use throws when failure is a real possibility that the caller should handle, such as a network failure, a missing file, or invalid data from a server. Define error types that describe what went wrong in terms the caller can act on, rather than a single generic failure. A caller can then decide to retry a network error, show a friendly message for missing data, or report an unexpected error for investigation. Throwing is not a sign of bad code; it is honest communication about the uncertain world outside your program.

When a function both awaits and throws, its signature combines both markers, and callers use try await together. The order, try before await, is fixed in Swift, and you will get used to it quickly. Inside the function, you may await several throwing calls in sequence, and the first failure will stop the function and propagate the error, skipping later steps. That behaviour matches ordinary throwing code, which makes async error handling feel familiar. You do not need a new mental model for errors; you just add waiting to the model you already know.

Testing becomes easier when functions return values and throw clearly. A test can call the function, await its result, and assert on the value, or it can assert that a particular error is thrown for bad input. That testability is a reason to prefer returning and throwing over printing or silently swallowing failures. As you build larger apps, this discipline compounds: every small async function becomes a reliable building block, and the larger flows built from them are easier to reason about and to fix.

func fetchAge() async throws -> Int {
    return 30
}

Section 9: Using async with try and await Together

When an async function can also throw, callers write try await, with try first and await second. That fixed order is easy to remember once you understand what each word does. Try says this call may throw an error, so be ready to handle it. Await says this call may pause, so be ready to suspend. Together they describe a call that may both pause and fail, which is exactly what most real network and file operations do. Writing both words makes both facts visible in a single line.

You will usually handle try await inside a do-catch block. The do block contains the awaited calls, and the catch block handles any error that escapes. That structure lets you group related steps: fetch data, parse it, and save it, all inside one do block, with one place to handle failures. If any step throws, later steps are skipped and control jumps to catch. That is often the behaviour you want, because later steps usually depend on earlier ones succeeding. For example, there is no point parsing data that failed to download.

Sometimes you want finer control. You might catch an error from one specific call, retry it, and then continue with other work. In that case, you can use a smaller do-catch around just that call, or use try? to turn a thrown error into an optional nil. Each choice has trade-offs. A broad catch is simpler but loses detail about which step failed. A narrow catch is more precise but adds code. Choose based on what the user needs: if any failure means the whole operation fails, a broad catch is fine; if one step can fail safely while others continue, handle it narrowly.

Another pattern is to let errors propagate. If your function is itself async and throwing, you can simply write try await inside it without catching, and the error will travel to your caller. That is often the cleanest choice for small helper functions, because they do not know how the user interface should present the error. Letting the error rise to a layer that does know, such as a view model or a screen, keeps responsibilities clear. The helper stays focused on its job, and the presentation layer decides on messages, retries, and fallbacks.

Practise reading try await lines aloud: try to fetch, and await the result. That phrasing captures the dual nature of the call. Over time, the combination will feel as natural as a plain function call. The key is never to hide either aspect. Do not use try! to force success unless you are truly certain, and do not avoid await by blocking. Keep both words present, handle errors thoughtfully, and your async code will be both readable and resilient in the face of real-world failures.

func showAge() async {
    do {
        let age = try await fetchAge()
        print(age)
    } catch {
        print("Failed: \(error)")
    }
}

Section 10: The Task Type in Plain Words

A Task in Swift is a unit of concurrent work. You can think of it as a job ticket: it describes work to be done, it can run alongside other jobs, it can be cancelled, and it eventually produces a result or an error. When you create a Task, you give it a closure containing async code, and Swift schedules that closure to run. The Task itself is a value you can store, cancel, or await, which gives you control over the job after it starts. That control is what makes Task more useful than simply calling an async function and forgetting about it.

Tasks are the bridge between synchronous and asynchronous worlds. A button handler, which is synchronous, can create a Task to run async work without blocking the interface. A background job can create child tasks to split its work. The Task type therefore appears in many places, and understanding it unlocks much of Swift concurrency. You do not need to know how the scheduler works internally to use Task well. You need to know how to create one, how to get its result, how to cancel it, and when not to create one because structured alternatives are better.

One important detail is that a Task captures its context. It inherits properties such as priority and, in some cases, actor isolation from the place where it was created, unless you explicitly detach it. That inheritance is usually helpful, because it keeps related work consistent. For example, a Task created from the main actor will often start on the main actor, which matters for interface updates. Detached tasks, which you will meet later, do not inherit that context, and that difference can cause subtle bugs if you use them without understanding it. For now, prefer ordinary Task creation and let inheritance work for you.

Tasks also have a lifecycle. They start, they may suspend at awaits, they may be cancelled, and they finish with a value, an error, or cancellation. You can await a Task's value property to wait for that finish, which is useful when you store a Task and need its result later. You can also simply let a Task run and handle its result inside its own closure, which is common for fire-and-forget interface work, provided you handle errors inside the closure so they are not lost. Uncaught errors in a Task can be easy to miss, so handle them deliberately.

As you learn TaskGroup and actors later in this chapter, keep Task as your basic building block. Groups organise many Tasks, actors protect data that Tasks share, and MainActor guides Tasks that touch the interface. None of those ideas replaces Task; they build on it. A solid grasp of Task, including its creation, result, and cancellation, will make every later pattern easier to understand and to use correctly in real apps.

let task = Task {
    await fetchName()
}

Section 11: Starting Work with Task

Starting work with Task is straightforward, but the details matter for correctness. You write Task, followed by a closure, and inside that closure you write async code as if you were inside an async function. Swift schedules the closure to run concurrently, and the code that created the Task continues immediately without waiting. That immediate return is the point: the creator is not blocked, the interface stays responsive, and the new work proceeds in parallel with whatever else is happening. This pattern is ideal for responding to user actions.

You will most often start a Task from a synchronous context, such as a button tap, a view appearing, or an older callback. In those places, you cannot simply await, because there is no async context to await in. Task provides that context. Inside the Task, you can await network calls, handle errors with do-catch, and update state when the work finishes. Outside the Task, life goes on. That separation is clean, but it also means you must think about what happens if the surrounding screen disappears before the Task finishes, a topic you will revisit with cancellation.

Tasks can also be started from async contexts, where you might wonder why you need a Task at all when you could just await directly. The answer is that a Task lets work run concurrently with the current task, rather than sequentially. If you await directly, the current task pauses until that work finishes. If you start a Task, the current task continues while the new task runs, and you can await its result later, or not at all. That choice between sequential awaiting and concurrent Task creation is a key design decision, and you should make it deliberately based on whether the work can usefully overlap.

When you start a Task, consider its lifetime. A Task that outlives its screen can update state that no longer exists, or keep doing work the user no longer wants. In simple examples, that risk is small, but in real apps it matters. Store the Task if you may need to cancel it, or use structured patterns that tie the Task's lifetime to a scope. Swift's structured concurrency, including async let and TaskGroup, exists precisely to tie lifetimes together safely. Unstructured Task creation is powerful, but it places the responsibility for lifetime management on you.

Finally, keep Task closures focused and safe. Handle errors inside them, avoid capturing large objects unnecessarily, and avoid updating the interface from a Task that is not on the main actor without proper handling. A Task closure that is short, handles its own errors, and reports its result through safe state or an actor is much easier to maintain than a sprawling closure that touches everything. Start small, verify the behaviour, and only then add complexity.

    print("Work started")
}

Section 12: Task Values and Results

Every Task eventually finishes with a result: a value on success, or an error on failure. You can obtain that result by awaiting the Task's value property. That await suspends the awaiting task until the Task finishes, then delivers the value or throws the error. This mechanism lets you start work early, do other things, and collect the result later when you actually need it. It is a simple but powerful pattern for overlapping work without losing track of results.

Storing a Task in a variable is what makes this pattern possible. Without a stored reference, you cannot await its value later or cancel it. With a stored reference, you have a handle to the job. For example, you might start a Task to fetch a profile, show a loading indicator, do some local setup, and then await the Task's value to display the profile. The overlap saves time, and the stored handle keeps the flow clear. If you never need the result later, you do not need to store the Task, but you should still handle errors inside its closure.

A common question is what happens if you await a Task's value twice, or if you never await it at all. Awaiting twice returns the same result, because the Task's result is remembered after it finishes. Never awaiting it is allowed, but then its result and any error are effectively ignored unless handled inside the closure. That can hide failures, so for important work, either await the value or handle errors inside the Task. For truly fire-and-forget work, such as logging, ignoring the result may be acceptable, but make that choice consciously, not by accident.

Errors from a Task deserve attention. If the Task's closure throws and nobody awaits its value, the error may be reported in ways that are easy to miss during development, and silently lost in production. A good habit is to wrap Task bodies in do-catch when the Task is not awaited elsewhere, and to log or present the error appropriately. When the Task is awaited, handle the error at the awaiting site with do-catch. Either way, every Task should have a clear error path, just as every function should.

Task values also interact with cancellation. If a Task is cancelled before it finishes, awaiting its value may throw a cancellation error, depending on how the Task handles cancellation. You will learn cancellation in the next sections, but it is worth noting here that awaiting a value is not just about success. It is about learning the full outcome: success, failure, or cancellation. Handling all three outcomes, even briefly, makes your concurrent code robust and honest about what really happened.

let task = Task { 42 }
let value = await task.value
print(value)

Storing a Task also helps with debugging, because you can inspect whether it has finished, whether it was cancelled, and what result it produced, rather than guessing about work that disappeared into the background. That visibility is valuable when a feature behaves inconsistently, since you can trace the stored Task through its lifecycle and see exactly where its result was used or ignored.

Section 13: Cancelling a Task

Cancellation is how you tell a Task that its work is no longer needed. You call cancel on the Task value, and Swift marks the Task as cancelled. That mark does not forcibly stop the work in the middle of a calculation. Instead, it sets a flag that the Task's code can check, and it causes certain waiting operations to throw a cancellation error. The Task cooperates by noticing the flag at sensible points and stopping early. This cooperative design is safer than forcible termination, which could leave data half-updated.

Cancellation matters most when work is slow, expensive, or tied to a screen the user has left. If a user searches for one term, then quickly types another, the first search is wasted work and its result could overwrite the second search's result if it arrives later. Cancelling the first Task avoids both waste and confusion. Similarly, if a user navigates away from a screen while it is loading, cancelling its Tasks frees resources and prevents updates to a screen that no longer exists. Good apps cancel eagerly and often.

You should cancel from the place that knows the work is no longer wanted. That might be a view disappearing, a new search starting, or a timeout expiring. Store the Task so you can cancel it later, and cancel it in the appropriate lifecycle event. Do not rely on the Task to guess that it should stop. The Task knows how to stop, but only its owner knows when stopping is correct. That division of responsibility keeps cancellation predictable and testable.

It is also important to understand that cancelling a parent does not automatically cancel every unstructured child, though structured concurrency does propagate cancellation to child tasks in a scope. With unstructured Tasks, you must manage cancellation yourself, which is one reason structured patterns are preferred when possible. If you create a Task and store it, remember to cancel it when its purpose ends. If you use async let or a TaskGroup, cancellation of the scope will flow to its children, which simplifies your job considerably.

Finally, cancellation is not an error in the moral sense. It is a normal outcome, not a failure to be ashamed of. Your code should handle it gracefully, without showing alarming error messages for work the user intentionally abandoned. A cancelled search should simply stop, not display a frightening alert. Treating cancellation as a normal, expected outcome, rather than as a bug, leads to calmer interfaces and cleaner code.

let task = Task { await fetchName() }
task.cancel()

A practical example makes this clearer. Imagine a photo app that starts downloading a large image when a user opens a detail screen. If the user quickly swipes to another photo, the first download is no longer needed, and cancelling it saves bandwidth and prevents the old image from flashing briefly on the new screen. Without cancellation, the app would waste data and risk showing the wrong photo, even though the user had already moved on.

Section 14: Checking for Cancellation

A Task will not stop by itself just because it has been marked cancelled. Its code must check for cancellation and act on it. Swift provides several ways to do this. You can check Task.isCancelled, which returns a Boolean, or you can call Task.checkCancellation, which throws a cancellation error if the Task has been cancelled. You can also use withTaskCancellationHandler to run cleanup code when cancellation occurs. Each tool fits a different situation, and learning when to use each one is part of writing responsive concurrent code.

Checking is most important in long-running work. If your Task loops over many items, checks a flag each iteration, and exits early when cancelled, it will stop quickly and waste little time. If it never checks, it will run to completion even though nobody wants its result, wasting time, battery, and network data. A good rule is to check at natural boundaries: between items in a loop, before starting a new expensive step, and after each await that might have taken a long time. Those checkpoints cost almost nothing when cancellation has not happened, and save a lot when it has.

Awaited operations also respond to cancellation in useful ways. Many built-in async functions, such as network calls and sleeps, throw a cancellation error when the Task is cancelled while they are waiting. That means a Task that is simply awaiting a slow operation may stop automatically, without an explicit check. Your code should handle that error as cancellation, not as a generic failure. In a do-catch block, you can distinguish cancellation from other errors and avoid showing a misleading message. That distinction keeps the user experience calm.

Cleanup deserves thought too. If a Task opens a file, starts a download, or reserves a resource, cancellation should release those resources, not leave them dangling. A cancellation handler lets you run cleanup code promptly when cancellation arrives, even if the main body is suspended. Use it for releasing resources, closing connections, or resetting temporary state. Without cleanup, cancelled work can leak resources and cause later work to behave strangely, which is a subtle bug that is hard to trace.

In short, checking for cancellation is a habit, not a one-time fix. Sprinkle checks at sensible points, handle cancellation errors separately from other errors, and clean up promptly. Code that checks well feels responsive: when the user taps cancel or moves on, the app actually stops, quickly and quietly. Code that never checks feels stubborn: it keeps working long after its result is useless, and the user notices that waste even if they cannot name the cause.

func work() async throws {
    try Task.checkCancellation()
    print("Still running")
}

You should also check cancellation after long awaits, not only inside loops. An await that took several seconds may return after the user has already cancelled, and the code that follows should not blindly continue as if nothing happened. A quick check after such an await lets you stop before doing further expensive work or updating the interface with a result nobody wants anymore.

Section 15: Structured Concurrency in Plain Words

Structured concurrency is Swift's way of giving concurrent work a clear shape, with a beginning, an end, and a parent that waits for its children. In structured concurrency, child tasks are created inside a scope, such as an async let binding or a TaskGroup, and that scope does not finish until all its children finish. That guarantee is powerful: you cannot accidentally leak a child task that keeps running after its parent has returned, and you cannot forget to wait for results, because the scope enforces waiting for you. The structure itself prevents a whole class of bugs.

Contrast this with unstructured concurrency, where you create a Task and it runs independently, with no parent scope waiting for it. Unstructured tasks are flexible, but they place all lifetime responsibility on you. If you forget to store, cancel, or await them, they may outlive their purpose. Structured concurrency removes that burden for common cases by tying child lifetimes to a visible scope in your code. When you read a structured block, you can see exactly where its children start and where they must finish, which makes reasoning about the code much easier.

Structured concurrency also handles errors and cancellation cleanly. If one child throws, the scope can cancel its siblings and propagate the error to the parent, rather than leaving siblings running pointlessly. If the parent is cancelled, cancellation flows to its children, so the whole group stops together. That coordinated behaviour is hard to implement correctly by hand with unstructured tasks, but it comes naturally from the structure. You get correct group behaviour by choosing the right structure, not by writing extra bookkeeping code.

The two main structured tools you will learn next are async let and TaskGroup. Async let is ideal when you know in advance how many parallel pieces of work you need, such as fetching a profile and a list of posts at the same time. TaskGroup is ideal when the number of pieces is dynamic, such as processing a list whose length you only know at runtime. Both tools share the same structured guarantees, but they fit different shapes of problems. Learning to choose between them is a key skill.

Think of structured concurrency like a team with a manager who waits until every team member reports back before declaring the job done. No member is left behind, no report is lost, and if one member fails, the manager can decide what to do with the rest. Unstructured concurrency is like sending people off with no manager: it can work, but you must track everyone yourself. Whenever possible, prefer the manager. Your future self, debugging at midnight, will thank you for code whose concurrent shape is visible and enforced.

func loadBoth() async {
    async let a = fetchName()
    async let b = fetchName()
    let _ = await (a, b)
}

Section 16: Using async let for Parallel Work

Async let lets you start several async jobs at once and collect their results later, all within a structured scope. You write async let, followed by a name and an async call, and Swift starts that call as a child task immediately, without waiting for it to finish. You can write several async let bindings in a row, and they will run concurrently. Later, when you await each binding, you receive its result. If you await them together as a tuple, you wait for all of them at once. This pattern is concise, readable, and safe, which makes it a favourite for parallel fetching.

A classic example is loading a screen that needs two independent pieces of data, such as a user profile and that user's recent posts. Fetching them one after another takes the sum of both times. Fetching them with async let takes roughly the longer of the two times, because they overlap. The saving is real, especially on slow networks, and the code remains easy to read: two bindings at the top, then a single await that gathers both results. There are no explicit Task creations, no manual waiting logic, and no risk of forgetting a child, because the scope enforces completion.

Async let bindings have a scope, and that scope matters. The bindings live only until the end of the enclosing scope, and the scope will not exit until all bindings have finished or been cancelled. If you return early or throw before awaiting a binding, Swift will still wait for it or cancel it appropriately, depending on the situation. That safety is the structured guarantee in action. You cannot accidentally abandon an async let child by forgetting it, because the compiler and runtime conspire to keep the scope honest. That is a strong reason to prefer async let over manual Tasks for fixed parallel work.

You should use async let when the number of parallel jobs is known when you write the code. Two fetches, three calculations, or a profile plus settings are perfect fits. If the number depends on runtime data, such as a list of unknown length, TaskGroup is a better tool, which you will learn soon. Trying to force a dynamic list into fixed async let bindings leads to awkward code, while TaskGroup handles dynamic counts naturally. Choosing the right tool for the shape of the problem keeps your code simple and expressive.

Finally, remember that async let jobs run concurrently, but they are not independent in the sense of ignoring each other. If one throws, the scope may cancel the others, and the error propagates. If the parent is cancelled, the children are cancelled too. That coordination is usually what you want for a single screen's data: if one essential piece fails, there is little point continuing to fetch the rest. Async let gives you that coordination for free, with code that still reads like a straightforward list of jobs to do.

func load() async {
    async let name = fetchName()
    async let greeting = fetchGreeting(for: "Ada")
    print(await name, await greeting)
}

Section 17: Waiting for async let Values

Waiting for async let values is where parallel work turns back into usable results. Each async let binding creates a child task, and you await the binding's name to receive its value. Awaiting one binding waits only for that job, while awaiting a tuple of bindings waits for all of them. The order in which you await matters for performance in subtle ways, but not for correctness: awaiting in any order will eventually give you all results, because the jobs started concurrently regardless of the await order. The await order only decides when your code resumes after each result.

A useful pattern is to await all bindings together when you need all results before proceeding. Writing await with a tuple, such as await (profile, posts), waits for both and gives you both values at once. That single line makes it clear that the next step depends on everything. If you await bindings one by one, your code will resume after the first, do something, then wait for the second, which may be fine if the steps are independent, but it can also serialize work unintentionally. Think about dependencies: if the next step needs all results, await them together; if steps are independent, awaiting separately may be clearer.

Errors need care when awaiting async let values. If a child throws, awaiting it will throw that error to your code, where you can catch it. If you await bindings in a tuple and one throws, the error propagates and other children may be cancelled. That behaviour is usually correct for a screen that needs all its data, but you should handle it explicitly with do-catch so the user sees a sensible message rather than a crash. If you need partial results even when one job fails, async let may not be the best fit, and a TaskGroup with per-child error handling might serve you better.

Another detail is that you must await, or otherwise handle, every async let binding before its scope ends. The compiler will warn or error if you forget, because abandoning a binding would break the structured guarantee. That enforcement is helpful: it forces you to decide what to do with every parallel job you started. Do not silence it by discarding bindings carelessly. Instead, await them, even if you only need to confirm they finished, so the scope's contract remains clear to readers and to the runtime.

In practice, waiting for async let values feels like gathering ingredients you started preparing earlier. You started the rice and chopped the vegetables in parallel, and now you bring them together to finish the dish. The gathering step is simple, but it is where the parallel work pays off. Practise writing small functions that start two or three async let jobs, await them together, and combine their results into a single return value. That exercise builds the intuition you need for larger parallel flows.

func combined() async -> String {
    async let a = fetchName()
    async let b = fetchName()
    return await "\(a) and \(b)"
}

Section 18: When async let Goes Wrong

Async let is simple, but it can go wrong in a few predictable ways, and learning those pitfalls early will save you debugging time. One common problem is starting parallel work that is not actually independent. If the second job depends on the result of the first, starting both at once is incorrect: the second will run with missing or stale input. Async let is for independent jobs only. If jobs depend on each other, await the first, then start the second. Parallelism is a performance optimisation, not a licence to ignore dependencies, and forcing dependent jobs into parallel form creates subtle, timing-dependent bugs.

Another pitfall is over-parallelism. Starting dozens of async let jobs at once may overwhelm a server, exhaust connections, or consume excessive memory. Async let is designed for a small, fixed number of jobs, not for unbounded lists. If you find yourself wanting many bindings, that is a sign you need a TaskGroup with limits, or a sequential loop, or a batching strategy. More parallelism is not always better; the right amount depends on the server, the device, and the nature of the work. Measure and think, rather than parallelising everything by default.

Error handling is a third area where async let can surprise beginners. If one child throws, siblings may be cancelled, and the error propagates from the await. If your code is not prepared for that, a failure in one optional job can cancel an essential job and fail the whole operation. Consider whether each job is essential or optional before grouping them. Essential jobs belong together in one scope, where failure should stop everything. Optional jobs might belong in separate scopes or in a TaskGroup where failures can be handled individually. That design thinking prevents a minor failure from becoming a total failure.

Scope mistakes also occur. Async let bindings cannot escape their scope, so you cannot return a binding itself or store it for later use outside the function. If you try, the compiler will stop you. That restriction is intentional, because it preserves structured guarantees, but it surprises people who expect bindings to behave like Task values. If you need work that outlives the current scope, you need an unstructured Task, with all its lifetime responsibilities, or a redesign that keeps the work inside a scope. Do not fight the restriction; let it guide you toward safer structure.

Finally, remember that async let does not protect shared data by itself. If two parallel jobs mutate the same variable, you still have a data race, even though the jobs are structured. Structure guarantees lifetimes, not safety of shared mutation. For shared state, you need actors or other safe patterns, which you will learn later. Async let is a tool for starting and gathering parallel work, not a substitute for safe data design.

    async let name = fetchName()
    print(await name)
}

Section 19: TaskGroup Basics

A TaskGroup is a structured container for a dynamic number of child tasks. Unlike async let, where you write a fixed number of bindings, a TaskGroup lets you add tasks in a loop, based on runtime data. You create a group with a helper such as withTaskGroup, give it a closure, and inside that closure you add tasks with group.addTask. The group waits for all its children to finish before the closure returns, so the structured guarantee holds even when you do not know in advance how many children there will be. That flexibility makes TaskGroup essential for real apps.

TaskGroups are typed: all child tasks in a group produce the same result type, or they produce no result and just perform side effects. That uniformity simplifies collecting results, because you know what type to expect from each child. If your children produce different types, you need a different structure, such as separate async let bindings or separate groups, or you need to wrap results in a common type. The type requirement is not a limitation to work around carelessly; it encourages you to design child jobs that are truly similar, such as fetching many items of the same kind.

A TaskGroup's closure receives the group as a parameter, and you use that parameter to add tasks and to iterate over results. Iteration with a for await loop over the group yields results as children finish, not necessarily in the order they were added. That completion order is important: if you need results in original order, you must track order yourself, perhaps by including an index in each child's result. If order does not matter, completion order is efficient, because you can process each result as soon as it is ready, rather than waiting for the slowest child before processing any.

Groups also handle errors through a throwing variant, withThrowingTaskGroup, which you will meet in a later section. In the non-throwing variant, children cannot throw, or they must handle errors internally. Choosing between throwing and non-throwing groups is a design decision: if child failures should fail the whole group, use the throwing variant; if failures should be handled per child, handle them inside each child and return a result that records success or failure. Both patterns are valid, and the choice depends on what the group represents.

Think of a TaskGroup as a team assembled for a job whose size is only known when the job starts. A manager gathers workers, each worker does a similar piece, and the manager collects reports as they arrive, waiting until everyone is done before declaring the job complete. That picture captures the essence: dynamic membership, uniform work, completion-order results, and a guaranteed wait for all members. Mastering that picture will make dynamic parallel work feel manageable rather than chaotic.

func count() async -> Int {
    await withTaskGroup(of: Int.self) { group in
        group.addTask { 1 }
        group.addTask { 2 }
        var total = 0
        for await value in group { total += value }
        return total
    }
}

Section 20: Adding Work to a TaskGroup

Adding work to a TaskGroup happens inside the group's closure, using group.addTask with a closure that performs one piece of work. You typically add tasks in a loop, iterating over input data and adding one task per item. Each addTask call creates a child task that runs concurrently with the others, and the group tracks it until it finishes. You do not need to store the child tasks yourself, because the group owns them and will wait for them. That ownership is the structured guarantee in action, applied to a dynamic collection.

The closure you pass to addTask can capture values from the surrounding code, such as the current item in a loop. Be careful with capture in loops: capture the loop's current value in a way that each task gets its own copy, not a shared variable that changes as the loop continues. In Swift, a for loop's element is a fresh constant per iteration, so capturing it is usually safe, but if you use an index or a mutable variable, double-check that each task sees the value you intend. Bugs from incorrect capture are subtle, because tasks may run after the loop has moved on, and they can be hard to reproduce.

You can also add tasks conditionally, based on runtime checks. For example, you might skip items that are already cached, or add extra tasks for items that need special handling. The group does not require a fixed number of tasks, and adding zero tasks is allowed: the group will simply finish immediately with no results. That flexibility lets you write code that adapts to its input without special cases for empty lists, which is a pleasant property. Empty input should lead to empty output, not to a crash or a special branch.

Ordering of addition does not guarantee ordering of completion. Tasks added first may finish last, depending on how long each takes. Do not rely on addition order for correctness unless you explicitly track order in results. If your final output must preserve input order, include the original index in each child's result and sort afterwards. If output order does not matter, you can process results as they arrive, which is often more efficient and simpler. Making that decision explicitly, rather than assuming order, prevents a whole class of intermittent bugs.

Finally, remember that adding a task does not wait for it. The addTask call returns immediately, and the child runs concurrently. Waiting happens later, when you iterate over the group or when the group's closure returns. That separation between adding and waiting is what gives TaskGroup its power: you can add all tasks quickly, then collect results as they complete. Keep adding and collecting as distinct steps in your mind, and your group code will stay clear and correct.

func sum(values: [Int]) async -> Int {
    await withTaskGroup(of: Int.self) { group in
        for value in values { group.addTask { value } }
        var total = 0
        for await item in group { total += item }
        return total
    }
}

Section 21: Collecting Results from a TaskGroup

Collecting results from a TaskGroup usually means iterating over the group with a for await loop. Each iteration yields the next completed child's result, in completion order. The loop suspends when no child has finished yet, resumes when one finishes, and ends when all children are done. That loop is both the collection mechanism and the waiting mechanism: you do not need a separate wait step, because the loop itself waits for each result and finishes only when the group is empty. It is a concise and expressive pattern.

Inside the loop, you can process each result immediately, append it to an array, add it to a total, or update a summary. Processing as results arrive is efficient, because you do not wait for the slowest child before handling faster ones. For example, if you are downloading many small images, you can display each image as soon as it arrives, rather than waiting for all of them. That incremental progress feels responsive to the user, and it spreads work over time instead of bunching it at the end. When order matters, store results with their indices and sort after the loop, as discussed earlier.

You should also consider what happens if you break out of the loop early. Leaving the group's closure before all children finish does not abandon them silently: the group will wait for or cancel remaining children as part of structured cleanup. Breaking early is therefore safe in terms of lifetimes, but it may waste work or lose results you actually needed. If you break because you found what you were looking for, such as the first successful result, that waste may be acceptable, and cancellation of remaining children is desirable. If you break by accident, you may lose results. Make early exits deliberate and documented.

An alternative collection style is to gather all results into an array and return that array after the loop. That style is simple and works well when the caller needs the complete set before proceeding. It is less incremental, because the caller waits for everything, but it is easier to reason about and to test: given input, the function returns a complete collection. Choose between incremental processing and complete collection based on what the caller needs and what the user should see. Both are valid; the mistake is to choose without thinking about responsiveness.

Finally, remember that the for await loop over a group is itself an async operation. It can only appear in an async context, and it suspends as described. That suspension is cooperative and efficient, freeing the thread for other work while waiting. When you read group code, look for the loop as the gathering point, just as you look for await as the gathering point for async let. Recognising those gathering points helps you understand the flow of any concurrent function at a glance.

func collect(values: [Int]) async -> [Int] {
    await withTaskGroup(of: Int.self) { group in
        for value in values { group.addTask { value } }
        var results: [Int] = []
        for await item in group { results.append(item) }
        return results
    }
}

Section 22: TaskGroup Errors and Cancellation

TaskGroup error handling uses a throwing variant called withThrowingTaskGroup. In that variant, child tasks may throw, and the group collects the first error and propagates it, while cancelling remaining children. That behaviour matches the idea that a group represents a single coordinated job: if one essential piece fails, the whole job fails, and continuing other pieces is wasteful. You handle the error at the call site with do-catch, just as you handle errors from any throwing async function. The throwing group therefore integrates cleanly with ordinary error handling.

Cancellation in a group is similarly coordinated. If the parent task is cancelled, the group cancels its children, and children that check for cancellation or await cancellable operations will stop. If you explicitly cancel the group, using group.cancelAll, all children are marked cancelled, and they should stop cooperatively. That coordinated cancellation is a major advantage of structured groups over manual Tasks, where you would have to track and cancel each Task yourself. With a group, one cancellation decision reaches all members, which keeps the code simple and the behaviour consistent.

Sometimes you do not want one child's failure to fail the whole group. For example, you might be fetching many independent items, and one missing item should not prevent the others from displaying. In that case, handle errors inside each child: catch the error in the child's closure and return a result that records failure for that item, such as an optional or a result type. The group itself can then be non-throwing, and the caller can decide how to present partial results. That pattern preserves useful work while still reporting failures honestly, and it is a good fit for bulk fetching where partial success is valuable.

You should also think about cleanup when a group is cancelled or fails. Children that hold resources should release them in cancellation handlers or in defer blocks, so a cancelled group does not leak. The group guarantees that children finish before the scope ends, but it does not guarantee that their cleanup is correct; that remains your responsibility. Writing child closures with clear cleanup, especially for files and network connections, prevents leaks that only appear under cancellation, which are notoriously hard to test if you never test cancellation itself.

Testing group errors and cancellation is worth the effort. Write tests where one child throws and verify that the group reports the error and other children stop. Write tests where the parent is cancelled and verify that children do not keep running indefinitely. Those tests catch coordination bugs early, before they appear as wasted work or confusing partial results in production. Structured groups make such tests possible, because the group's behaviour is defined and repeatable, unlike unstructured tasks whose lifetimes are harder to control.

func loadAll() async throws -> [Int] {
    try await withThrowingTaskGroup(of: Int.self) { group in
        group.addTask { 1 }
        var results: [Int] = []
        for try await item in group { results.append(item) }
        return results
    }
}

Section 23: Unstructured Tasks and When to Avoid Them

Unstructured tasks are Tasks created with Task or Task.detached that are not tied to a structured scope. They run independently, and nothing automatically waits for them, cancels them, or collects their results. That independence is sometimes necessary, for example when starting work from a synchronous button handler, where no structured scope exists. In those cases, unstructured tasks are the correct bridge. The danger comes when unstructured tasks are used casually inside async code, where structured alternatives would be safer and clearer.

The main risk of unstructured tasks is lifetime leakage. A Task created inside a function continues running after that function returns, unless you store and manage it. If the function was loading data for a screen that has since disappeared, the Task may update dead state or waste resources. If it was part of a larger operation, its result may be lost because nobody awaits it. Structured scopes prevent these problems by tying lifetimes to visible code blocks. Unstructured tasks require you to replicate that discipline manually, which is error-prone, especially as code grows and changes hands.

Another risk is lost cancellation and error propagation. Cancelling a parent does not automatically cancel an unstructured child, and an error in an unstructured child does not automatically propagate to the parent. You must wire those behaviours yourself, by storing Task handles, cancelling them at the right time, and awaiting or handling their results. That wiring is possible, but it adds code and opportunities for mistakes. Structured scopes wire it for you, with well-tested semantics. Preferring structure is therefore not just a style choice; it is a way to reduce the amount of manual coordination you must get right.

There are legitimate uses for unstructured tasks beyond button handlers. Long-lived background work, such as a task that runs for the lifetime of an app and listens for events, may not fit a short-lived scope. Work started from a delegate callback or an older API may also need an unstructured bridge. In those cases, manage the Task deliberately: store it in a property, cancel it when its owner is deallocated or its purpose ends, and handle its errors inside its closure. Document why structure was not possible, so future readers do not mistake a deliberate choice for carelessness.

A good rule of thumb is to ask, when you are about to write Task, whether a structured alternative fits. Could this be an async let binding? Could it be a child of a TaskGroup? Could the caller be made async so you can simply await? If any answer is yes, prefer that alternative. Use unstructured Task only when no structured scope can contain the work, and then manage its lifetime explicitly. That discipline keeps most of your code structured and safe, while reserving unstructured power for the places where it is truly needed.

Section 24: Detached Tasks and Their Dangers

Detached tasks, created with Task.detached, are a special kind of unstructured task that does not inherit context from its creator. Ordinary Tasks inherit priority and actor isolation, which keeps related work consistent. Detached tasks start fresh, with no actor isolation and a default or explicitly specified priority. That freshness can be useful in rare cases, such as starting truly independent background work from an actor without blocking that actor, but it also removes helpful defaults and can cause surprising behaviour if used without understanding.

One danger is actor isolation. If you create a detached task from inside an actor, the task does not run on that actor, so it cannot safely access the actor's state without awaiting the actor's methods. Beginners sometimes assume a detached task created inside an actor is still protected by that actor, and they access shared state directly, creating a data race. Ordinary Task creation avoids that trap by inheriting isolation. Detached tasks require you to think explicitly about where the work runs and how it accesses shared data, which is an extra burden that is easy to get wrong.

Another danger is priority and context loss. Detached tasks do not inherit task-local values or other contextual information from their creator, which can break logging, tracing, or configuration that relies on that context. If your app uses task-local values to carry request identifiers or user context, a detached task will lose them unless you pass them explicitly. That loss can make debugging harder, because logs from the detached task will lack the context that ties them to the original request. Ordinary tasks preserve that context automatically.

Detached tasks also share the lifetime risks of all unstructured tasks, with even less connection to their creator. Nothing waits for them, nothing cancels them automatically, and their errors do not propagate. If you create a detached task and forget it, it may run long after its purpose has ended, with no owner to stop it. That independence is occasionally what you want, but it should be a deliberate decision, documented and managed, not a default habit. Many bugs attributed to concurrency are actually bugs from forgotten detached tasks.

In practice, you will rarely need Task.detached. Most work fits ordinary Task creation, async let, or TaskGroup. If you think you need a detached task, pause and ask why inheritance is harmful in this case. Is the work truly independent of the actor and priority? Does it need to avoid blocking an actor? Can you achieve the same result with a structured pattern or an ordinary Task? If you cannot answer those questions clearly, do not detach. When you do detach, store the handle, handle errors inside, and cancel it explicitly when done.

Task.detached {
    print("Detached work")

Section 25: Priorities: How Tasks Get Scheduled

Task priorities are hints to Swift's scheduler about how important a piece of work is relative to other work. Priorities range from low to high, with user-initiated work in the middle-high range and background work at the low end. When you create a Task, it inherits the priority of its creator by default, unless you specify a different priority. The scheduler uses those hints to decide which ready tasks run first when resources are limited, but priorities are not guarantees: a low-priority task will still run, just later if higher-priority work is waiting. Understanding priorities as hints, not promises, prevents over-reliance on them.

Choosing priorities well improves responsiveness. Work that the user is directly waiting for, such as loading the current screen, should have a higher priority than work the user is not waiting for, such as prefetching data for a screen they might visit later. Background maintenance, such as cleaning caches, should have a low priority so it does not compete with interface work. If everything is high priority, nothing is, and the scheduler cannot distinguish urgent from optional work. Assign priorities based on user-visible urgency, not on how interesting the code is to you.

It is easy to misuse priorities by escalating everything or by setting priorities that do not match reality. A common mistake is to mark background analytics as high priority, which can delay interface work and make the app feel sluggish. Another mistake is to mark user-visible work as background, which can make it feel slow even on a fast device. A good habit is to leave the default inherited priority alone unless you have a clear reason to change it. Inheritance usually gives you a sensible priority, because work created from a user action inherits that action's urgency. Override only when the new work's urgency differs from its creator's.

Priorities also interact with structured concurrency. Child tasks in async let and TaskGroup inherit priorities in structured ways, and the scheduler may raise a parent's priority to match a child that is being awaited, a phenomenon called priority inversion avoidance. You do not need to manage those details manually, but it helps to know that Swift tries to prevent a low-priority parent from blocking high-priority work it is waiting for. That automatic adjustment is another reason structured concurrency is safer than manual thread management, where such adjustments had to be handled by hand.

Finally, do not use priorities as a substitute for good design. If a task is too slow, raising its priority will not make its algorithm faster, and it may starve other work. If tasks contend for a shared resource, priorities will not fix a data race or a deadlock. Priorities are a scheduling hint for well-designed work, not a cure for poorly designed work. Focus first on correct structure, cancellation, and efficient algorithms, then use priorities sparingly to express genuine urgency differences that the scheduler should respect.

Section 26: Actors: Safe Boxes for Shared Data

An actor is a Swift type that protects its mutable state by ensuring only one piece of code accesses that state at a time. You can think of an actor as a safe box with a single key: many tasks may want to open the box, but only one can hold the key at any moment, and others must wait their turn. That serial access eliminates a whole class of bugs where two tasks read and write the same data simultaneously and produce inconsistent results. Actors achieve this protection without requiring you to write locks manually, which are notoriously easy to get wrong.

Declaring an actor looks similar to declaring a class, but with the keyword actor instead of class. Inside, you write properties and methods as usual. The difference is in how those properties and methods can be accessed from outside. External code cannot directly read or write an actor's mutable properties; it must call methods, and those calls are async, because the caller may need to wait for its turn. That waiting is expressed with await, which makes the protection visible in the code. When you see await before an actor method, you know the call may suspend until the actor is free.

Actors are reference types, like classes, which means they are shared by reference and their identity matters. That sharing is intentional: the whole point of an actor is to be a single shared box that many tasks use. If actors were value types copied on every use, each copy would have its own state and the protection would be meaningless. Because they are shared, you must think about who holds a reference to an actor and for how long, just as you would with any shared object. The actor protects its internal state, but it does not protect you from holding onto it longer than needed.

A key benefit of actors is that their methods run one at a time, but they can still suspend at awaits inside the method. When an actor method awaits, it temporarily releases the actor, allowing another task to run on the actor in the meantime. That reentrancy is important: it prevents deadlocks where an actor waits for work that needs the same actor, but it also means you cannot assume state is unchanged across an await inside an actor method. After an await, re-check any assumptions about state, because another task may have modified it while you were suspended. That discipline is the price of safe, non-blocking actors.

Actors are not a cure for every concurrency problem, but they are the standard Swift tool for shared mutable state. When several tasks need to read and write the same counter, cache, or list, placing that state inside an actor and accessing it only through actor methods gives you a clear, compiler-checked boundary. The compiler enforces isolation, so violations are caught at build time rather than discovered as intermittent runtime bugs. That shift from runtime debugging to compile-time checking is one of the most valuable features of modern Swift concurrency.

actor Counter {
    private var count = 0
    func increment() { count += 1 }
    func value() -> Int { count }
}

Section 27: Calling Actor Methods with await

Calling an actor method from outside the actor requires await, because the call may need to wait for the actor to become free. That await is not just ceremony; it marks a potential suspension point where the caller pauses and the actor finishes work for another task first. When the actor is free, the method runs, and the result returns to the caller. From the caller's perspective, the call looks like any other async call, but its purpose is specific: it is waiting for exclusive access to the actor's protected state, not just for slow work like a network request.

Inside the actor, methods can access state directly without await, because they are already holding the actor's protection. That distinction is important for readability: code inside the actor reads like ordinary synchronous code, with direct property access, while code outside uses await to cross the isolation boundary. When you read an actor, you can tell at a glance which code is inside the protected region and which code is outside requesting access. That clarity helps you reason about what state is safe to touch and where.

Sometimes you will call an actor method from another actor method, or from a Task that is already isolated to an actor. In those cases, Swift may still require await, because the target actor is different from the current actor, and crossing between actors always requires waiting for the target. If the caller and the target are the same actor, no await is needed, because the caller already holds the protection. Learning to predict when await is required comes with practice: ask whether the caller is already inside the target actor. If not, expect await.

Actor method calls can also throw, in which case you write try await, just as with other throwing async calls. Errors from actor methods should be handled like any other errors, with do-catch at a sensible level. Do not ignore actor errors, especially for operations that update shared state, because a failed update may leave the caller with a false belief about what was stored. Handle the error, retry if appropriate, or report it, so the shared state and the caller's expectations stay consistent.

A practical tip is to keep actor methods focused and short. Long actor methods that await many slow operations will hold the actor for a long time, or will suspend and resume repeatedly, which can complicate reasoning about state. Prefer actor methods that do a small, well-defined update or query, and do slow work outside the actor before calling it with the prepared result. For example, fetch data outside the actor, then call a short actor method to store it. That pattern keeps the actor responsive and its methods easy to understand and test.

func useCounter(_ counter: Counter) async {
    await counter.increment()
    let value = await counter.value()
    print(value)
}

Section 28: Actor Isolation Explained

Actor isolation is the rule that an actor's mutable state can only be accessed by code running on that actor. The Swift compiler enforces this rule, so you cannot accidentally read or write actor state from outside without going through an awaited method call. Isolation is what makes actors safe: it turns a runtime hope, that nobody will touch shared data at the wrong time, into a compile-time guarantee. If your code compiles, you know that direct access from outside is impossible, because the compiler would have rejected it.

Isolation applies to properties and to methods that touch those properties. A method declared inside an actor is isolated to that actor by default, meaning it runs with the actor's protection. A property is similarly isolated, unless it is a constant that can be safely shared, or it is explicitly marked nonisolated, a topic covered next. This default is helpful: you do not need to annotate every method, because isolation is the normal state inside an actor. You only need to think about exceptions, when you deliberately want code to run outside the actor's protection.

Understanding isolation also explains why actor methods are async from outside. Crossing an isolation boundary requires waiting, because the target actor may be busy. The await is the visible cost of that crossing. Inside the actor, no crossing occurs, so no await is needed for state access. That asymmetry is intentional and useful: it makes the boundary visible in the code, so readers can see where protection begins and ends. When you see await before an actor call, you are seeing the isolation boundary in action.

Isolation does not mean an actor's methods cannot suspend. As noted earlier, an actor method that awaits will release the actor during the suspension, allowing other tasks to interleave. That behaviour is called reentrancy, and it is a deliberate design choice to avoid deadlocks. It does, however, mean that isolation protects against simultaneous access, not against interleaved access across awaits. After an await inside an actor method, state may have changed. You must write actor methods with that possibility in mind, re-reading state after awaits rather than assuming it is unchanged.

Finally, isolation is a tool for design, not just for safety. When you place state inside an actor, you are declaring a boundary and a set of operations for that state. That declaration forces you to think about what operations are needed, what invariants must hold, and how concurrent callers should interact. That thinking often leads to cleaner designs, with state and its operations grouped together, rather than state scattered across many files with ad hoc locking. Isolation therefore improves both safety and organisation.

actor Store {
    private var items: [String] = []
    func add(_ item: String) { items.append(item) }
}

A helpful way to test your understanding is to draw the boundary for a small actor on paper. List its properties, list its methods, and mark which code runs inside the boundary and which code must cross it with await. That drawing often reveals methods that do not need state at all, which may be candidates for nonisolated, and methods that touch state in surprising ways.

Section 29: Nonisolated Methods and Properties

Nonisolated marks code inside an actor that does not need the actor's protection and can therefore run without waiting for the actor. You use it for methods and properties that do not touch mutable state, such as a method that returns a constant, performs a pure calculation, or accesses only immutable data. Marking such code nonisolated allows callers to use it synchronously, without await, which is convenient and efficient. It also signals to readers that this code is safe to call concurrently, because it does not depend on protected state.

A common use for nonisolated is a method that computes something from its arguments alone, without reading actor properties. For example, an actor that stores user records might have a nonisolated method that validates an email address format, because validation does not need the stored records. Callers can validate without waiting for the actor, which keeps the actor free for work that truly needs protection. Using nonisolated thoughtfully therefore improves responsiveness, by reserving actor time for stateful operations only.

You must be careful not to mark stateful code as nonisolated. If a nonisolated method reads or writes mutable actor state, the compiler will reject it, because that would break isolation. That rejection is protective, not obstructive. If you find yourself wanting to mark stateful code as nonisolated to avoid await, that is a sign the design needs rethinking: either the state should not be in the actor, or the caller should await properly. Do not work around the compiler by moving state outside the actor just to avoid await; that defeats the actor's purpose.

Nonisolated properties are typically constants. A let property that is immutable after initialisation can be safely shared without actor protection, because it never changes and therefore cannot be involved in a race. Swift allows such properties to be accessed without await, which is convenient for configuration values or identifiers stored in an actor. Mutable properties, by contrast, must remain isolated. That distinction between immutable and mutable is a recurring theme in concurrency: immutable data is inherently safe to share, while mutable data needs protection.

In practice, use nonisolated sparingly and deliberately. Most actor code should remain isolated, because most actor code touches state. Reserve nonisolated for genuinely stateless helpers and immutable values, and document why they are safe. Overusing nonisolated can obscure the actor's boundary and make it harder to tell which code is protected. A clear boundary, with a few well-chosen nonisolated exceptions, is easier to maintain than a boundary full of holes.

actor Account {
    let id: String
    init(id: String) { self.id = id }
    nonisolated func isValid() -> Bool { !id.isEmpty }
}

Be cautious with nonisolated methods that call other methods. A nonisolated method cannot call an isolated method without awaiting it, because that call crosses the boundary back into the actor. That rule keeps the boundary consistent, even when the call chain is indirect. If you find a nonisolated method that needs isolated state, it probably should not be nonisolated at all, and the design should be reconsidered.

Section 30: Data Races and Why They Hurt

A data race occurs when two tasks access the same mutable data at the same time, and at least one of them writes to it, without proper coordination. The result is unpredictable: one task may read a half-updated value, a write may be lost, or the data may end up in an inconsistent state that neither task intended. Data races are particularly harmful because they are intermittent. A program may work correctly a hundred times, then fail once under different timing, making the bug hard to reproduce, hard to debug, and easy to dismiss as a fluke. That intermittency is what makes races feared by experienced programmers.

A simple example helps. Imagine two tasks each increment a shared counter by one, starting from zero. Each increment involves reading the current value, adding one, and writing it back. If both tasks read zero before either writes, both will write one, and the final counter will be one instead of two. One increment is lost, even though both tasks did their work. No error is reported, no crash occurs, and the program continues with a wrong answer. In a real app, that wrong answer could be a lost purchase, an incorrect balance, or a missing item in a list. The damage is silent, which makes it worse than a loud crash.

Data races hurt more than just counts. They can corrupt complex structures, such as arrays or dictionaries, leaving them in states that cause crashes later, far from the original race. A crash that happens minutes after the race is hard to connect to its cause, and debugging becomes a search through unrelated code. Races can also cause security problems, if a check and an action are separated by a race that allows an unauthorised action to slip through. For all these reasons, preventing races is not optional polish; it is a core correctness requirement for concurrent programs.

Swift's concurrency model helps prevent races in several ways. Actors isolate mutable state, Sendable restricts what can be shared, and the compiler warns about unsafe sharing in many cases. Those tools do not make races impossible, especially when you use older APIs or manual sharing, but they make the safe path the easy path. When you follow the model, using actors for shared mutable state and immutable values for sharing, races become rare. When you bypass the model, with shared mutable variables accessed from multiple tasks, you reintroduce the risk.

The lesson is to treat any shared mutable data as a danger zone. Ask who can read it, who can write it, and how simultaneous access is prevented. If you cannot answer clearly, redesign: move the data into an actor, make it immutable, or give each task its own copy. That discipline, applied consistently, prevents the silent, intermittent failures that make data races so costly. Concurrency is powerful, but only when shared data is handled with respect.

Section 31: How Actors Prevent Data Races

Actors prevent data races by serialising access to their state. Only one task runs on an actor at a time, except during suspensions at awaits, when the actor is temporarily released but still tracks who may run next. That serialisation means two tasks cannot read and write the same property simultaneously, because the second must wait for the first to finish its current stretch of work. The lost-increment example from the previous section cannot happen inside an actor, because increments are performed one at a time, each reading, adding, and writing without interleaving from another task's increment.

That guarantee depends on all access going through the actor. If some code accesses the actor's state directly, bypassing the actor, the guarantee breaks. Swift's isolation rules prevent that bypass by making direct external access a compile error. You must call actor methods with await, which ensures the actor can schedule your access correctly. The compiler is therefore part of the prevention mechanism, not just a checker of syntax. By enforcing the boundary, it ensures that the runtime serialisation actually covers all access, not just the access that happens to follow the rules voluntarily.

Actors also prevent races by making state changes atomic from the perspective of other tasks, at least between awaits. A method that updates several related properties without awaiting in between will complete those updates before another task can observe the state. Other tasks will see either the old consistent state or the new consistent state, never a half-updated mixture. That atomicity is valuable for invariants, such as a balance and a transaction log that must stay consistent. If a method awaits in the middle, that atomicity is split, and you must design accordingly, but the default case of short, non-suspending methods gives you strong consistency.

It is important to understand what actors do not prevent. They do not prevent logical races, where two tasks each perform correct individual operations but in an order that produces an undesired overall result. For example, two tasks might each check a balance and then withdraw, both passing the check, even though the combined withdrawals exceed the balance. Each actor method was safe in isolation, but the sequence of check-then-act across methods was not atomic. To prevent logical races, you need to combine the check and the act into a single actor method, so they happen together without interleaving. Actors give you the tool to do that, but you must design the method boundaries correctly.

In summary, actors prevent low-level data races by serialising access and enforcing isolation, and they enable prevention of higher-level logical races by letting you group related operations into single methods. Use them for shared mutable state, keep methods short, combine check-and-act into one method when correctness depends on it, and respect awaits inside methods. Those habits turn actors from a theoretical safety feature into a practical guarantee that your concurrent code behaves predictably.

actor SafeCounter {
    private var count = 0
    func increment() { count += 1 }
    func value() -> Int { count }
}

Section 32: MainActor and the Main Thread

MainActor is a special global actor that represents the main thread, where user interface work must happen. In Swift, user interface frameworks require that interface updates occur on the main thread, because interface objects are not thread-safe. MainActor enforces that requirement by isolating code to the main actor, so it runs on the main thread. When you mark a function or type with @MainActor, Swift ensures its code runs on the main actor, and callers from elsewhere must await to cross into it. That enforcement turns a runtime rule, update the interface only on the main thread, into a compile-time guarantee.

The main thread's role deserves emphasis. It draws the screen, processes taps, and runs animations. If interface code runs on a background thread, it may corrupt interface state, crash, or produce visual glitches that are hard to reproduce. Older code relied on programmers remembering to dispatch to the main thread manually, and forgetting was a common bug. MainActor reduces that burden by making main-thread execution part of the type system. When you see @MainActor, you know the code is main-thread code, and when you do not see it, you know to be careful about interface updates.

MainActor is a global actor, which means there is a single shared instance for the whole program, unlike ordinary actors where you create instances. That global nature fits the main thread, which is also singular. You do not create a MainActor; you refer to it with the @MainActor attribute or by awaiting MainActor.run for a block of code. That block runs on the main actor and then returns, which is useful for a small interface update inside otherwise background work. Using MainActor.run for targeted updates is often cleaner than marking an entire function as main-actor isolated.

A common pattern is to keep data work off the main actor and interface work on it. Fetch and process data in background tasks or ordinary actors, then hop to MainActor to update the screen with the result. That separation keeps the interface responsive, because heavy work does not block the main thread, while still ensuring updates are safe. If you mark everything @MainActor, you lose that benefit, because heavy work will run on the main thread and freeze the interface. MainActor is a tool for interface code, not a default for all code.

Understanding MainActor is essential for any Swift app with a user interface. It connects the abstract ideas of actors and isolation to a concrete, everyday requirement: only the main thread touches the interface. By using @MainActor deliberately, for view models, view controllers, or specific update functions, you make that requirement explicit and compiler-checked, which prevents a whole class of interface bugs that once plagued Swift apps.

Section 33: Updating the User Interface Safely

Updating the user interface safely means doing two things: updating only on the main actor, and updating with the correct, latest data. The first part is enforced by MainActor, as discussed. The second part requires design: when background work finishes, it should deliver its result to the main actor, which then updates the interface in one coherent step. Avoid updating the interface piecemeal from background tasks, or from multiple tasks racing to update the same label. A single, main-actor update with a complete result is clearer and less prone to flicker or inconsistency.

A typical safe flow starts with a user action, such as tapping refresh. The handler starts a Task, shows a loading indicator on the main actor, performs the fetch in the background, and then hops to the main actor to hide the indicator and show the result or an error. Each hop is explicit, either through @MainActor isolation or through MainActor.run. That explicitness helps readers follow the flow: loading shown, work done, result presented. It also helps testing, because you can test the background fetch separately from the presentation logic.

Cancellation complicates interface updates, because background work may finish after the user has left the screen. Before updating the interface, check whether the Task was cancelled, and check whether the screen is still present. Updating a dismissed screen is wasteful and can cause crashes or confusing state if the screen is later recreated. A good pattern is to store the Task, cancel it when the screen disappears, and check cancellation before the final main-actor update. That pattern respects the user's navigation and prevents stale updates from overwriting newer content.

Another safe practice is to update interface state through a single source of truth, such as a view model isolated to the main actor. Background tasks deliver results to the view model, and the view model updates its published properties, which the interface observes. That indirection prevents background tasks from touching interface objects directly, and it centralises update logic in one place. When something goes wrong, you know where to look: the view model, not scattered background closures. That centralisation is worth the small extra code.

Finally, keep interface updates fast. Main-actor code should not perform heavy calculations, large file reads, or network calls, because those will block the interface just as surely as background work mistakenly run on the main thread would. Do heavy work first, off the main actor, then deliver a ready-to-display result for a quick update. That discipline, combined with MainActor isolation and cancellation checks, produces interfaces that feel smooth, honest, and reliable, even under slow networks and rapid user navigation.

    print("Interface update")
}

Timing also matters for interface updates. If two background tasks finish at nearly the same time and both try to update the same screen, the later update should win only if it represents newer data. Checking a version number or a request identifier before updating prevents an older result from overwriting a newer one, which is a common source of confusing flicker in real apps.

Section 34: @MainActor on Types and Functions

You can apply @MainActor to a function, a type, or a property, and each application has a slightly different effect. Applying it to a function means that function always runs on the main actor. Callers from outside must await it, and callers already on the main actor can call it directly. That is useful for functions that update the interface or manipulate interface state, because it guarantees their execution context without requiring manual dispatch inside the function body. The guarantee is visible in the signature, so readers know the function's context immediately.

Applying @MainActor to a type, such as a class or struct, means all of its methods and properties are isolated to the main actor by default, unless they are explicitly marked nonisolated. That is useful for view models or controllers that are inherently interface objects: their entire API becomes main-actor code, and you do not need to annotate each method individually. The trade-off is that any heavy work in such a type will also run on the main actor unless you deliberately move it out, so you should still keep heavy work in separate, non-main-actor helpers and deliver results to the type for presentation.

Applying @MainActor to a single property isolates that property to the main actor, while leaving the rest of the type unaffected. That can be useful when only one piece of state is interface-related, but it is less common than annotating a whole type. Property-level isolation requires careful thought about access patterns, because reading or writing that property from outside will require awaiting the main actor, even if the surrounding code is background work. Use it when the isolation boundary is genuinely at the property level, not as a shortcut to avoid designing the type's concurrency properly.

A related technique is to mark a closure or a Task as @MainActor, which makes that specific piece of work run on the main actor even if its surrounding context does not. For example, Task { @MainActor in } creates a Task whose body is main-actor isolated. That is handy for a small interface update inside a background flow, without marking an entire function. It keeps the main-actor scope as small as possible, which is good practice: isolate only the code that truly needs the main thread, and leave the rest free to run elsewhere.

When you choose where to place @MainActor, think about the type's role. If the type is a view model, main-actor isolation for the whole type often makes sense. If the type is a data service, it probably should not be main-actor isolated, because its work is background work. If a function is a pure helper, it should not be main-actor isolated either. Placing @MainActor thoughtfully, at the right granularity, gives you safety without sacrificing responsiveness, which is the balance every Swift app needs.

Section 35: Sendable and Safe Sharing

Sendable is a protocol that marks a type as safe to share across concurrent tasks. A type is Sendable if its values can be passed between tasks without creating a data race. Immutable value types, such as structs with only immutable properties, are often Sendable automatically. Classes are Sendable only if they are carefully designed, for example by being immutable or by synchronising their mutable state internally, such as with an actor or a lock. Sendable is Swift's way of making safe sharing explicit, so the compiler can warn you when you try to share something unsafe.

The concept matters because Task closures capture values from their surrounding context, and those captured values are shared with the new task. If a captured value is a mutable class instance shared with other tasks, concurrent access could race. If it is a Sendable value, sharing is safe by construction. Swift's strict concurrency checking therefore inspects Task captures and warns when a non-Sendable value crosses a task boundary in a way that could be unsafe. Those warnings are not pedantry; they are early alerts about potential races, delivered at compile time when they are cheapest to fix.

Many common types are Sendable without extra work. Integers, strings, and structs composed of Sendable properties are Sendable, because they are values copied on sharing, not shared references. That copying is safe: each task gets its own copy, so no race can occur. Actors are also Sendable, because their isolation protects their state even when shared by reference. Final classes with only immutable properties can be Sendable too, because immutability removes the race. Understanding these cases helps you design types that are naturally safe to share, without needing special annotations.

When you encounter a Sendable warning, do not silence it casually with an unchecked annotation unless you truly understand why the type is safe. An unchecked Sendable declaration tells the compiler to trust you, and if you are wrong, the race you suppressed will appear at runtime. Instead, first consider redesigning: make the type a struct, make its properties immutable, move mutable state into an actor, or copy the value before sharing. Those redesigns preserve the compiler's protection and often lead to cleaner code. Unchecked annotations should be a last resort, documented with a clear reason.

Sendable, together with actors and isolation, forms Swift's safety net for sharing. Actors protect mutable shared state, immutability makes sharing safe by default, and Sendable marks the boundary between safe and unsafe sharing for the compiler to check. Learning to think in Sendable terms, asking whether a value is safe to send to another task, will improve your concurrent designs even before you write any concurrency code, because it encourages immutable, well-bounded types throughout your app.

Section 36: Async Sequences in Plain Words

An async sequence is a sequence whose elements arrive over time, rather than all being available at once. An ordinary array holds all its elements immediately; you can count them, index them, and iterate them without waiting. An async sequence, by contrast, produces elements asynchronously: each element may require waiting, such as a network chunk arriving, a sensor reading appearing, or a stream of events occurring. You consume an async sequence with a for await loop, which suspends when the next element is not yet ready and resumes when it arrives. That model fits streaming data naturally.

Examples of async sequences are everywhere in modern apps. A stream of location updates, a sequence of messages arriving in a chat, a series of download progress reports, or lines arriving from a file being read over time are all async sequences. In each case, the total number of elements may be unknown in advance, and the elements arrive at irregular intervals. Trying to model such data as an array would force you to wait for all elements before processing any, which defeats the purpose of streaming. An async sequence lets you process each element as soon as it arrives, which feels responsive and uses memory efficiently.

Swift provides several built-in async sequences, and you can create your own, as you will learn soon. The protocol AsyncSequence defines the contract: a sequence has an iterator that can produce the next element asynchronously, returning nil when the sequence ends. That contract is simple, but it supports powerful patterns, including mapping, filtering, and combining sequences, similar to ordinary sequences but with waiting. The familiarity of sequence operations, combined with async waiting, makes async sequences approachable even though they handle time-varying data.

It is important to distinguish an async sequence from a TaskGroup. A TaskGroup manages a set of concurrent tasks and yields their results in completion order. An async sequence models a single stream of values over time, which may come from one source, not necessarily from many parallel tasks. The two tools overlap in that both use for await, but their purposes differ: groups are for parallel jobs, sequences are for streaming values. Choosing the right mental model helps you choose the right tool and write code that matches the shape of your data.

When you design with async sequences, think about termination and cancellation. A sequence may be infinite, such as a stream of sensor readings, or finite, such as lines in a file. For infinite sequences, you need a way to stop consuming, usually by breaking out of the for await loop when a condition is met or when the task is cancelled. For finite sequences, the loop ends naturally when the iterator returns nil. Handling both cases deliberately, with clear stopping conditions, prevents loops that run forever or consume resources after the user has moved on.

Section 37: for await Loops

A for await loop is how you consume an async sequence, one element at a time, waiting for each element as needed. You write for await, followed by a variable name and the sequence, and the loop body runs once per element. If the next element is not ready, the loop suspends, freeing the thread for other work, and resumes when the element arrives. If the sequence ends, the loop exits cleanly. That structure makes streaming code read like ordinary loop code, with waiting hidden inside the loop mechanics rather than spread through callbacks.

The loop body should handle each element promptly and safely. Because elements may arrive rapidly, avoid heavy work inside the body that could cause a backlog. If processing an element is slow, consider buffering, batching, or moving heavy processing to a separate task, while the loop itself stays light and focused on receiving. That separation keeps the stream flowing and prevents the producer from being blocked by slow consumption. Think of the loop as a conveyor belt receiver: it should take each item quickly and pass it on, not stop the belt to assemble the whole product.

Breaking out of a for await loop is how you stop consuming a sequence early. You might break when you find a desired element, when the user cancels, or when a timeout expires. Breaking is safe: the sequence's iterator is released, and any resources it holds can be cleaned up, provided the sequence is implemented correctly. For infinite sequences, breaking is essential, because otherwise the loop would never end. Always ensure your loop has a clear exit condition, whether that is sequence termination, a found result, or cancellation. A loop without an exit is a bug waiting to happen.

Cancellation interacts with for await loops in a useful way. If the enclosing Task is cancelled while the loop is suspended waiting for the next element, the wait may throw a cancellation error, or the loop may check cancellation and exit. You should handle that case gracefully, without treating cancellation as a failure. In a do-catch around the loop, distinguish cancellation from other errors, and clean up any partial state. That handling ensures that a cancelled stream stops quietly, rather than displaying an error for work the user intentionally abandoned.

Practise for await loops with simple streams, such as a sequence of numbers arriving with delays, before using them for real network or sensor streams. That practice builds intuition about suspension, completion order, and breaking conditions in a safe setting. Once the loop feels natural, applying it to real streams becomes straightforward, because the pattern is the same regardless of the source: wait for the next element, handle it, repeat until done or cancelled.

Section 38: AsyncStream Basics

AsyncStream is Swift's tool for creating an async sequence from code that produces values over time, such as callbacks, notifications, or events. You create an AsyncStream by providing a closure that receives a continuation, and you use that continuation to yield values as they become available, and to finish the stream when done. Consumers then use a for await loop to receive those values. AsyncStream therefore bridges the older callback world and the modern async world, letting you wrap event sources in a clean async interface.

A typical use is wrapping a delegate or notification source. Suppose an object calls a delegate method whenever new data arrives. Instead of forcing callers to implement the delegate, you can create an AsyncStream whose continuation yields each new piece of data, and whose finishing logic unregisters the delegate when the stream ends. Callers then simply loop over the stream, without worrying about delegate lifecycles. That wrapping pattern is powerful, because it turns many awkward APIs into straightforward async loops, and it centralises lifecycle management in one place.

AsyncStream also has buffering policies, which control what happens when values are produced faster than they are consumed. The default policy buffers values, so slow consumers do not lose data, but unbounded buffering can consume memory if production vastly outpaces consumption. Alternative policies can drop the oldest or newest values when the buffer is full, which is appropriate for streams where only the latest value matters, such as location updates. Choosing a buffering policy is a design decision: ask whether every value matters, or only the latest, and how much memory you are willing to use for buffering.

Finishing an AsyncStream is important. When the producer is done, it should call finish on the continuation, so consumers' loops exit cleanly. If the producer never finishes, consumers will wait forever unless they break or are cancelled. Similarly, when consumers stop early by breaking, the producer should clean up, for example by removing observers, which can be done in the stream's onTermination handler. That handler runs when the stream ends for any reason, including cancellation, and it is the right place for cleanup code. Neglecting termination handling is a common source of leaks in stream code.

AsyncStream is also useful for testing. You can create a stream, yield a few test values, finish it, and verify that consumer code handles them correctly, without needing a real network or sensor. That testability makes stream logic easier to verify than callback logic, where tests must simulate delegate calls in the right order. As you learn to build streams, practise both production wrapping and test streams, so you become comfortable with continuations, buffering, and termination from both sides.

let stream = AsyncStream<Int> { continuation in
    continuation.yield(1)
    continuation.finish()
}

When you design a stream, think carefully about who owns the producer. If the producer is a network connection or a sensor, closing it promptly when the stream ends saves battery and bandwidth. The termination handler is the right place for that cleanup, because it runs whether the stream ends normally, is cancelled, or is abandoned by a consumer that breaks early.

Section 39: Building Your Own AsyncSequence

Building your own AsyncSequence means defining a type that conforms to the AsyncSequence protocol, with an associated iterator that produces elements asynchronously. That sounds formal, but the idea is simple: your sequence knows how to create an iterator, and your iterator knows how to produce the next element, waiting if necessary, and returning nil when done. Once you implement those two pieces, your type can be consumed with for await, just like any built-in async sequence, and it can participate in sequence operations such as mapping and filtering.

A good first custom sequence is a simple one, such as a countdown that yields numbers with delays, or a sequence that yields lines from a source one at a time. Implementing a simple sequence teaches you the protocol requirements without the complexity of real networking or hardware. You will define an iterator struct with a mutating next method that is async and returns an optional element. Inside next, you can await a delay, compute the next value, and return it, or return nil to signal the end. That method is the heart of the sequence, and keeping it focused makes the whole type easy to understand.

State management in iterators deserves care. An iterator is a struct with mutable state, such as a current index or a remaining count, and each call to next updates that state. Because iterators are typically used by a single consumer at a time, that mutable state is safe, provided you do not share the same iterator across concurrent tasks. If you need concurrent consumption, you need a different design, such as an AsyncStream with multiple consumers handled carefully. For most cases, single-consumer iteration is the correct assumption, and you should document it.

Error handling in custom sequences uses the throwing variant of the protocol when elements can fail to be produced. A throwing iterator's next method can throw, and consumers handle errors with a do-catch around the for await loop, or with for try await syntax. Decide whether your sequence's failures should be thrown or represented as values, based on what consumers need. If a failure means the sequence cannot continue, throwing is appropriate. If a failure is just one bad element among many good ones, representing it as a value, such as an optional or a result, may be better.

Custom async sequences are an advanced but rewarding skill. They let you model any time-varying data source as a clean, composable sequence, rather than as a tangle of callbacks. Start simple, test with a for await loop that prints each element, and verify termination and cancellation behaviour. Once a simple sequence works, you can apply the same pattern to real sources, such as paginated network APIs, where each next call fetches the next page, or file readers, where each next call reads the next chunk.

        var current = 3

            guard current > 0 else { return nil }
            defer { current -= 1 }
            return current
        }
    }
    func makeAsyncIterator() -> AsyncIterator { AsyncIterator() }
}

Section 40: Timeouts and Slow Work

Timeouts protect your app from waiting forever for work that may never finish. A network request to an unresponsive server, a stream that never produces its next element, or a task that hangs on a slow calculation can all leave the user waiting indefinitely if you do not impose a limit. A timeout says: if this work has not finished within a reasonable time, stop waiting, cancel it, and report a timeout so the user can retry or move on. That bound transforms an indefinite wait into a manageable failure, which is far better for user experience.

Swift does not have a single built-in timeout operator for all async work, so timeouts are typically implemented by racing work against a delay, using a TaskGroup or a Task that sleeps and then cancels. One pattern is to start the real work as a child task, start a timeout task that sleeps for the limit, and whichever finishes first wins: if the work finishes first, cancel the timeout; if the timeout finishes first, cancel the work and report a timeout error. That race pattern is a good exercise in structured concurrency, because it uses groups and cancellation together. Implement it carefully, ensuring both branches clean up correctly.

Choosing a timeout duration is a design decision, not a magic number. Too short, and legitimate slow work on poor connections will fail unnecessarily, frustrating users. Too long, and users will still wait painfully before learning something is wrong. Consider the operation: a quick lookup might deserve a few seconds, while a large download might deserve minutes, with progress updates along the way. Also consider retrying: a short timeout with a retry may feel better than a long timeout with no retry, because the user sees progress and the system can try a different server or route. Match timeouts to user expectations for each operation.

Slow work that is not a timeout case also needs handling. Long calculations should be broken into chunks, progress should be reported, and cancellation should be checked regularly, so the user can stop work they no longer want. Slow work that blocks the main actor is particularly harmful, because it freezes the interface, so move it off the main actor and report progress back to the main actor periodically. That combination of chunking, progress, and cancellation turns slow work from a frustration into a tolerable, transparent process.

Finally, always communicate timeouts and slowness honestly. Show a message that says the operation timed out and offers a retry, rather than a generic failure or silence. Show progress for slow work, even if it is only a spinner with a reassuring message. Users tolerate waiting better when they know what is happening and have a next step. Timeouts are therefore not just technical safeguards; they are part of honest communication between your app and its users.

Section 41: Retrying Async Work Safely

Retrying is the practice of attempting an async operation again after it fails, in the hope that a transient problem, such as a brief network glitch, has resolved. Retrying can dramatically improve reliability, because many real failures are temporary. A single failed request does not necessarily mean the server is down or the data is invalid; it may mean a packet was lost, a connection was briefly interrupted, or a server was momentarily busy. A thoughtful retry can turn such a failure into a success without bothering the user, which feels like magic when it works well.

Safe retrying requires limits. Unlimited retries can hammer a struggling server, drain battery, and delay a genuine failure report indefinitely. A good retry policy specifies a maximum number of attempts, such as three, and a delay between attempts, often increasing with each attempt, a strategy called exponential backoff. For example, wait one second, then two seconds, then four seconds. That increasing delay gives the server time to recover and reduces load during an outage, while still giving the operation a fair chance. Implement retries with a loop that awaits a delay between attempts and checks cancellation before each retry.

Not all failures should be retried. Network timeouts and temporary server errors are often retryable, while authentication failures, invalid input, and permanent not-found errors are not. Retrying a request with bad credentials will fail every time and may lock the account. Retrying invalid input will waste time without changing the outcome. Design your error types to distinguish retryable from non-retryable failures, and only retry the former. That distinction is a key part of safe retry design, and it prevents retries from turning a clear failure into a confusing delay.

Cancellation must be respected during retries. If the user cancels while a retry delay is waiting, the operation should stop, not continue retrying after cancellation. Check for cancellation before each attempt and after each delay, and propagate cancellation errors promptly. Similarly, if the overall operation has a timeout, retries should respect it, not extend the total time indefinitely. A retry loop that ignores cancellation and timeouts is worse than no retry at all, because it keeps working after the user has moved on.

Finally, log retries and report them honestly when appropriate. For background work, logging retries helps debugging: you can see that an operation succeeded on the second attempt, rather than wondering why it was slow. For user-visible work, you usually should not show a message for each retry, because that would be noisy, but you should show a final message if all retries fail, explaining that the operation could not complete and offering a manual retry. That balance, silent retries for transient failures and clear reporting for ultimate failure, gives users reliability without noise.

    var lastError: Error?
    for _ in 0..<3 {
        do { return try await fetchAge().description } catch { lastError = error }
    }
    throw lastError!
}

Section 42: Testing async Code

Testing async code means writing tests that can await async functions, verify their results, and check their error and cancellation behaviour. In modern Swift testing, test functions themselves can be async, so they can await the code under test directly, just like production callers. That symmetry makes async tests readable: arrange inputs, await the function, assert on the result. You do not need special waiting helpers or expectations for simple async tests, because the test itself suspends at awaits and resumes with results, exactly as production code does.

Good async tests cover success, failure, and cancellation. A success test verifies that a function returns the expected value for typical input. A failure test verifies that it throws the expected error for bad input or a failing dependency. A cancellation test verifies that a long-running function stops promptly when its Task is cancelled, rather than running to completion. Covering all three outcomes is important, because concurrent code can fail in ways that success-only tests miss. A function that returns correct values but ignores cancellation will pass success tests and still waste resources in production.

Dependencies in async tests should be controlled. If your function fetches from a real network, your test will be slow, flaky, and dependent on external services. Instead, inject a fake or mock dependency that returns predetermined values, throws predetermined errors, or simulates delays. That control lets you test success, failure, and slow behaviour deterministically, without network variability. Fakes also let you test timing-sensitive behaviour, such as cancellation during a delay, by using short, controlled delays rather than real network times. Deterministic tests are trustworthy tests.

Testing actors and TaskGroups, covered next, builds on the same principles, but with additional attention to ordering and concurrency. For ordinary async functions, the main challenge is ensuring tests do not become flaky due to timing. Avoid asserting on exact timing, such as requiring a function to finish within a precise number of milliseconds, because machine load can vary. Instead, assert on outcomes: values, errors, and whether cancellation was honoured. Use generous timeouts for test infrastructure, and keep test delays short, so the suite remains fast while still exercising async paths.

Finally, keep async tests focused and isolated. Each test should verify one behaviour, set up its own dependencies, and clean up after itself, including cancelling any Tasks it created. Tests that share mutable state or leave Tasks running can interfere with each other, producing flaky failures that are hard to diagnose. That isolation discipline mirrors the discipline of production concurrency: clear ownership, explicit cancellation, and no shared mutable surprises. Well-written async tests not only verify your code; they document how it is supposed to behave under waiting, failure, and cancellation.

func testFetch() async {
    let name = await fetchName()
    print(name)
}

Another useful test is a test that verifies a function does not return too early. For example, a function that should fetch two items in parallel should not return after only one item is ready. A fake dependency that records the order and timing of calls can reveal such premature returns, which success-only tests with instant fakes might otherwise miss completely.

Section 43: Testing Actors and TaskGroups

Testing actors requires verifying that their state remains correct under concurrent access, not just under sequential calls. A sequential test that calls actor methods one after another will pass even for an unsafe design, because it never exercises concurrency. A concurrent test, by contrast, creates many tasks that call actor methods simultaneously, awaits all of them, and then checks the final state. For example, incrementing a counter from a hundred concurrent tasks should result in exactly one hundred, if the actor is correct. That test would fail for a shared variable without actor protection, which is precisely the point.

When testing actors, use TaskGroups or async let to create genuine concurrency, and await all tasks before asserting. Do not rely on sleeps or timing to ensure tasks have run; instead, structure the test so completion is guaranteed by awaiting. For example, use a TaskGroup that adds a task per increment, collect its results, and then check the actor's final value. That structure ensures the test waits for all work, regardless of scheduling, and produces deterministic results. Flaky actor tests usually come from incomplete waiting, not from actor bugs.

Testing TaskGroups involves verifying both results and coordination. A test should check that a group returns the correct collection of results, that it handles errors as designed, and that cancellation propagates to children. For result tests, use known inputs and verify outputs, including order handling if order is supposed to be preserved. For error tests, make one child throw and verify that the group reports the error and other children stop, if that is the designed behaviour. For cancellation tests, cancel the parent and verify that children do not keep running indefinitely. Each of those tests exercises a different aspect of group coordination.

It is also valuable to test the absence of deadlocks. A group or actor test that hangs indefinitely is a signal, not just an inconvenience. Use test timeouts to detect hangs, and investigate them as potential deadlocks or missing termination conditions, rather than simply increasing the timeout. Deadlocks in actor code often come from an actor method awaiting another actor method that waits back on the first actor, forming a cycle. Tests that exercise realistic call patterns can reveal such cycles early, before they appear in production under rare timing conditions.

In summary, testing concurrent structures means testing under concurrency, with complete waiting, deterministic inputs, and coverage of success, error, and cancellation. Sequential tests are necessary but not sufficient. Concurrent tests are the ones that prove actors and groups actually provide the safety they promise. Write them early, run them repeatedly, and treat flakiness as information, not as noise to be ignored, because flaky concurrent tests often point to real races or coordination bugs.

Section 44: Common Mistake: Forgetting await

Forgetting await is the most basic concurrency mistake, and Swift's compiler usually catches it, which is fortunate. If you call an async function without await in a context that requires it, you will get a compile error, not silent misbehaviour. That error is a helpful reminder that waiting cannot be ignored. However, there are subtler forms of forgetting await that the compiler cannot fully prevent, such as starting a Task and forgetting to await its value, or using an async let binding and forgetting to await it before its scope ends. Those omissions can lead to lost results, unhandled errors, or wasted work.

One subtle case is fire-and-forget Tasks where the result matters. A developer might start a Task to save data, assume it will complete, and never check whether it succeeded. If the save fails, the failure is silent, and the user believes their data is safe when it is not. The fix is to either await the Task's value where the result is needed, or handle errors inside the Task's closure and report them through safe state. Fire-and-forget should be reserved for work whose outcome truly does not matter, such as best-effort logging, and even then, errors should be logged, not ignored entirely.

Another subtle case is awaiting in the wrong order, which is not forgetting await entirely but forgetting that await order affects performance. Awaiting two independent async calls sequentially, one after another, loses the parallelism that async let or a TaskGroup would provide. The code is correct but slow, because the second call does not start until the first finishes. The fix is to start both jobs before awaiting either, using async let or a group. Recognising sequential awaits as a performance smell, not just a correctness issue, helps you write code that is both correct and efficient.

Forgetting await also appears in actor contexts, where a developer might try to access actor state directly instead of awaiting a method call. The compiler will reject direct access, but the temptation to work around it, by moving state out of the actor or marking methods nonisolated incorrectly, is a form of forgetting the protection await represents. Resist that temptation. Await is not boilerplate to be removed; it is the visible cost of safe, coordinated access. Keeping it visible keeps the code honest about waiting and protection.

The best defence against forgetting await is to read your code for waiting points. Scan for async calls and check that each one is awaited, handled, or deliberately fire-and-forget with error handling. Use compiler warnings as allies, not annoyances, and treat any warning about an unused async let binding or an unawaited Task as a prompt to decide explicitly what should happen to that work. Explicit decisions, even when they are to ignore a result, are better than accidental omissions.

    print(name)
}

Section 45: Common Mistake: Blocking the Main Thread

Blocking the main thread is a mistake that makes apps feel frozen, and it often happens indirectly, through code that looks innocent. A synchronous network call, a long loop, a large file read, or a heavy calculation performed on the main actor will block interface updates, taps, and animations until it finishes. The user sees a frozen screen, even though the app is working hard. The fix is to move slow or unpredictable work off the main actor, and to reserve the main actor for quick interface updates only. That separation is the central discipline of responsive apps.

Blocking can also happen through await misuse. Awaiting a long task on the main actor does not block the thread in the same way as a synchronous block, because suspension frees the thread, but awaiting a task that itself blocks, or performing synchronous waiting inside an async function on the main actor, can still freeze the interface. For example, using a semaphore or a busy loop to wait for async work on the main actor defeats suspension and blocks the thread. Avoid synchronous waiting primitives in async code, especially on the main actor. Let awaits suspend, rather than forcing threads to wait.

Another indirect block comes from marking too much code @MainActor. If a view model is main-actor isolated, and it performs a large data transformation in one of its methods, that transformation runs on the main thread and blocks the interface, even though no explicit thread code is present. The solution is to split the work: perform the transformation in a non-isolated helper or background task, then deliver the ready result to the main actor for a quick update. That split requires a little extra code, but it preserves responsiveness, which is worth far more than the saved lines.

Detecting main-thread blocks requires observation. If your app freezes during a particular operation, suspect main-thread work. Instruments and thread checks can confirm it, but even simple reasoning helps: ask where this code runs, and whether it could take a long or unpredictable time. If the answer is yes and the context is the main actor, move it. Profiling under realistic conditions, with slow networks and large data, reveals blocks that fast test conditions hide. Test on older devices and poor connections, not just on fast simulators.

Finally, remember that blocking the main thread is not just a performance issue; it is a trust issue. Users interpret freezes as crashes, unreliability, or poor craftsmanship, and they may abandon the app. Keeping the main thread free is therefore a user-experience requirement, not an optimisation for enthusiasts. Design every screen with that requirement in mind: load in the background, show progress on the main actor, and update quickly when results arrive. That pattern, repeated consistently, prevents the most common and most visible concurrency mistake.

Section 46: Common Mistake: Ignoring Cancellation

Ignoring cancellation is a mistake that wastes resources and confuses users. When a Task is cancelled, its code is expected to stop promptly, but if it never checks for cancellation and never awaits a cancellable operation, it will run to completion regardless. The user may have tapped cancel, navigated away, or started a new search, yet the old work continues, consuming battery, network, and CPU, and possibly delivering a stale result that overwrites newer content. That behaviour feels stubborn and wasteful, and it is entirely preventable with regular cancellation checks.

Cancellation is ignored most often in loops and in long calculations without awaits. A loop that processes thousands of items without checking Task.isCancelled will finish all items even after cancellation, because there is no suspension point where cancellation can intervene. A calculation that runs for seconds without awaits similarly cannot be interrupted cooperatively. The fix is to check cancellation at natural boundaries, such as each loop iteration or between calculation chunks, and to break or throw when cancelled. Those checks are cheap when not cancelled, and invaluable when cancellation occurs.

Another form of ignoring cancellation is catching a cancellation error and treating it as a generic failure. If a network call throws CancellationError and your catch block shows a frightening error alert, the user will see an error for work they intentionally cancelled, which is confusing. Instead, distinguish cancellation from other errors, and handle it quietly: stop, clean up, and return without alarming the user. That distinction requires explicit code, such as checking for CancellationError in a catch block, or using a cancellation handler. It is a small effort that greatly improves the user experience.

Ignoring cancellation also affects structured concurrency. If a parent is cancelled, its children should stop, but children that ignore cancellation will keep running until the group waits for them, delaying the parent's exit. That delay can make cancellation feel slow, even though the parent tried to cancel. Ensuring children check cancellation, and that group code does not wait indefinitely for uncooperative children, keeps cancellation responsive. In tests, verify that cancelled groups actually finish promptly, rather than assuming they will.

The habit to build is simple: whenever you write long or looping async work, ask where cancellation should be checked. Add checks, handle cancellation errors separately, and clean up resources. Whenever you write a retry or timeout loop, check cancellation before each attempt. Whenever you update the interface after background work, check cancellation first. Those habits, applied consistently, make cancellation a real, user-visible feature, rather than a flag that is set but never honoured. Responsive apps respect the user's decision to stop, promptly and quietly.

func loopWork() async throws {
    for i in 0..<100 {
        try Task.checkCancellation()
        print(i)
    }
}

A related mistake is checking cancellation only at the start of a long operation. A check at the start is useful, but it does nothing if cancellation arrives halfway through. The user expects cancellation to work at any point, not only before work begins, so checks must be distributed throughout the operation, especially before each new expensive step and before any final update.

Section 47: Common Mistake: Sharing Mutable State

Sharing mutable state across tasks without protection is the classic concurrency mistake, and it causes data races. A developer might declare a variable outside a Task, capture it in several Tasks, and mutate it from each, assuming the mutations will interleave safely. They will not. Without an actor, a lock, or another synchronisation mechanism, concurrent mutations can lose updates, corrupt structures, or produce inconsistent results. The code may appear to work in tests with few tasks, then fail intermittently in production with many tasks, which is the hallmark of a race.

The temptation to share mutable state comes from convenience. It is easy to write a variable, capture it, and update it, without designing an actor or a safe boundary. That ease is deceptive, because the cost appears later, in debugging and in user-visible bugs. A better approach is to design sharing explicitly from the start: if several tasks need to update the same state, place that state in an actor and update it through actor methods. If tasks only need to read shared data, make that data immutable, so sharing is safe by construction. If each task needs its own working copy, give it a copy, not a shared reference.

Another mistake is sharing mutable state through a class that looks safe but is not. A class with mutable properties, shared across tasks, is not safe just because it is a class or because its methods are short. Safety comes from synchronisation, not from type. If a class is shared, it must either be immutable, be an actor, or synchronise internally with a lock or similar mechanism. Assuming safety from appearance, rather than from design, is a common source of races. When in doubt, ask how simultaneous access is prevented, and if you cannot answer, redesign.

Captures in Task closures deserve special attention. A Task closure captures variables from its context, and if those variables are mutable references, the Task shares them with the creator and with other Tasks. That sharing is often unintentional: the developer meant to capture a value, but captured a reference instead. Using value types, such as structs, for captured data reduces this risk, because each Task gets its own copy. Using Sendable checks helps too, because the compiler will warn about unsafe captures. Pay attention to those warnings; they are pointing at exactly this mistake.

Fixing shared mutable state usually means restructuring, not adding a quick lock in one place. Move the state into an actor, define clear update methods, and let tasks interact through those methods. That restructuring may feel like extra work, but it replaces a fragile, implicit sharing pattern with a clear, compiler-checked boundary. The result is code that is easier to reason about, easier to test concurrently, and far less likely to produce intermittent failures. Safe sharing is not an afterthought; it is a design decision that should be made before concurrent code is written.

    func add(_ item: String) { items.append(item) }
}

Section 48: Choosing Between Task, TaskGroup, and Actors

Choosing between Task, TaskGroup, and actors is a design decision based on what problem you are solving. Tasks represent individual pieces of concurrent work, especially unstructured work started from synchronous contexts. TaskGroups represent dynamic collections of parallel jobs that should be managed as a coordinated group. Actors represent shared mutable state that needs protection. Those roles are distinct, and confusing them leads to awkward code: using a Task to protect state, using a group to hold state, or using an actor to run parallel jobs. Keep the roles clear, and your designs will be clearer too.

Start by asking what you need. If you need to run one piece of work concurrently with current work, and you need its result or cancellation handle, use a Task, or simply await if sequential execution is fine. If you need to run many similar jobs in parallel and collect their results, use async let for a fixed number or a TaskGroup for a dynamic number. If you need to protect shared data that many tasks will read and write, use an actor. That decision tree covers most cases, and it prevents over-engineering: do not create a group for a single job, and do not create an actor for work that has no shared state.

Combinations are common and powerful. A TaskGroup's children might call actor methods to update shared state safely, combining parallel work with protected state. An actor method might start a TaskGroup to fetch many items in parallel, then store the results in the actor. A Task started from a button handler might use async let to fetch several pieces of data, then deliver them to a main-actor view model. Those combinations work because each tool does its own job: groups parallelise, actors protect, and Tasks bridge contexts. Designing with combinations in mind, rather than searching for a single tool that does everything, leads to robust architectures.

Also consider lifetime and error handling when choosing. Structured tools, async let and TaskGroup, give you automatic lifetime, cancellation, and error coordination, which is valuable for work scoped to a function. Unstructured Tasks give you flexibility but require manual lifetime management, which is valuable only when no scope fits. Actors give you state protection but do not parallelise work by themselves; they serialise access to state. Matching the tool's guarantees to your needs, rather than to familiarity, is the key skill. If you always reach for Task because it is familiar, you will miss the safety of structure and the protection of actors.

Finally, practise choosing by refactoring. Take a piece of code that uses manual Tasks and ask whether a group would be clearer. Take code that shares a variable and ask whether an actor would be safer. Take code that does sequential awaits and ask whether async let would be faster. Those refactoring exercises build judgement faster than reading about tools in isolation, because they connect tool choice to real code shapes. Over time, the choice will become intuitive: parallel jobs suggest groups, shared state suggests actors, and bridges suggest Tasks.

Section 49: A Small Complete Concurrency Example

A small complete example ties together the ideas of this chapter in a realistic flow. Imagine a screen that shows a user's name and their recent posts. Loading that screen requires two independent fetches: one for the profile, one for the posts. Using async let, you start both fetches concurrently, await both results together, and then deliver them to the main actor for display. If either fetch fails, you catch the error and show a friendly message. If the user navigates away, you cancel the loading Task, and the fetches stop cooperatively. That single flow uses async, await, Task, async let, error handling, cancellation, and MainActor together.

The structure of that example is worth studying. The button or view-appearance handler starts a Task, because it is synchronous. Inside the Task, a loading state is shown on the main actor, then async let starts the parallel fetches. The Task awaits both results, checks for cancellation before updating, and then hops to the main actor to present the data or an error. Each step has a clear role, and each concurrency tool is used for its intended purpose. No tool is stretched beyond its role, and no step is hidden. A reader can follow the flow from tap to display without guessing about threads or callbacks.

You can extend that example in instructive ways. Replace async let with a TaskGroup if the posts are fetched per category, with a dynamic number of categories. Add an actor to cache profiles, so repeated loads do not refetch unnecessarily, and to protect the cache from concurrent access. Add a retry policy for transient network failures, with backoff and a limit. Add a timeout, so a hung fetch does not leave the user waiting forever. Each extension introduces one new tool or pattern, while keeping the core flow intact, which is a good way to practise without overwhelming yourself.

Testing that example is also instructive. You can test the fetch functions separately with fake dependencies, test the actor cache with concurrent increments, and test the loading flow with a fake that simulates slow, failing, and cancelled fetches. Those tests verify each layer independently, which is easier than testing the whole flow at once. When a test fails, you know which layer is responsible, because the layers are separated. That separation, between fetching, caching, and presentation, is a hallmark of good concurrent design, not just good testing.

Keep this example in mind as a template. Many real screens follow the same shape: start from a user action, show loading, fetch in parallel, handle errors and cancellation, and update on the main actor. Variations change the details, such as the number of fetches or the caching strategy, but the shape remains. Mastering that shape gives you a reliable starting point for most concurrent features, and it prevents you from reinventing the flow, and its bugs, for every new screen.

Section 50: Review: Thinking in async and await

Thinking in async and await means thinking in terms of waiting, not threads. Instead of asking which thread runs this code, ask where this code may pause, what it waits for, and what should happen while it waits. That shift in thinking is the core skill of this chapter. Threads still exist underneath, and Swift's scheduler still manages them, but you do not need to manage them directly for most work. You need to mark waiting honestly with async and await, structure parallel work with async let and TaskGroup, protect shared state with actors, and update the interface on MainActor. Those habits cover the vast majority of concurrent needs.

Review the mental models that help. Concurrency is overlapping work, not necessarily simultaneous work. Await is a polite suspension, not a block. A Task is a job ticket you can cancel and await. Structured concurrency ties child lifetimes to a visible scope, so nothing leaks. Actors are safe boxes with a single key, and MainActor is the special box for the interface. Async sequences are streams of values over time, consumed with for await. Data races are silent, intermittent failures from unprotected shared mutation, and Sendable marks safe sharing. Holding those models together gives you a coherent picture, rather than a list of disconnected keywords.

Common mistakes are also worth reviewing, because avoiding them is as important as using tools correctly. Do not forget await, in its subtle forms as well as its obvious form. Do not block the main thread, directly or through overuse of MainActor. Do not ignore cancellation, in loops, retries, or interface updates. Do not share mutable state without protection, through captures or shared classes. Do not use unstructured or detached tasks casually when structured alternatives fit. Each mistake has a simple counter-habit: await explicitly, keep the main actor quick, check cancellation, protect state, and prefer structure. Those counter-habits are easy to state and require practice to internalise.

Your next steps should be practical. Build the screen-loading example from the previous section with fake fetches, then extend it with a cache actor, a TaskGroup, and a retry policy. Test it for success, failure, and cancellation. Then apply the same pattern to a real feature in your own app, starting small and verifying responsiveness on a slow connection. Concurrency skill grows through building and testing, not through reading alone. The patterns in this chapter are tools, and tools become familiar only through use, mistakes, and fixes.

Finally, remember that concurrency is ultimately about users. A responsive app that shows honest progress, respects cancellation, and handles failures gracefully feels trustworthy, even when networks are slow and servers fail. An app that freezes, ignores cancellation, or loses data feels unreliable, even when its features are impressive. Swift's concurrency tools exist to help you build the trustworthy kind of app, with code that is readable, testable, and safe. Use them with that goal in mind, and your apps will feel alive in the way users notice, even if they never learn the words behind it.
