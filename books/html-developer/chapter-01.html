Chapter 1: HTML, Browsers and Your First Page

Section 1: What HTML Is and Why It Exists

Do not memorise every detail at once, and instead learn the pattern behind what html is and why it exists so that you can apply it in new situations. Patterns repeat across HTML, and once you recognise a pattern you will spot it again in pages you did not write yourself. Try explaining what html is and why it exists to a friend in plain words, because explaining forces you to notice any gap in your own understanding. If you cannot explain a choice plainly, that is a useful signal to pause, test a smaller example, and look again at the result. Examples in this book are deliberately short, because a short example lets you focus on one idea without extra distraction.

After studying an example, close the book or hide the example and try to rebuild it from memory, since rebuilding reveals what you truly know. Mistakes are a normal part of learning markup, and the fastest learners are usually the ones who check their mistakes carefully rather than hiding them. One common mistake is to copy a pattern without understanding it, which works until the pattern needs to change for a new page. Another common mistake is to add markup for appearance alone, which makes the page harder to maintain and harder for assistive tools to interpret. A third common mistake is to skip testing, assuming that code which looks right must behave right, when only a browser test can confirm it.

<!DOCTYPE html>
<html lang="en">
<head>
  <meta charset="utf-8">
  <title>My first page</title>
</head>
<body>
  <h1>Hello, web</h1>
  <p>This is my first page.</p>
</body>
</html>

Good markup is often invisible to a casual reader, and that invisibility is a strength, because the reader can focus on the content itself. Think about a reader on a small phone, a reader using a keyboard only, and a reader using a screen reader, since all three use the same markup differently. Designing for those readers is not extra work added at the end, but part of writing honest and useful markup from the start. Keep notes on what you tried and what happened, especially when a result surprises you, because those notes become your personal reference.

Over time your notes will show your progress clearly, and they will save you from solving the same small problem twice. When you feel stuck, return to the question of what the content means, and let that meaning guide your next small step. Break a larger page into small sections in your mind before you write any code, because a clear plan makes the writing itself much easier. A plan does not need to be formal or long, and a short list of headings and paragraphs is often enough to start with confidence.

Write the words first when you can, then add markup around them, because words carry the meaning and markup carries the structure. This order also prevents a common trap, which is to build an empty structure and then struggle to fit real content inside it. Test your page by reading it as a document, not only by looking at it, because reading reveals problems that appearance can hide. Use the browser tools to inspect a part that puzzles you, and compare what you intended with what the browser actually understood.
