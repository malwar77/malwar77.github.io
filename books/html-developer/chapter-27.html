Chapter 27: Validation: Finding and Fixing Markup Errors

Section 1: What Validation Means

When you study what validation means, you are learning to use validation as a normal part of writing, not as a punishment at the end. A page can look correct on your screen while still containing structural errors that cause trouble elsewhere. Validation gives you a second pair of eyes, one that checks rules systematically rather than by impression. This section explains what to look for, how to read reports without panic, and how to turn each message into a small, confident fix.

To grasp what validation means, think of a validator as a careful proofreader for structure. It reads the whole page, checks each element against the language rules, and lists anything that does not fit. It will not tell you whether your words are clear or your design is attractive, and that is fine, because those are separate jobs. Its value lies in precision: it notices a missing closing tag, a misplaced element or a duplicate identifier that a busy human eye might pass over without a second thought.

A frequent pitfall with what validation means is ignoring warnings entirely, or treating every warning as an emergency. Warnings and errors are different, and a good author learns to tell them apart. Some warnings point to genuine risks worth fixing, while others simply note a choice you made knowingly. The mistake is to decide without reading. Read each message, understand what it is asking, and then decide deliberately, rather than dismissing the whole report or chasing every note blindly.

To grow skill with what validation means, practise on pages of increasing size. Start with a single page, then check a template that is reused across a site, and notice how one fix can improve many pages at once. Share reports with teammates when you work together, and discuss the messages you find confusing. Validation is a shared language for quality, and talking about it openly helps a whole team write stronger markup, rather than leaving each author to guess alone. Fixing markup early is cheaper than fixing it later, when the same pattern has been copied across many pages. Each report is a lesson about the rules of the language, not a judgement about the person who wrote it. Keep a calm, methodical pace, and treat every error as a separate small job rather than a large failure. Valid pages are easier for browsers, search tools and assistive technology to interpret in the same way. When a page works in one browser but not another, a validation report is often the fastest place to start.

Section 2: Why Valid Markup Matters

Section 3: The Difference Between Valid and Working

This section introduces the difference between valid and working as part of validation, starting from a reassuring fact: every author makes markup mistakes, and validation exists to catch them kindly. A validator is a tool that checks a page against the rules of the language and reports problems in plain language. It does not judge your ideas or your design. It simply points to places where structure, attributes or nesting do not follow the rules, so you can fix them before readers, browsers or assistive tools run into trouble.

The central idea behind the difference between valid and working is that markup follows rules, and tools can test those rules for you. Elements have allowed parents and children, attributes have allowed values, and some elements require particular partners, such as a label for a control or alternative text for an image. When a rule is broken, a validator reports it, often with a line reference and a short explanation. Understanding that report is the main skill here, because the fix usually follows naturally once you see which rule was broken and where.
