Chapter 18: Figures, Captions and Media Groups

Section 1: What a figure Does on a Page

A picture, a diagram, or a chart rarely speaks entirely for itself, and this section looks closely at what a figure does on a page. A figure groups a piece of media together with the words that explain it, so the media and its explanation travel as one unit instead of drifting apart when a page is restyled or read aloud. That grouping is a statement of meaning: it says these words belong with this image, and together they form one idea.

The figure element is the container for this grouping, and the figcaption element holds the caption inside it. The caption is not a decoration and it is not a repeat of the image description; it is the sentence or two that tells the reader what to notice, why the media matters, or where it came from. A strong caption adds information the image alone cannot give, such as the result shown in a chart, the step shown in a diagram, or the source of a photograph. A weak caption merely restates what is already obvious, and that wastes the reader's time without adding understanding.

When you write the markup for what a figure does on a page, start by choosing the media and writing its caption before you worry about placement. Place the caption inside the figure, either before or after the media, and keep it short enough to read at a glance while still saying something useful. Then check the figure in context: does the surrounding text refer to it clearly, does the caption make sense on its own, and does the whole group still read well if the page is viewed on a narrow screen? If any answer is no, revise the words first, because a figure is only as good as the explanation that travels with it.

Access is where what a figure does on a page shows its real value. A screen reader can announce a figure and its caption together, which helps a listener understand the relationship between media and explanation. That does not remove the need for good alt text on an image, because alt text and a caption do different jobs: alt text replaces the image for someone who cannot see it, while the caption explains the image for everyone. Writing both, with different words and different purposes, is one of the simplest ways to make a media-rich page genuinely welcoming instead of merely decorated.

Finish by asking three plain questions about what a figure does on a page. Does the media and its caption together form one clear idea? Does the caption add information rather than repeating what is already visible? And would the figure still make sense if it were moved to another part of the page? If any answer is no, adjust the grouping or the words before publishing. That short review keeps figures purposeful, keeps captions honest, and helps every reader, whether they see the media or hear it described, understand exactly what it is for.
