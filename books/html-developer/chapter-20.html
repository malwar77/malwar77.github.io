Chapter 20: IDs, Classes and Naming Well

Section 1: What an id Does on a Page

Names are how you point at a particular part of a page, and this section looks closely at what an id does on a page. An identifier names one specific element, while a class names a group of elements that share a purpose, and choosing the right one, with a clear name, makes a page easier to style, link to, and maintain. A good name tells the next reader, who may be you in six months, what the part is for, without needing to guess from its appearance.

The difference between the two kinds of name is simple to state and easy to forget under pressure. An id must be unique on a page, because it points at exactly one element, which is why it is used for anchor links, skip links, and connecting a label to its field. A class, by contrast, may be reused as often as needed, because it describes a shared pattern, such as a card, a note, or a navigation item.

When you write the markup for what an id does on a page, start by asking what the part is for, not what it looks like today. A name based on meaning, such as a name for a main article or a contact form, survives a redesign, while a name based on colour, size, or position breaks the moment the design changes. Keep names short enough to type accurately, long enough to be understood, and consistent in style across the whole page, so similar parts receive similar names. Then search the page for every use of the name you chose, and check that each use matches the meaning you intended, because a name used for two different purposes is a name that no longer means anything.

Names also matter for readers, not only for authors. An anchor link that points at a well-named identifier lets a reader jump straight to a section, and a skip link lets a keyboard user bypass repeated navigation and reach the main content quickly. Those benefits depend on identifiers being unique and descriptive, so a link lands where the reader expects it to land. Classes matter differently: consistent class names make repeated patterns predictable, which helps everyone who reads, styles, or tests the page. In both cases, the discipline is the same, which is to name the purpose, keep the promise of that name, and avoid clever or cryptic shortcuts.

Finish by asking three plain questions about what an id does on a page. Does the name say what the part is for, rather than what it happens to look like? Is the name used consistently, with identifiers unique and classes reused only for genuinely similar parts? And would a stranger understand the name without asking you to explain it? If any answer is no, rename the part before publishing, and update every place that refers to it. That short review keeps pages maintainable, keeps links reliable, and turns naming from a chore into one of the most useful habits a markup author can build.
