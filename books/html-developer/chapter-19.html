Chapter 19: The Document Outline and Heading Order

Section 1: What a Document Outline Means

Readers rarely read a long page from the first word to the last, and this section looks closely at what a document outline means. Headings form the outline of a document: they are the skeleton a reader can scan to decide where to start, where to skip, and where to return later. A clear outline turns a wall of text into a map, while a muddled outline leaves even good writing feeling hard to follow.

Headings are ranked, from the first level down to the sixth, and that ranking is about structure, not appearance. The top heading names the whole page, the next level names its main parts, and each deeper level names a smaller part inside the one above it. When that order is respected, a reader or a screen reader can move through the page level by level and always know where they are. When the order jumps around, that sense of place breaks, and a section can appear to belong to a part of the page it has nothing to do with. Size and boldness may follow the ranking in a design, but they are not the ranking itself.

When you write the markup for what a document outline means, start by listing the headings alone, in order, before you write the paragraphs beneath them. Read that list as if it were a table of contents: does it tell the story of the page, does each heading say what its section is about, and does the order feel natural? If a heading is vague, if two headings say almost the same thing, or if a level is skipped, fix the outline before you polish the prose. A page whose headings read well on their own is usually a page whose full text reads well too, because structure and clarity grow together.

Access is where what a document outline means matters most. Many screen reader users navigate a page primarily by its headings, jumping from one heading to the next to find the part they need. A logical outline lets them do that quickly and confidently, while a broken outline forces them to read through everything in order, which is slow and frustrating. Keyboard users and skimming readers benefit in the same way, even if they navigate differently. Choosing a heading for its meaning, rather than for its size, is therefore not a technical nicety; it is a direct act of respect for the reader's time and attention.

Finish by asking three plain questions about what a document outline means. If you read only the headings, would you understand what the page covers and in what order? Does every heading describe its own section honestly, without borrowing importance from a larger size than it deserves? And does the outline still work if the styling is removed entirely? If any answer is no, revise the headings before publishing. That short review keeps outlines honest, keeps long pages navigable, and makes your writing easier to read, search, and maintain for years.
