Chapter 19: Accounts, Permissions and Least Privilege

Section 1: What an Account Really Is

An account is an identity that a system recognises, with a set of permissions attached to it, and understanding that definition is the foundation for everything in this chapter. When you sign into a computer, a website, or an app, you are presenting an account, and the system checks who you are, through your password and possibly a second factor, and then decides what you may do, based on the permissions tied to that account. An account is therefore not just a username and a password; it is a bundle of identity plus permission, and changing either part changes what the account can do. Accounts exist at many levels: a user account on your computer, an account with a website, an account for an app, and an administrator account with broad powers, and each one follows the same pattern of identity plus permission. The power of accounts is that they let a system distinguish between people and give each person only what they need, which is the basis for privacy, for accountability, and for safety. The danger of accounts is that they concentrate power: anyone who controls an account controls everything that account can do, which is why stolen accounts are so valuable to attackers. That is also why account hygiene, strong unique passwords, second factors, and regular reviews, matters so much, because each account is a doorway, and its strength determines who can enter. In this chapter you will learn how accounts work, why standard accounts are safer than administrator accounts for daily use, what least privilege means in practice, and how to review and tidy the permissions in your life. Accounts are the units of trust in digital systems, and managing them well is one of the most practical security skills you can build, for your home, your family, and any team you are part of. Accounts also have a lifecycle that mirrors a person's relationship with a service, and recognising that lifecycle helps you manage them through change. An account is created when you join, it is used while you participate, its permissions change as your role changes, and it should be closed when you leave, with its data handled according to the service's policy and your wishes. That lifecycle is easy to neglect at the closing stage, because leaving a service feels like an ending that needs no action, but an account left open after departure is an account that continues to exist without an owner watching it. Treating closure as part of the lifecycle, rather than as an optional extra, ensures that every account you create has a planned ending as well as a beginning. When you create a new account, note, even briefly, what it is for and when you expect to stop needing it, because that note will guide its eventual closure. Accounts with lifecycles, managed from creation to closure, are accounts that never become forgotten doors, and that completeness is the quiet discipline behind all account safety. Section 2: Why One Account Per Person Matters

One account per person is a simple rule with powerful consequences, because it makes actions traceable, permissions personal, and problems fixable. When each person has their own account, the system can tell who did what, which matters when something goes wrong, when a file is changed, or when a setting is altered, because the record points to a person, not to a shared mystery. Personal accounts also let each person have permissions fitted to their needs: a child can have a restricted account, a colleague can have access to their own projects, and you can have access to yours, without anyone gaining access to everyone else's work. Shared accounts destroy those benefits: if everyone signs in as the same user, no one can be held accountable, no one can have personal settings, and a password change affects everyone at once, which usually means the password is never changed. Shared accounts also make leaving harder, because when a person leaves a household or a team, a shared password must be changed everywhere, and that change is often forgotten, leaving the departed person with access they should no longer have. For families, personal accounts have an additional benefit, which is that each person's files, browsing, and settings stay separate, preserving privacy within the household as well as security. Setting up personal accounts takes a few minutes per person, and it is one of the easiest improvements a household or a small team can make. If you currently share an account with someone, consider creating separate accounts today, moving each person's files to their own space, and keeping a shared folder only for things you genuinely want to share. One account per person is not bureaucracy; it is the foundation that makes every other account rule in this chapter possible, because permissions can only be personal if accounts are personal first. Personal accounts also protect relationships, because they prevent the small frictions that shared accounts create in daily life. When two people share an account, one person's changes, such as a changed password, a new setting, or a deleted file, affect the other without warning, and those surprises breed frustration that has nothing to do with security. Personal accounts remove that friction, because each person's changes stay in their own space, and sharing happens deliberately, through shared folders or family features, rather than accidentally through a shared login. That deliberate sharing is also more respectful, because it says that each person's space matters, even within a close relationship, and that respect strengthens trust rather than weakening it. A household that uses personal accounts is a household that has chosen clarity over convenience, and clarity, in accounts as in relationships, prevents misunderstandings. The few minutes spent creating personal accounts repay themselves many times over in fewer arguments, fewer accidents, and clearer boundaries. Section 3: Strong Passwords and Accounts Together

Strong passwords are the first guard for every account, and while you have met passwords in earlier chapters, their relationship to accounts deserves a focused reminder here, because an account is only as strong as the secret that protects it. A long, unique password for each account ensures that a breach at one service does not cascade to others, and a password manager makes long, unique passwords practical, because you no longer need to memorise them all. Two-factor authentication adds a second guard, requiring something you have, such as your phone or a hardware key, in addition to something you know, so that a stolen password alone is not enough to enter the account. Recovery options are the third part of account protection: a recovery email, a recovery phone number, or backup codes should be current, secure, and separate from the account they protect, because a recovery option controlled by an attacker becomes a back door. It is also worth reviewing which accounts matter most, because not all accounts deserve equal effort: your email account deserves the strongest protection, because it can reset many other accounts, followed by banking, cloud storage, and social media, while a throwaway account for a single article deserves less. That prioritisation lets you focus your strongest habits where they matter most, without exhausting yourself on every minor account. If you discover that you have reused a password, change it on the most important accounts first, starting with email, and work through the rest over days, because a prioritised fix beats a perfect plan that never starts. Accounts and passwords are partners, and the partnership works when each account has its own strong secret, a second factor where offered, and a recovery path you control. That trio, unique password, second factor, and safe recovery, is the baseline for every account worth protecting. Account prioritisation also helps when you are overwhelmed by the sheer number of accounts you own, because it gives you permission to fix the important ones first and to accept that minor accounts can wait. Start with email, then banking and cloud storage, then social media and work accounts, and only then move to shopping, forums, and single-use services, because the damage from a compromised email account cascades, while the damage from a compromised forum account usually does not. That ordering also helps you allocate second factors wisely, because enabling a second factor everywhere at once is daunting, while enabling it for five important accounts this week is achievable. Progress, measured in important accounts protected, is more motivating than perfection, measured in every account protected, and motivation is what sustains a habit. Make a list of your ten most important accounts, protect those first, and let the rest follow in their turn, because a prioritised plan that you actually follow beats a perfect plan that you never start. Section 4: Administrator Accounts Versus Standard Accounts

Computers offer different kinds of accounts, and the most important distinction for daily safety is between administrator accounts and standard accounts. An administrator account can install software, change system settings, alter other users' accounts, and modify protected files, which is powerful and necessary for maintenance, but dangerous for everyday use, because any program you run, including malware, inherits the account's powers. A standard account can use installed programs, browse the web, create and edit its own files, and change its own settings, but it cannot install system-wide software or change other users' settings without an administrator's approval, which limits what malware can do if it runs under that account. That limitation is the whole point: a standard account contains damage, while an administrator account amplifies it. Most operating systems create the first user as an administrator for convenience, and many people continue using that account daily without realising the risk, but the safer pattern is simple: create a standard account for daily use, keep an administrator account for maintenance, and switch to the administrator only when you deliberately need to install or change something system-wide. That switch takes only a moment, usually a password prompt, and it creates a natural pause in which you can ask whether the change is genuinely needed. For families, the pattern scales: adults can hold administrator accounts, children can hold standard or restricted accounts, and guests can hold guest accounts, each fitted to their needs. The distinction is not about trust in the person; it is about limiting the blast radius of a mistake or an attack, so that a single wrong click does not compromise the whole machine. Administrator for maintenance, standard for daily life, is a habit that quietly protects you every day. The administrator-standard distinction also applies to your phone, though it is less visible there, because phones are designed around app permissions rather than user accounts. On a phone, the equivalent of administrator powers is the set of broad permissions, such as accessibility access, device administrator status, or full file access, that a few apps may request, and granting those broadly is like running as administrator daily. Most apps do not need those broad powers, and a phone that grants them casually is a phone whose apps can do far more than their jobs require. The habit transfers directly: grant broad phone permissions only to apps you deeply trust, for a clear reason, and revoke them when the reason ends, just as you would elevate to administrator only for maintenance. That transfer shows that least privilege is not a computer-only idea, but a principle that follows you across devices, adapting its form while keeping its question the same: what is the minimum necessary for this job? Section 5: Least Privilege Explained in Plain Words

Least privilege is the principle that every person, program, and device should have only the permissions it needs to do its job, and no more, and it is one of the most powerful ideas in all of security. The name describes it exactly: the least privilege necessary, neither more nor less, for the task at hand. A word processor needs to read and write your documents, but it does not need to change system settings; a game needs to save its progress, but it does not need access to your contacts; a colleague needs to read a shared project, but does not need to delete it or to see your private files. Giving each one only what it needs limits the damage if it is misused, compromised, or simply mistaken, because its reach is bounded by its permissions. Least privilege also makes systems easier to understand, because permissions reflect jobs, and a permission that does not fit a job stands out as a question. The principle applies at every level: user accounts, app permissions, file sharing, and team roles all benefit from asking, what is the minimum this needs, and granting only that. It is not about suspicion or stinginess; it is about precision, giving each part exactly what it needs to succeed, and nothing extra that could be misused. Least privilege also changes over time, because jobs change, projects end, and people move roles, so permissions granted for a past job should be reviewed and removed when that job ends. That ongoing review is part of the principle, not an afterthought, because privilege that lingers after its purpose ends is privilege waiting to be misused. In the rest of this chapter you will see least privilege applied to accounts, files, apps, teams, and devices, and you will learn to ask its central question, what is the minimum necessary, in every permission decision you make. Least privilege also has a psychological benefit that is rarely mentioned, which is that it reduces the fear of making mistakes. A person working under a standard account, with narrow permissions, can experiment, try new things, and learn, knowing that a mistake will be contained to their own space, while a person working as administrator may fear that a single wrong click could damage the whole system. That freedom to experiment safely is valuable for learning, especially for children and for beginners, because learning requires mistakes, and mistakes require containment. Least privilege therefore supports growth, not only safety, by creating a space where errors are cheap and recovery is easy. When you set up a standard account for yourself or for a learner, frame it that way, as a safe space to explore, rather than as a restriction, because that framing encourages the very experimentation that builds skill. Section 6: Why Using Administrator Powers All Day Is Risky

Using an administrator account all day is risky for a reason that is easy to overlook, which is that your account's powers become every program's powers, including programs you did not intend to empower. When you run a web browser, a document viewer, or a downloaded tool under an administrator account, that program can, in principle, do anything the administrator can do, including installing software, changing settings, and accessing other users' files, if it is malicious or compromised. Under a standard account, the same program is confined to your own files and settings, and it must ask for administrator approval before doing anything system-wide, which gives you a chance to stop it. That difference is the difference between a single compromised program and a fully compromised machine, and it is why security guidance consistently recommends standard accounts for daily use. The risk is not only from malware; it is also from mistakes, because an administrator can accidentally delete a system file, change a critical setting, or alter another user's data with a single wrong click, while a standard account's mistakes are confined to its own space. The convenience of always being administrator is real, because it avoids password prompts, but that convenience is purchased with exposure, and the prompts you avoid are precisely the pauses that protect you. Think of administrator powers like a master key to a building: you would not carry it on every errand and use it to open every door, because losing it, or having it copied, would compromise everything, and you would instead carry an ordinary key for daily doors and fetch the master key only when you need it. Your computer deserves the same discipline: daily key for daily work, master key for maintenance, and a pause between them that lets you decide deliberately. That discipline is least privilege in its most personal form. Administrator risk also includes the risk of a compromised update or a trusted program turning malicious, because even programs you trust can be hijacked, and an administrator account gives a hijacked program the keys to everything. A browser extension that turns malicious, a document viewer with a newly discovered flaw, or a trusted tool whose update is tampered with can all become attackers' vehicles, and the damage they can do depends entirely on the account under which they run. Under a standard account, their damage is bounded; under an administrator account, it is not, and that bounding is the difference between a contained incident and a system-wide compromise. That is why security professionals treat account type as a first-line defence, not as a minor setting, because it determines the blast radius of every other failure. You cannot prevent every program from ever being compromised, but you can decide, in advance, how far a compromised program can reach, and that decision is made by choosing a standard account for daily work. Section 7: Setting Up a Standard Account for Daily Use

Setting up a standard account for daily use is a practical task that takes only a few minutes and pays off every day afterwards, and it is worth doing deliberately rather than postponing. Start by creating a new standard user account on your computer, with its own username and a strong password, and sign into it to confirm that your everyday programs work: your browser, your documents, your mail, and your media should all work normally under a standard account. Next, move your daily files to the new account's folders, or, if your system makes moving awkward, use the new account going forward and keep the old account's files accessible only when needed, rather than trying to move everything at once. Keep your administrator account, with a strong, different password, for maintenance tasks: installing software, updating system settings, and managing other accounts, and sign into it only for those tasks, signing back out afterwards. If you are the only user of the computer, you may be tempted to skip this, because switching accounts feels like extra work, but the switch is usually a single sign-out and sign-in, or a password prompt for a specific task, and it quickly becomes routine. For families, set up standard accounts for each person, as you learned earlier, and keep administrator access with the adults, so each person's daily work is contained and each person's files are separate. Test the setup by trying a task that needs administrator approval, such as installing a program, and confirm that the system asks for the administrator password, because that prompt is the protection working. A standard daily account is not a restriction on your freedom; it is a container for your daily life, and containers are what keep a spill from becoming a flood. Set it up today, use it tomorrow, and the habit will soon feel as natural as locking your front door. Setting up a standard account also gives you a natural moment to tidy your files, because moving to a new account prompts you to decide what belongs in daily use and what can stay archived. That tidying is a bonus, not a burden, because a daily account cluttered with years of old files is harder to back up, harder to search, and harder to protect than a tidy one. Use the move as an opportunity to archive old projects, delete files you no longer need, and organise what remains into clear folders, so your new daily space starts clean. That clean start also makes future maintenance easier, because a tidy account is easier to review, easier to back up, and easier to hand over if needed. A standard account is therefore not only a security improvement but a fresh start, and framing the move that way, as a tidy-up rather than a chore, makes it more appealing. Take the opportunity, tidy as you move, and your new account will serve you better in every way, not only in safety. Section 8: Elevation: Using Administrator Powers Only When Needed

Elevation is the process of temporarily gaining administrator powers for a specific task, and understanding it helps you use those powers safely rather than living with them constantly. When a standard user tries to install software or change a system setting, the system asks for an administrator's approval, usually by prompting for the administrator password or by asking an administrator to confirm, and that prompt is elevation in action. Elevation is valuable because it is temporary and specific: you gain the powers for that task, and then you return to standard powers, so a program you run afterwards does not inherit administrator rights. The prompt is also a decision point, and it deserves the same pause you give to firewall prompts: ask whether you expected this prompt, whether the program requesting it is one you trust, and whether the task genuinely needs administrator powers. A prompt that appears unexpectedly, for example when opening a document or visiting a website, is a warning sign, because ordinary documents and websites do not need administrator powers, and you should decline and investigate. A prompt that appears when you deliberately install trusted software is expected, and approving it is reasonable, provided you obtained the software from a trusted source. Be careful with prompts that appear in quick succession, or that reappear after you declined, because persistence can be a tactic to wear down your caution. Elevation is least privilege in motion: powers granted for a moment, for a purpose, and then withdrawn, rather than powers held constantly. Practising elevation well means approving deliberately, declining suspiciously, and returning to standard powers promptly, so that your computer spends most of its life in its safest state. That rhythm, standard by default and elevated by decision, is the healthy heartbeat of account safety. Elevation prompts also deserve a habit of reading, because prompts often contain details, such as the program's name and publisher, that help you judge them, and those details are easy to miss when you click quickly. A prompt for a program you recognise, from a publisher you trust, for a task you initiated, is a different proposition from a prompt for an unfamiliar program, from an unknown publisher, for a task you did not initiate, and reading the prompt is how you tell the difference. Make it a rule to read the program name aloud, silently or literally, before approving, because that tiny pause forces attention onto the detail that matters most. That reading habit also protects you against prompts that mimic legitimate ones, because a mimicked prompt often gets a detail slightly wrong, such as a misspelled publisher name, and a reader will catch what a clicker will miss. Elevation prompts are questions, and questions deserve to be read before they are answered, especially when the answer grants powerful access. Section 9: Permission Prompts and How to Answer Them

Permission prompts appear whenever a program or a system asks for more access than it currently has, and answering them well is a skill that protects you daily. A prompt usually names what is being requested, such as administrator approval, access to your files, or access to your camera, and it offers you a choice to allow or deny, sometimes with an option to allow only once or only while using. The worst habit is approving automatically to make the prompt disappear, because that habit grants powers you have not considered, and it trains you to ignore the very moments when your judgement matters most. The best habit is a short pause with three questions: do I recognise the program making the request, does its job need this permission, and did I expect this request at this moment? If all three answers are yes, approving is reasonable, and approving only once, or only while using, is safer than approving permanently where that option exists. If any answer is no, deny and investigate, because a denied permission rarely causes permanent harm, and you can approve later once you understand it. Be especially careful with prompts that appear after installing software from an unfamiliar source, or that request broad permissions, such as full disk access, for a simple job, because broad requests from simple programs deserve scepticism. Also watch for prompt fatigue, where many prompts in a row tempt you to approve blindly, and if you face a flood of prompts, stop and investigate what is causing them, rather than approving your way through. Prompts are the system's way of asking your permission, and your permission is meaningful only if you give it thoughtfully. Answer prompts as you would answer a stranger asking for your house key: with recognition, with a reason, and with a limit, and your permissions will remain yours. Permission prompts also appear in a form that is easy to overlook, which is the prompt that asks for permission once, versus the prompt that asks for permission permanently, and choosing the temporary option is almost always wiser. A program that needs your location for a single task does not need it forever, and a program that needs your files for one import does not need them always, so granting once, or only while using, gives the program what it needs without a lasting exposure. Temporary grants also force the program to ask again next time, which gives you another chance to judge whether the need is still genuine, and that repeated judgement is a form of ongoing review. Permanent grants, by contrast, are decisions made once and forgotten, and forgotten decisions are how privilege creep happens on personal devices. Whenever a prompt offers a temporary option, choose it by default, and reserve permanent grants for the few programs whose job genuinely requires continuous access, such as a backup tool or a security app. Temporary by default is least privilege in its most convenient form, because it requires no review schedule; the review happens automatically, each time the program asks again. Section 10: Permissions on Files and Folders

Files and folders have permissions that control who can read, write, and execute them, and understanding those permissions helps you share safely and protect privately. On most systems, each file and folder has an owner, usually the person who created it, and permissions for the owner, for a group, and for everyone else, specifying who may read the contents, who may change them, and who may run them if they are programs. A private document should be readable only by you, a shared project folder should be readable and writable by its team, and a public file should be readable by everyone, and setting those permissions deliberately is how you enforce those intentions. The most common mistake is leaving sensitive files readable by everyone on a shared computer, because default permissions are often more open than people realise, and a quick check of a sensitive folder's permissions is a worthwhile habit. Another common mistake is sharing a whole folder when only one file needs sharing, because the folder's other files become exposed along with the intended one, and sharing the single file, or a copy of it, is safer. Permissions also interact with backups and cloud sync, because a file shared in a cloud folder may inherit that folder's sharing settings, and a private file accidentally placed in a shared folder becomes shared. To practise good file permissions, review the sharing settings of folders containing sensitive material, ensure private folders are private, share individual files rather than whole folders where possible, and remove sharing when its purpose ends. File permissions are the quiet fences around your documents, and tending them, like tending any fence, keeps your private work private and your shared work shared only with the people you chose. That tending is least privilege applied to your files, one folder at a time. File permissions also matter for backups, because a backup of a private folder should remain private, and a backup that inherits broader sharing can expose files that were private in their original location. When you back up to a cloud service or a shared drive, check whether the backup location's sharing settings match the original folder's privacy, because a mismatch can turn a private archive into a shared one without you noticing. That check is especially important for folders containing financial, health, or family documents, where a broader backup share could expose sensitive material to people who should not see it. A simple rule helps: a backup should be at least as private as the original, and preferably more so, because a backup is a complete copy that persists longer than the original may. Reviewing backup sharing alongside file sharing, during your twice-yearly checkup, ensures that your copies do not undo your originals' privacy. Files are only as private as their least private copy, and backups are copies that deserve the same care as originals.

Section 11: Sharing a Computer Safely

Sharing a computer safely is mostly a matter of separate accounts, clear boundaries, and a few shared spaces used deliberately, rather than a single trick. Start with separate accounts for each person, as you learned earlier, so each person's files, settings, and browsing stay in their own space, and no one accidentally, or deliberately, alters another's work. Next, create a shared folder for things you genuinely want to share, such as family photos or household documents, and keep everything else in personal folders, so sharing is a choice, not a default. Next, agree on who holds administrator access, usually the adults or the person responsible for maintenance, and ensure that others know to ask before installing software or changing system settings, because those changes affect everyone. Next, set up a guest account for visitors, so a friend who borrows the computer for a quick task does not gain access to anyone's personal account, and does not leave their own browsing history in yours. Next, ensure each account has its own strong password and, where offered, its own second factor, because a shared computer with weak individual passwords is only as strong as its weakest account. Also consider screen privacy: a person using a shared computer should lock the screen when stepping away, because an unlocked account on a shared machine is an open door to that person's files. Sharing a computer need not mean sharing everything, and the best shared computers feel personal to each user while offering a few well-chosen shared spaces. That balance, personal spaces plus deliberate sharing, is least privilege applied to a household, and it preserves both privacy and harmony, because each person controls their own space and shares only what they choose. A shared computer set up this way is a pleasure to use, not a source of friction. Sharing a computer also involves the question of what happens when someone forgets to sign out, because a signed-in account on a shared machine is an open door, and forgetting is common. The habit that covers forgetting is a short auto-lock time, so that a computer left unattended locks itself quickly, requiring a password to resume, and that setting protects every account on the machine, not only yours. Agreeing, as a household, that everyone locks their screen when stepping away, by pressing a key combination or closing the lid, turns that setting into a shared norm, and norms are more reliable than settings alone. That norm also teaches children a valuable habit for workplaces later, where locking a screen is a basic professional courtesy as well as a security practice. A shared computer with short auto-lock and a locking norm is a computer where forgetting to sign out is a minor lapse, not a major exposure, because the machine locks itself and the habit backs it up. Small settings plus shared norms are the combination that makes shared computing safe in real, forgetful life. Section 12: Guest Accounts for Visitors

A guest account is a temporary, limited account for someone who borrows your computer briefly, and it is one of the simplest protections for a shared machine. When a visitor signs into a guest account, they get a clean desktop, a browser, and basic programs, but they cannot see your files, your browsing history, or your saved passwords, and their activity is usually discarded when they sign out, leaving no trace on your machine. That separation protects you from a visitor accidentally altering your settings or viewing your private files, and it protects the visitor too, because their browsing and sign-ins do not linger on your computer for the next user to find. Setting up a guest account is usually a single switch in your system's user settings, and enabling it before you need it means you are ready when a visitor asks to borrow the computer, rather than scrambling to protect your account in the moment. If your system does not offer a guest account, a limited standard account with a simple password, used only for visitors and cleaned regularly, serves a similar purpose. Be clear with visitors about the guest account's limits, such as that files they save may be deleted on sign-out, so they save important work elsewhere, like a USB drive or a cloud account. Guest accounts also teach a broader lesson, which is that access should be fitted to the visitor's purpose: a visitor who needs a browser for ten minutes does not need your account, your files, or your administrator powers, and giving them only what they need is least privilege in action. A guest account is hospitality with boundaries, and boundaries make hospitality comfortable for everyone, because the host need not worry and the guest need not fear overstepping. Guest accounts also help in a scenario that is easy to forget, which is the repair shop, where a technician may need to use your computer briefly to test it. A guest account, or a limited account created for the repair, lets the technician test without accessing your personal account, your files, or your saved passwords, and that separation protects both you and the technician. Before handing a computer for repair, back up your files, note your recovery options, and consider whether the repair genuinely needs your personal account, or whether a guest or test account would serve, because most hardware tests do not need personal data at all. That preparation also protects you if the computer is kept for days, because your personal account remains locked while the machine is out of your hands. Guest accounts are therefore not only for visitors at home, but for any brief, third-party use of your machine, and thinking of them that way extends their value far beyond the living room. Section 13: Children's Accounts and Family Controls

Children's accounts combine safety with learning, because they give children a real, personal space on a computer while limiting what they can do, and they teach responsibility gradually rather than all at once. A child's account is typically a standard or restricted account, without administrator powers, so a child cannot install software system-wide, change other users' settings, or accidentally alter critical files, which contains both mistakes and malware. Family controls, offered by most systems, let a parent set additional limits, such as screen-time schedules, content filters, and approval for purchases or new apps, and those limits should be explained to the child, not merely imposed, because explanation builds understanding that outlasts the controls themselves. As children grow, their accounts should grow with them: permissions can be widened gradually, new responsibilities can be added, and conversations about why limits exist can deepen, so that a teenager's account reflects a teenager's judgement, not a young child's restrictions. It is also important to give children privacy within safety, because a child's account that an adult monitors constantly, without explanation, can feel like surveillance, while an account with clear, agreed boundaries feels like trust. The goal is a balance: enough restriction to prevent serious harm, enough freedom to learn, and enough conversation to build judgement. Parents should also model good account habits themselves, using standard accounts daily and strong passwords, because children learn more from what adults do than from what adults say. A child's account, set up thoughtfully and adjusted as the child grows, is a training ground for digital life, where mistakes are contained, lessons are learned, and independence is earned gradually. That gradual independence is the real purpose of family controls, not permanent restriction. Children's accounts also need a conversation about privacy within the family, because a child's account that is monitored without explanation can feel like surveillance, while one with clear, agreed boundaries feels like trust. Agree with your child, in age-appropriate terms, what you will check and why, such as checking for purchases or for contact with strangers, and what you will not check, such as private conversations with trusted friends, unless there is a safety concern. That agreement respects the child's growing autonomy while preserving the parent's responsibility, and it models the same balance the child will need in workplaces later, where monitoring exists but is bounded by policy. As the child grows, revisit the agreement, widening privacy as judgement grows, so that the boundaries evolve with the child rather than remaining frozen at a young age. A family that talks about account boundaries openly raises children who understand privacy as a shared value, not as a parental restriction, and that understanding will guide them long after parental controls are gone. Conversation is the most important family control of all. Check: Why should a child's account permissions widen gradually as the child grows?

Section 14: Work Accounts Versus Personal Accounts

Work accounts and personal accounts should be kept separate, because they belong to different worlds with different rules, different owners, and different risks, and mixing them creates problems for both. A work account is provided by your employer, for work purposes, and it may be monitored, managed, and revoked by the employer, while a personal account is yours, for your private life, and it is governed by your choices alone. Using a work account for personal matters, such as private messages, personal photos, or personal browsing, puts those matters under the employer's visibility and control, which most people would not choose if they thought about it. Using a personal account for work matters puts work data outside the employer's management, which can breach policy, complicate collaboration, and create a copy of work data that the employer cannot protect or revoke. The separation also helps when you change jobs: work accounts are closed, and work data stays with the employer, while personal accounts continue uninterrupted, with no awkward extraction of personal data from a work system. Practically, separation means using work email for work, personal email for personal matters, work cloud storage for work files, and personal storage for personal files, and being careful about the boundary cases, such as a personal phone that accesses work email, where work policies may apply to the device. If your employer provides a device, use it for work and keep personal activity on your own device, and if you must use one device for both, use separate accounts and profiles within it, so the worlds remain distinct even on shared hardware. Separation is not about hiding work from your employer or hiding personal life from yourself; it is about keeping each world under its proper rules, so each can be managed, protected, and left behind cleanly when its time ends. Work-personal separation also applies to messaging and social media, where a work contact added to a personal account, or a personal friend added to a work account, can blur boundaries in ways that are hard to undo. A work colleague who can see your personal posts, or a personal friend who can see your work messages, gains a view into a world they were not meant to enter, and that view can cause awkwardness, misunderstandings, or policy issues. Keeping work and personal contacts in their respective accounts, and being careful about friend requests that cross the boundary, preserves both worlds' integrity. That separation also helps when you leave a job, because work contacts remain in the work account that closes, while personal contacts remain in your personal account that continues, with no painful extraction needed. Boundaries between work and personal life are easier to maintain when they are built into accounts, rather than relying on memory to keep conversations separate. Separate accounts are separate worlds, and separate worlds are easier to manage, to leave, and to return to. Section 15: Old Accounts You Forgot You Had

Old accounts you forgot you had are a quiet security risk, because they persist long after you stopped using them, holding personal data, old passwords, and access that you no longer monitor. Most people accumulate dozens of accounts over years: shopping sites, forums, apps tried once, newsletters, and services tied to old jobs or old devices, and each forgotten account is a doorway you no longer guard. If one of those services is breached, an old password, especially a reused one, can be tried against your current accounts, and if the old account still holds personal data, that data can be exposed or misused. Forgotten accounts also complicate recovery, because their recovery email or phone number may be outdated, making them hard to reclaim if you ever need to, and hard to close if you decide to. The remedy is a periodic account audit: search your email for sign-up messages, review your password manager's list, check your browser's saved logins, and list accounts you no longer use. For each one, decide whether to keep it, in which case update its password and second factor, or close it, in which case follow the service's deletion process and confirm the closure. That audit takes time, but it can be spread over days, tackling a few accounts at a time, starting with the most sensitive. It is also a good moment to change any reused passwords you discover, because an old account with a reused password is a bridge to your current accounts. Old accounts are the digital equivalent of old keys: keys to houses you no longer live in, still in circulation, still usable, and still worth collecting and discarding. An account audit, done once a year, keeps your collection of keys manageable and your exposure limited to the doors you actually use. Old accounts also include accounts tied to old devices, such as an old phone's cloud account, an old tablet's app store account, or an old computer's backup account, and those device-tied accounts are easy to forget because the device itself is gone. When you retire a device, its accounts should be reviewed as part of retirement: sign out of them on the device, close the ones you no longer need, and update recovery options for the ones you keep, because a retired device's accounts can linger in the cloud long after the hardware is recycled. That retirement review is a natural companion to the device disposal habits you learned earlier, and pairing them ensures that neither the hardware nor its accounts are left exposed. A device retired without an account review is a device whose cloud shadow persists, holding data and access that you no longer monitor. Make account review part of device retirement, and your digital footprint will shrink as your physical devices do, rather than growing silently in the cloud while your hardware gathers dust. Section 16: Dormant Accounts and Why They Are Dangerous

A dormant account is an account that exists but is not actively used, and it is dangerous precisely because its inactivity makes it invisible to its owner while remaining visible to attackers. Dormant accounts often have old, weak passwords, outdated recovery options, and no second factor, because they were created before current habits, and they are rarely checked, so a compromise can go unnoticed for months or years. Attackers value dormant accounts because they can be taken over quietly, used for spam, fraud, or as a stepping stone to other accounts, without the owner noticing, since the owner is not watching. Dormant accounts also accumulate risk through policy changes: a service may change its terms, suffer a breach, or be acquired, and the dormant account's data is affected by those events without the owner knowing. The defence is the same as for old accounts, but with an emphasis on decision: for each dormant account, either revive it properly, updating its password, second factor, and recovery options, and adding it to your regular use or monitoring, or close it properly, following the service's deletion process. There is no safe middle ground of leaving it dormant and hoping, because hope is not a security control. For workplaces, dormant accounts are an even greater risk, because a former employee's account, left active after departure, is a known doorway for attackers, and leaver processes must close such accounts promptly. For individuals, a dormant account audit, done alongside your old-account audit, closes the invisible doors in your digital life. Dormancy is not safety; it is neglect, and neglect is what attackers count on, because an account no one watches is an account no one defends. Close it or revive it, but do not leave it dormant. Dormant accounts also pose a risk through password reuse that is easy to underestimate, because a dormant account's password may be one you reused years ago, before you adopted a password manager, and that old password may still match a current account's password. Attackers who breach a dormant service gain a list of old passwords, and they try those passwords against current services automatically, hoping for a match, in a process called credential stuffing. That process succeeds precisely because people reuse passwords, and because dormant accounts preserve old passwords long after the user has forgotten them. The defence is twofold: close dormant accounts, so their passwords cease to be useful, and ensure current accounts use unique passwords, so an old password cannot match a current one. That twofold defence breaks the chain between past and present, and it is why an account audit that closes old accounts and updates current passwords is so powerful. Past passwords should not endanger present accounts, and closing the past is how you ensure they do not. Check: Why is a dormant account more attractive to an attacker than an actively used one?

Section 17: Service Accounts in Plain Words

Service accounts are accounts used not by people, but by programs and services, to do their jobs automatically, and understanding them helps you recognise a kind of account that is easy to overlook. When a backup program runs at night, a website fetches data, or an app syncs in the background, it often signs in as a service account, which has permissions fitted to that specific job, such as reading one folder or writing to one database. Service accounts are necessary, because automated jobs need identities too, but they deserve the same least-privilege care as human accounts: a backup service needs to read your files, but it does not need to delete them or to access your email, and a service account granted broad powers becomes a powerful target if compromised. For most individuals, service accounts are managed by the systems and apps they use, and your job is mainly to notice them when they appear in permission lists, and to question any service account with surprisingly broad access. For small businesses and developers, service accounts deserve deliberate management: each service gets its own account, with only its needed permissions, a strong, unique secret stored safely, and a review when the service changes or ends. Sharing one service account between several services is as bad as sharing a human account between several people, because a compromise of one service then affects the others. Service accounts also need rotation, meaning their secrets should be changed periodically and whenever someone who knew them leaves, because a secret that never changes is a secret that accumulates exposure. You may never create a service account yourself, but recognising them in permission lists, and applying least privilege to them, completes your picture of accounts: people, programs, and services all have identities, all have permissions, and all deserve only what they need. Service accounts also appear in your personal life in a form you may not recognise, which is the connected app or integration that acts on your behalf in the background. A calendar sync, a fitness tracker, a smart home automation, or a social media cross-posting tool all act as service accounts, with tokens that let them read or write your data automatically, and those tokens deserve the same narrow scope as any service account. When you connect such a tool, check what it can read and write, and prefer tools that request the minimum, because a tool that can read everything to do a narrow job is a tool with excessive privilege. Review those connections twice a year, as you would review any service account, and revoke the ones you no longer use, because a forgotten automation is a forgotten service account. Recognising background tools as service accounts changes how you judge them, from convenient helpers to privileged identities, and that judgement leads to narrower grants and more frequent reviews. Helpers deserve permissions fitted to their help, and no more. Check: What is a service account, and why should each service have its own?

Section 18: App Permissions at the System Level

App permissions at the system level are the gates through which apps access your device's sensitive resources, and reviewing them is a direct application of least privilege to your daily tools. When an app asks for access to your location, your contacts, your photos, your camera, your microphone, or your notifications, it is asking for a permission that should be judged against its job, as you learned in the privacy chapter. Modern systems let you grant permissions in limited ways, such as only while using the app, or only once, and those limited grants are safer than permanent ones, because they expire and must be requested again. The habit to build is a periodic permission review: open your system's settings, list apps with sensitive permissions, and ask, for each one, whether its job explains the permission, and whether a narrower grant would serve. Revoke permissions that surprise you, and consider whether an app that demands broad permissions for a simple job deserves to stay installed at all. Pay special attention to permissions that are rarely needed but broadly granted, such as background location, full disk access, or accessibility access, because those are powerful permissions that deserve strong justification. Also review permissions after app updates, because updates sometimes request new permissions, and a new request is a moment to rejudge the app. For children, permission review deserves adult involvement, because children may grant permissions without understanding them. System-level app permissions are where least privilege becomes tangible in everyday life, because each permission is a concrete decision about what an app may do, and those decisions, made thoughtfully and reviewed regularly, keep your device's capabilities under your control rather than under the control of every app you ever installed. System app permissions also include a category that deserves special attention, which is accessibility and device-administrator permissions, because those are among the most powerful permissions a phone or computer can grant. An app with accessibility access can read screen contents, simulate taps, and observe much of what you do, while a device administrator can enforce policies, wipe data, or lock the device, and both should be granted only to apps you deeply trust, for a clear reason, such as a screen reader for accessibility or a security tool from a reputable maker. A game, a shopping app, or a simple utility should never need those powers, and a request for them from such an app is a strong warning sign. Reviewing accessibility and administrator grants specifically, during your permission review, catches the most dangerous grants, because those are the ones that can bypass other protections. Powerful permissions deserve powerful justification, and when the justification is missing, revocation is the safe answer, even if the app complains. Your device's most powerful gates deserve its most careful guards. Check: How should you judge whether an app's system permission is reasonable?

Section 19: Permissions on Phones in Practice

Phone permissions in practice are where most people meet least privilege daily, because phones ask for permissions constantly, and each request is a small decision that accumulates into your phone's overall exposure. The most sensitive phone permissions are location, contacts, photos, camera, microphone, and notifications, because each gives an app access to a rich part of your life, and each should be granted narrowly. Location should usually be granted only while using, and only to apps that genuinely need it, such as maps, while background location should be rare and justified. Contacts should be granted only to messaging, calling, and similar apps, not to games or shopping apps that have no need to know who you know. Photos should be granted selectively where your phone offers it, such as allowing access to chosen photos rather than your entire library, because a whole-library grant exposes far more than a single-photo grant. Camera and microphone should be granted only while using, and you should notice your phone's indicators that show when they are active, because an indicator appearing unexpectedly is information worth investigating. Notifications deserve attention too, because an app with notification access can read message previews, and that access should be limited to apps you trust. The practical routine is a quarterly permission review, done in your phone's settings, where you scroll through apps, revoke what surprises you, and uninstall apps you no longer use, because an unused app with permissions is a forgotten risk. Phone permissions are not a one-time setup, but a living list, and tending that list is one of the most effective, most accessible forms of least privilege available to any user. Your phone's gates are yours to tend, and tending them regularly keeps your most personal device personal. Phone permissions also include a setting that many people overlook, which is the permission to draw over other apps or to appear on top, because an app that can overlay other apps can mimic login screens and trick you into entering credentials. That overlay permission, like accessibility access, is powerful and should be granted rarely, only to apps with a clear need, such as a screen filter or a trusted floating tool. A request for overlay permission from an unfamiliar app, especially one that also requests other sensitive permissions, deserves scepticism and investigation, because overlay plus other permissions is a combination used in phishing attacks. Reviewing overlay grants during your permission review, and revoking any that surprise you, closes a subtle but real attack path. Phone permissions are a system of gates, and the most powerful gates are often the least obvious, so a review that covers only location and contacts misses the gates that matter most. Review all gates, especially the powerful, obscure ones, and your phone's permissions will be genuinely narrow. Section 20: Signing In With Another Account

Signing in with another account, through buttons like "Sign in with Google" or "Sign in with Apple," is convenient, but it creates a relationship between accounts that deserves understanding before you click. When you use such a button, you are allowing a new service to verify your identity through an existing account, rather than creating a new password, and that service may also request access to some of your data, such as your name, email, or contacts, depending on what you approve. The convenience is real: fewer passwords to remember, faster sign-up, and a single place to manage access, but the concentration is also real: if the central account is compromised, many connected services become accessible, and if you lose the central account, you may lose access to the connected services too. The permissions granted through such sign-ins should be reviewed, because a service that needed only your email for sign-in should not also gain access to your contacts or your cloud files, and you can often limit the grant during sign-up. You should also review the list of services connected to your central account periodically, in that account's security settings, revoking access for services you no longer use, because a forgotten connection is a forgotten doorway. For the most sensitive services, such as banking or health, creating a separate account with its own strong password and second factor may be wiser than linking through a central account, because separation limits cascade risk. Sign-in buttons are a trade-off between convenience and concentration, and the right balance depends on the service's sensitivity: convenient linking for low-risk services, separate accounts for high-risk ones, and regular review of all connections. That balance, made deliberately, lets you enjoy convenience without surrendering control. Central sign-in also creates a single point of failure that deserves a specific mitigation, which is to protect the central account with the strongest available measures: a unique, long password, a hardware key or authenticator app for second factor, and recovery options that you control and review. That strong central protection compensates, in part, for the concentration risk, because a central account that is hard to compromise makes its connected services harder to compromise through it. It does not eliminate the risk, because a central account can still be lost, or its provider can still suffer a breach, but it reduces the risk to a level where the convenience becomes justifiable for low-risk services. For high-risk services, separation remains wiser, because no amount of central protection removes the cascade risk entirely. The balance is therefore: strengthen the centre, link only low-risk services, and separate high-risk ones, and that three-part balance gives you convenience where it is safe and separation where it matters. A strong centre plus selective linking is a practical compromise, honestly made. Check: What are the convenience benefits and the concentration risks of signing in with another account?

Section 21: What Third-Party Access Really Grants

Third-party access is the permission you grant when you let one service access another service on your behalf, and it is worth understanding precisely, because its scope is often broader than people realise. When you connect a calendar app to your email, a fitness app to your health data, or a game to your social account, you are granting a token, a kind of limited key, that lets the third party act as you within the scope you approved, without ever seeing your password. That token's scope matters: it may allow reading only, or reading and writing, it may cover all your data or only part of it, and it may last until you revoke it, which could be years. The risk is that a third party with broad, long-lived access becomes a powerful extension of your account, and if that third party is breached, misbehaves, or changes ownership, your data is exposed through a door you opened yourself. The defence is to grant narrowly, review regularly, and revoke promptly: when connecting a service, read the requested scope and decline extras where possible, preferring services that request the minimum; when reviewing, check the connected-apps list in each major account and remove anything you no longer use or recognise; and when a service's purpose ends, revoke its access immediately, rather than leaving it connected indefinitely. It is also worth distinguishing between services you actively use and services you tried once, because tried-once services are the most common source of forgotten access. Third-party access is a useful feature that enables the connected apps modern life relies on, but it is also a set of keys you hand out, and keys handed out should be counted, limited, and collected when no longer needed. Treat every connection as a key, and your connected-apps list as a key ring to be tidied regularly. Third-party tokens also have a lifetime that you can sometimes control, because some services let you grant access for a limited period, or let you choose a narrower scope, such as read-only instead of read-and-write. Choosing the limited option, even when the full option is offered first, is a small act of least privilege that reduces exposure without losing functionality, because most third-party tools need only to read, not to write, or only to access one folder, not your entire account. When a service offers a choice, read it carefully, because the limited option is often presented as a secondary button, while the broad option is prominent, and providers design that prominence deliberately. Choosing the limited option is a vote for narrow access, and it also teaches the provider that users value narrowness, which may influence future designs. Token scope is a negotiation, even if it feels like a take-it-or-leave-it prompt, because you can often leave it, choose a different tool, or contact the maker to ask for a narrower option. Negotiate scope where you can, and your tokens will be narrower for it. Check: What is a third-party access token, and why does its scope and lifetime matter?

Section 22: Revoking Access You No Longer Need

Revoking access is the act of taking back a permission you previously granted, and it is one of the simplest, most powerful habits in account safety, because it closes doors that no longer need to be open. Revocation applies to many things: third-party apps connected to your accounts, devices linked to your messaging or cloud accounts, old sessions on computers you no longer use, and sharing links for files you no longer want shared. Each major account, such as your email, cloud, and social accounts, has a settings page listing connected apps, devices, and sessions, and visiting those pages periodically is a habit worth building. When you revoke, the service loses its token and can no longer act as you, which is immediate and effective, though it does not delete data the service already collected, which is a separate matter. Revoke promptly when a service's purpose ends, when you stop using an app, when you sell or lose a device, and when you notice an entry you do not recognise, because an unrecognised entry may indicate a compromise, and revoking it is a safe first step before investigating further. It is also wise to revoke before a major life change, such as leaving a job or ending a relationship, because shared access that was convenient during the relationship can become a risk afterwards. Revocation is not rude or suspicious; it is maintenance, like collecting keys when a houseguest leaves, and most services expect it. Make revocation part of your twice-yearly checkup, and also do it immediately after specific events, such as a device change or a service trial ending, so that your access list always reflects your current life, not your past experiments. A tidy access list is a safe access list, and revocation is how you keep it tidy. Revocation also has a social dimension that is worth preparing for, because revoking someone's access, such as a former colleague's shared folder or a former partner's streaming profile, can feel confrontational, even when it is simply good hygiene. Framing revocation as routine maintenance, rather than as a personal judgement, eases that discomfort: you are tidying access as part of a regular review, not accusing anyone of wrongdoing. A brief, neutral message, such as that you are reviewing shared access as part of a routine clean-up, is usually enough, and most people understand it without offence. That framing also protects you, because it normalises revocation as a normal, expected event, rather than as a dramatic break, and normalised events are easier to perform promptly. Do not let social discomfort delay a necessary revocation, because delay is what turns a routine tidy-up into a lingering risk, and a risk that lingers because of politeness is still a risk. Revoke kindly, revoke promptly, and let routine be your explanation. Check: When should you revoke third-party access immediately, rather than waiting for a periodic review?

Section 23: Reviewing Connected Apps and Devices

Reviewing connected apps and devices is a structured habit that turns revocation from an intention into a routine, and it is worth doing methodically rather than by memory. Start with your most important accounts, email first, then cloud storage, then social media, and open each one's security settings to find the lists of connected apps, linked devices, and active sessions. For each entry, ask three questions: do I recognise this app or device, do I still use it, and does its access still fit its purpose? If any answer is no, revoke it, and if you do not recognise an entry at all, revoke it and investigate, changing your password and checking for suspicious activity, because an unrecognised entry can be a sign of compromise. Pay special attention to entries with broad access, such as apps that can read all your email or all your files, because broad access deserves stronger justification than narrow access. Also check for old devices, such as a phone you sold or a computer you replaced, because linked devices can often read your messages or files long after you stopped using them. Keep a brief note of what you revoked and when, so you can compare at the next review and notice patterns, such as a service that keeps reappearing. Doing this review twice a year, for each major account, takes less than an hour in total, and it closes more doors than most other single habits. Connected apps and devices are the extensions of your accounts into other services and hardware, and reviewing them is how you ensure those extensions still serve you, rather than serving someone else. A reviewed list is a controlled list, and control is the essence of account safety. Connected-device reviews also include devices you may have forgotten you linked, such as a smart television, a car system, or a borrowed computer where you signed into an account once and never signed out. Those forgotten devices can retain access for years, displaying your messages, your photos, or your files to anyone who uses them, and they are easy to miss because they are not phones or computers in the usual sense. During your review, look specifically for device types you do not expect, and revoke any device you do not currently use, because a device you do not use has no legitimate need for access. That sweep often surprises people, revealing a hotel television, an old tablet, or a friend's computer still linked, and each revocation closes a door that was open without your knowledge. Forgotten devices are forgotten doors, and a review that looks for unexpected device types is a review that finds them. Sweep broadly, revoke confidently, and your linked-device list will reflect only the devices you actually use. Section 24: Roles in a Team: Viewer, Editor, Administrator

Teams use roles to express least privilege in a structured way, assigning each person a role that bundles the permissions their job needs, and understanding roles helps you work in teams safely, even small ones. The most common roles are viewer, who can read but not change; editor or contributor, who can read and change their own area; and administrator, who can change settings, manage members, and control the whole workspace. Those roles are a ladder of increasing power, and each step should be granted only when the job requires it, because an administrator can affect everyone, while a viewer can affect no one. In a small team, such as a family business, a school club, or a volunteer group, roles are often informal, but making them explicit, even in a simple list, prevents the common problem where everyone is an administrator by default, because that was the easiest setup. Explicit roles also make changes easier: when someone's job changes, you change their role, rather than adjusting many individual permissions, and when someone leaves, you remove their role, rather than hunting for scattered permissions. Roles also support accountability, because actions can be traced to a role and a person, and a viewer who cannot delete cannot accidentally delete, which is a protection for the viewer as well as for the team. If you run a team, start with the smallest set of roles that covers your needs, assign the lowest role that lets each person do their job, and review roles when jobs change. If you join a team, ask what your role is, what it allows, and what it does not, because knowing your role prevents both overreach and frustration. Roles are least privilege made manageable, turning many individual decisions into a few clear categories that a team can understand and maintain together. Roles also help with a common team problem, which is the new member who is given broad access because no one knows what narrow access they need, and who then retains that broad access forever. A role system solves that problem by giving the new member a predefined role, fitted to their job, rather than an ad-hoc collection of permissions invented on the spot. That predefined role is easier to grant, easier to review, and easier to change, because it is a named bundle with a known scope, rather than a mysterious accumulation. When the new member's job changes, their role changes, and the old role's permissions are removed automatically by the change, preventing creep. Roles are therefore a kindness to new members as well as to security, because they give a clear, bounded start, rather than an overwhelming, unbounded one. A new member who starts with a clear role knows what they can do, what they cannot, and whom to ask for more, and that clarity helps them settle in faster. Roles are onboarding tools as much as security tools, and using them well serves both purposes. Section 25: Separation of Duties in Plain Words

Separation of duties is the principle that no single person should control all stages of an important process, because dividing a process between people prevents both mistakes and misuse. The classic example is handling money: the person who requests a payment should not be the same person who approves it and the same person who sends it, because dividing those steps means that a mistake or a dishonest act by one person is caught by another. The same principle applies to digital tasks: the person who creates a user account should not necessarily be the one who approves its administrator access, and the person who writes code should not necessarily be the one who deploys it to production without review. Separation of duties is least privilege applied to processes rather than to single permissions, and it recognises that even trustworthy people make mistakes, and that a second pair of eyes catches what the first missed. For small teams and households, full separation is not always practical, because there may be only one or two people, but the spirit can still be applied: a second person can review a significant change, a shared decision can be documented, or a sensitive action can require a deliberate confirmation step. For families, separation can be as simple as requiring an adult's approval for a child's purchase, or requiring two adults to agree before changing a shared account's recovery settings. The goal is not distrust, but resilience: a process that survives one person's error, absence, or dishonesty is stronger than one that depends entirely on a single person. When you design a process, whether for money, for accounts, or for publishing, ask whether any single person controls it end to end, and if so, consider where a second person, a review step, or a confirmation could break the chain. That break is separation of duties, and it is a quiet guardian of integrity. Separation of duties also applies to personal finances, where a simple separation, such as one person handling spending and another reviewing statements, can catch errors and misuse that a single person might miss. That household separation need not be formal or distrustful; it can be as simple as a monthly review together, where both partners look at statements and ask questions, turning a solitary task into a shared check. That shared check catches mistakes, such as duplicate charges or forgotten subscriptions, as well as misuse, and it also builds financial awareness for both partners. Separation, in this domestic form, is less about preventing dishonesty than about catching ordinary errors, and ordinary errors are far more common than dishonesty. A second pair of eyes, applied regularly to important processes, is a simple, powerful control, and it works in households as well as in businesses, because human error is universal. Invite a second pair of eyes to your important processes, and those processes will be stronger for it. Section 26: Privilege Creep and How It Happens

Privilege creep is the gradual accumulation of permissions beyond what a person currently needs, and it happens so quietly that it is one of the most common security problems in teams and households. It begins innocently: a person takes on a temporary project and gains access to a folder, covers for a colleague and gains access to a system, or helps with a task and gains administrator rights for a day, and when the project ends, the access remains, because no one remembers to remove it. Over years, that person accumulates a collection of permissions from many past roles, far beyond their current job, and that collection becomes a large target: if their account is compromised, the attacker gains all of it, and if they make a mistake, the mistake can reach far beyond their current work. Privilege creep also affects individuals: a personal account that has accumulated connected apps, shared folders, and old permissions over years carries a similar accumulation, and the same review habit applies. The defence is regular access review, where each person's permissions are compared with their current job, and anything that no longer fits is removed, a process that should happen at least when roles change and ideally twice a year. For households, privilege creep looks like a child who still has access to a parent's account from a past need, or a former housemate who still shares a cloud folder, and the same review, comparing access with current needs, fixes it. Privilege creep is not caused by malice; it is caused by forgetting, and forgetting is a normal human trait that systems must be designed to counter. Reviews, expiry dates on temporary access, and a habit of removing access when a task ends are the counters, and they turn a gradual accumulation into a controlled, current set of permissions. Least privilege is not a one-time grant; it is a current state, maintained by review. Section 27: Regular Access Reviews in Practice

Regular access reviews are the practical habit that keeps least privilege current, and doing them well is a matter of method rather than effort. An access review asks, for each person and each system, whether their current permissions still match their current job, and it removes anything that does not. Start with a list: who has access to what, which is often available from each system's settings or from a simple spreadsheet for a small team. Next, compare that list with current roles: who is still on the team, who changed roles, who finished a project, and whose access should therefore change. Next, act on the comparison: remove access that no longer fits, narrow access that is broader than needed, and document what you changed and why, so the next review can build on it. Next, check the special cases: administrator accounts, service accounts, shared folders, and connected apps, because those are the permissions most likely to be overlooked and most powerful if misused. For individuals, an access review is simpler: list your accounts, your connected apps, your shared folders, and your devices, and ask, for each, whether you still use it and whether its access still fits. Doing this twice a year, perhaps alongside your firewall and privacy checkups, keeps the habit manageable and ensures that drift is caught before it accumulates into creep. Section 28: Joiners, Movers, and Leavers

Joiners, movers, and leavers is the name for the three moments when access must change, and handling those moments well is the backbone of account management for any team, including a household. A joiner is someone new, who needs accounts created with only the permissions their role requires, a strong initial password that they change on first use, and a brief orientation to the team's account habits, so they start with least privilege rather than with broad access. A mover is someone whose role changes, who needs new permissions for their new role and removal of permissions for their old role, because keeping old permissions alongside new ones is privilege creep in action. A leaver is someone who departs, whose accounts must be disabled promptly, whose shared access must be revoked, whose files must be transferred to a successor where needed, and whose passwords for shared services must be changed, because a leaver with lingering access is a known risk. Section 29: Temporary Access and Expiry Dates

Temporary access is permission granted for a limited time, for a specific task, and it should always carry an expiry date, because a temporary permission without an expiry is a permanent permission in disguise. Examples are everywhere: a colleague covering your work while you are away, a contractor fixing a system for a week, a friend helping with a project for a weekend, or a child borrowing a parent's account for a single task, and in each case, the access should end when the task ends. Expiry dates enforce that ending automatically, so that even if everyone forgets, the access closes, and that automatic closure is far more reliable than relying on memory. Section 30: Emergency Access and Break-Glass Accounts

Emergency access is a planned way to regain control when normal access fails, and it is often called a break-glass account, because it is meant to be used only by breaking the glass in an emergency. Examples include a spare administrator account kept for when the main administrator is unavailable, a recovery code kept in a safe place for when a second factor is lost, or a trusted contact who can help restore access to an account. The paradox of emergency access is that it must be powerful enough to recover a system, yet protected enough not to become a back door, and resolving that paradox requires careful design: the emergency account should be separate from daily accounts, protected by a strong, unique secret stored safely, such as in a sealed envelope or a password manager's emergency feature, and its use should be logged and reviewed, so that any emergency use is visible afterwards. Emergency access should never be a shared password known to many people, because a widely known emergency secret is not an emergency measure, it is a vulnerability, and it should never be stored alongside the system it protects, because a thief who gains the system would also gain its recovery. For individuals, emergency access can be as simple as backup codes for important accounts, stored safely at home, and a trusted family member who knows where they are, without knowing their contents. For teams, it should be documented, with who may use it, under what circumstances, and how its use is reviewed, so that an emergency does not become an excuse for unchecked access. Planning emergency access before an emergency, when you are calm and can think clearly, is far wiser than improvising during a crisis, when pressure leads to poor choices. Break the glass only in a genuine emergency, log the break, and restore normal access afterwards, and emergency access will be a safety net rather than a hole. Emergency access also needs a test, because an untested emergency plan is a plan that may fail when it is needed most, and failure during an emergency compounds the crisis. Section 31: Password Managers and Account Separation

Password managers support account separation, because they make it practical to give every account its own long, unique password, which is the foundation of separation. Without a manager, people reuse passwords, and reuse links accounts together, so a breach at one site becomes a breach at many, defeating the separation that individual accounts are meant to provide. With a manager, each account stands alone, protected by its own secret, and a breach at one site remains contained to that site, which is separation in its most practical form. A manager also helps you organise accounts, by storing them with notes about their purpose, their second factor, and their recovery options, so that an account audit becomes a review of a list rather than a hunt through memory. The manager itself must be protected strongly, with a unique master password and a second factor, because it holds the keys to all your accounts, and its security is therefore the security of your whole account system. That concentration is a trade-off, but it is a favourable one, because a single well-guarded vault is easier to protect than dozens of weak, reused passwords scattered across sites. For families and teams, some managers offer sharing features that let you share a single password with a specific person without revealing it broadly, and that sharing should be used sparingly, for genuinely shared accounts, rather than as a way to share personal accounts. Section 32: Two-Factor Authentication for Every Important Account

Two-factor authentication should be enabled for every important account, because it transforms a stolen password from a total compromise into a minor incident, and that transformation is worth a few seconds per sign-in. Important accounts include email, banking, cloud storage, social media, and any account holding financial, health, or work data, because those accounts are the most valuable to attackers and the most damaging if compromised. Authenticator apps are generally preferable to SMS codes, because SMS can be intercepted or redirected, while an authenticator app generates codes locally on your device, and hardware keys are stronger still, because they resist phishing by verifying the site before responding. When you enable two-factor authentication, store its backup codes safely, in your password manager or a printed copy at home, because losing your second factor can lock you out, and recovery without backup codes can be slow or impossible. It is also worth checking which second-factor methods each account offers, and choosing the strongest available, rather than accepting the weakest by default. For accounts that do not offer two-factor authentication, consider whether the service is worth using for sensitive purposes, because a service without a second factor is a service with a single point of failure. Two-factor authentication is not only a security feature; it is an account hygiene habit, like a strong password, and enabling it across your important accounts is one of the highest-value actions in this whole book. Start with email, because email unlocks password resets for many other accounts, then work through banking, cloud, and social accounts, and keep a note of which accounts are protected, so you can see your progress. Section 33: Recovery Options and Account Safety

Recovery options are the paths by which you regain an account when you forget a password or lose a second factor, and they deserve as much care as the password itself, because an attacker who controls a recovery option controls the account. Common recovery options include a recovery email address, a recovery phone number, security questions, and backup codes, and each has its own risks: a recovery email that is itself weakly protected becomes a back door, a recovery phone number that changes hands can be used by its new owner, and security questions based on public facts, such as a mother's maiden name or a first school, can often be guessed or researched. The safest recovery setup is a recovery email that is itself strongly protected, with its own unique password and second factor, a recovery phone number that you currently control, and backup codes stored safely, rather than security questions with guessable answers. Review your recovery options periodically, because a recovery email from an old job, or a phone number you no longer own, is a recovery path that no longer leads to you, and may lead to someone else. When you change your phone number or email address, update recovery options everywhere promptly, because that moment of change is when recovery paths most often become stale. Also be careful about recovery options for shared accounts, because a recovery option shared with a former partner or colleague remains a path to the account after the relationship ends. Recovery is a safety net, and like any safety net, it must be attached to something solid, checked regularly, and replaced when it frays. A recovery option you control, protected strongly, and reviewed periodically is a net that will catch you, while a stale or weak recovery option is a hole disguised as a net. Recovery options also include a modern alternative to security questions that is worth preferring, which is a recovery code or a trusted device, because those methods do not depend on facts that can be researched or guessed. Security questions based on personal facts, such as a pet's name or a birthplace, are vulnerable precisely because those facts are often public, shared on social media, or known to acquaintances, and a determined attacker can discover them. Recovery codes, by contrast, are random values that only you hold, and trusted devices are physical objects that only you possess, so both are far harder to guess or research. Section 34: Reviewing Account Activity

Section 35: Signs That an Account Has Been Compromised

Signs that an account has been compromised are often subtle at first, and learning to recognise them early limits the damage an attacker can do. Section 36: What to Do If an Account Is Taken Over

If an account is taken over, a calm, practised response limits damage far better than panic, and the steps are worth learning before you need them. First, try to regain the account through its recovery options, changing the password to a new, unique one, and if recovery fails because the attacker changed those options, contact the provider's support through its official recovery process, providing the identification it requests. Second, once you regain access, or while you wait, protect connected accounts: change passwords for any account that shared the old password, enable two-factor authentication everywhere it was missing, and revoke unfamiliar sessions, devices, and connected apps. Third, check for damage: review sent messages, changed settings, forwarding rules, purchases, and new connections, and undo what you can, such as deleting spam sent in your name and removing forwarding rules. Fourth, warn your contacts, because an attacker may have messaged them in your name, and a brief warning prevents them from falling for a scam that appears to come from you. Fifth, report the takeover to the provider, because reports help providers identify attack patterns and protect other users, and if financial loss occurred, report to your bank and to local authorities promptly. Sixth, learn from the incident: ask how the account was taken over, whether through phishing, a reused password, or a weak recovery option, and fix that weakness, so the same route cannot be used again. A takeover is distressing, but it is also recoverable, especially if you act quickly and methodically, and many people emerge from one with stronger habits than before. Section 37: The Danger of Shared Passwords and Shared Accounts

Shared passwords and shared accounts are dangerous because they blur responsibility, complicate recovery, and multiply exposure, even when the sharing is done with good intentions. When several people know a password, no one can be sure who used it, a password change requires coordinating everyone, and a departure requires changing it everywhere, which is often forgotten, leaving former members with access. Shared accounts also prevent personalisation and accountability, because the system sees one identity, not several people, and it cannot distinguish one person's actions from another's. For families, sharing a streaming or shopping account may seem harmless, and for low-risk services it often is, but the habit should not extend to sensitive accounts, such as email, banking, cloud storage, or work systems, where sharing creates real risk. When sharing is genuinely needed, such as a household utility account or a team tool, use the service's own sharing features where available, which give each person their own sign-in with appropriate permissions, rather than sharing a single password. If no sharing feature exists, consider whether the account truly needs to be shared, or whether one person can manage it and share its outputs, rather than its access. When sharing ends, change the password immediately and revoke any connected access, because a shared secret that outlives its sharing is a standing invitation. Shared passwords feel convenient and trusting, but they trade accountability and control for that convenience, and the trade is rarely worth it for anything sensitive. Keep personal accounts personal, use proper sharing features for shared needs, and change shared secrets promptly when sharing ends, and your accounts will remain yours, even in a household or team that shares much else. Section 39: Permissions for Smart Devices and the Home

Smart devices in the home, from cameras and speakers to thermostats and door locks, have permissions that deserve the same least-privilege care as computer accounts, because each device is an account on your network with access to your home. When you set up a smart device, it typically asks for an account with the maker, permissions on your phone, access to your Wi-Fi, and sometimes access to your location, contacts, or other devices, and each of those grants should be judged against the device's job. A smart plug needs Wi-Fi and an on-off command, but it does not need your contacts or your location; a camera needs to record and notify, but it does not need access to your entire photo library. Devices also have their own internal permissions: who in the household can view the camera, who can unlock the door, and who can change settings, and those should be set per person, rather than giving everyone full control by default. Sharing a smart device with a visitor, a cleaner, or a former housemate should be time-limited where possible, and revoked when no longer needed, because a device permission that lingers is a key to your home that you forgot you gave away. Firmware updates for smart devices also matter, because they fix flaws that could let an attacker bypass permissions entirely, and a device that can no longer be updated should be reconsidered, especially if it guards something sensitive like a door. The habit for smart homes is a device inventory: list each device, its account, its permissions, and who can control it, and review that list twice a year, alongside your other checkups. A home full of smart devices is a home full of accounts and permissions, and tending that inventory is how you keep a convenient home a safe one as well. Smart device permissions also include the device's own data collection, because a smart device does not only act on your commands; it also observes, records, and sometimes shares data about your home, such as when you are home, when you speak, or what you watch. That collection is governed by the maker's privacy policy, and by the device's settings, which may let you limit recording, delete history, or opt out of data sharing for improvement purposes. Reviewing those settings during setup, and periodically afterwards, is part of smart device permission management, because a device's permissions include its data practices, not only its controls. A camera that records continuously, a speaker that stores voice recordings, or a television that tracks viewing habits each collect data that deserves a decision, and that decision should be yours, made deliberately, rather than a default you never examined. Smart devices are observers as well as servants, and managing their observation is as important as managing their control. Set their data permissions as carefully as their access permissions, and your smart home will observe only what you choose. Check: Why should a smart plug not receive your contacts or location permission?

Section 40: Permissions in Cloud Storage and Collaboration

Cloud storage and collaboration tools make sharing easy, which is their strength, but that ease also makes over-sharing easy, and permissions in those tools deserve deliberate attention. When you share a file or folder in the cloud, you typically choose an audience, such as specific people, anyone with the link, or the public, and a level, such as view, comment, or edit, and each choice should be the minimum that serves the purpose. Sharing with specific people is safer than sharing with anyone with the link, because a link can be forwarded, posted, or guessed, while a named share is tied to an account. View access is safer than edit access, because an editor can change or delete, while a viewer can only read, and comment access sits between them. Links deserve special care: a link shared in a chat, an email, or a public post can travel far beyond its intended audience, and many tools let you set an expiry date or a password for a link, which you should use for sensitive shares. Regularly review your shared items, in the tool's sharing management page, looking for shares you no longer need, shares with people who have left, and links that are still active long after their purpose ended, and remove or narrow them. Also check what happens when you share a folder: new files added to that folder are often shared automatically, which can expose a file you added later without thinking. Cloud collaboration is powerful, and its permissions are the controls that keep that power safe, so treat each share as a deliberate decision, with a specific audience, a minimum level, and an end date where possible. Shared with intention, cloud tools are a joy; shared by default, they are a leak waiting to happen. Cloud sharing also includes a risk that is easy to overlook, which is the risk of a shared folder's contents changing after sharing, because new files added to a shared folder are often shared automatically with the same audience. That automatic sharing means a file you add later, perhaps a private document you meant to keep personal, can become shared without a deliberate decision, simply because it landed in a shared folder. The habit that prevents this is to treat shared folders as public spaces within their audience, and to never place a private file in a shared folder, even temporarily, because temporary placement can become permanent sharing. Instead, keep private files in private folders, and move a copy to a shared folder only when you deliberately decide to share it, so that sharing is always a decision, not an accident of location. That discipline, private by default and shared by decision, mirrors least privilege itself, and it prevents the common surprise of a private file shared through a shared folder's automatic inheritance. Folders are audiences, and placing a file in a folder is publishing to that audience, whether you intended it or not. Check: Why is sharing with named people safer than sharing with anyone who has the link?

Section 41: Default Permissions and Why They Are Often Too Broad

Default permissions are the permissions a system, app, or service grants automatically, before you change anything, and they are often broader than most users would choose, because defaults are designed for convenience and compatibility rather than for least privilege. A new app may default to accessing your entire photo library, a new cloud folder may default to sharing with your whole team, and a new user account may default to administrator powers, all because broad defaults make the product work immediately, without configuration. That immediacy is convenient, but it means that a user who never changes a setting is operating with broad permissions by default, and most users never change settings, which is why defaults matter so much. The habit to build is to treat setup as a permission moment: when you install an app, create an account, or share a folder, pause and review the defaults before accepting them, narrowing what is broader than needed. That pause takes only a moment, but it prevents a broad default from becoming a permanent exposure, because defaults, once accepted, tend to persist for years. It is also worth revisiting defaults after updates, because an update can introduce a new default, such as a new sharing option or a new permission, that you never chose. For makers of software, secure defaults are a responsibility, and favouring products whose defaults are narrow is a way to reward that responsibility with your choice. For users, the rule is simple: never assume a default is safe, because defaults are chosen for the average user and the maker's convenience, not for your specific needs, and your specific needs are what least privilege is about. Review defaults at setup, review them after updates, and your permissions will reflect your choices rather than someone else's convenience. Default permissions also vary by platform in ways that are worth noticing, because a default that is narrow on one system may be broad on another, and a user who moves between systems can be caught by the difference. A photo app that defaults to selected-photos access on one phone may default to full-library access on another, and a cloud service that defaults to private folders on one plan may default to team-visible folders on another. That variation means you cannot rely on a single rule about defaults; you must check each new system, app, or service on its own terms, at setup, rather than assuming it matches the last one you used. That checking habit, applied consistently, prevents the surprise of a broad default on an unfamiliar platform, and it also teaches you how platforms differ in their approach to privacy. Defaults are choices made by makers, and makers differ, so your job is to check, not to assume, and to narrow where the maker's choice is broader than yours. A checked default is a chosen default, and a chosen default is one you can defend. Check: Why are default permissions often broader than least privilege would recommend?

Section 42: Minimum Necessary in Everyday Practice

Minimum necessary is least privilege expressed as a daily habit, and it becomes concrete when you apply it to ordinary decisions you make every week. When a colleague asks for access to a folder, share the specific files they need, not the whole folder, and set an expiry if the need is temporary. When an app asks for a permission, grant the narrowest option, such as only while using, or only selected photos, rather than always or all photos. When a family member asks to borrow your computer, offer the guest account, not your own account, because the guest account is the minimum necessary for their task. When a service asks for your phone number for sign-up, ask whether it truly needs it, or whether an email would serve, because a phone number is a sensitive identifier that can be misused. When you create a new account for a team, assign the lowest role that lets the person do their job, and explain that higher access can be granted later if needed, rather than granting it pre-emptively. Each of these decisions is small, but together they form a pattern of precision that dramatically reduces your exposure, because every unnecessary permission removed is a door closed. Minimum necessary also applies to data you collect about others: if you run a form, a survey, or a sign-up sheet, ask only for the information you genuinely need, because data you do not collect cannot be breached, misused, or demanded. That data minimisation is least privilege applied to collection, and it protects the people who trust you with their details. Practising minimum necessary daily, in small decisions, builds a mindset that makes larger decisions, such as team roles or cloud sharing, feel natural, because the same question, what is the minimum needed, guides them all. Small precision, repeated daily, is the whole practice. Minimum necessary also applies to the information you share about yourself when requesting help, because a support request, a forum post, or a helpdesk ticket often includes details that are not needed to solve the problem. Sharing only the details relevant to the issue, and redacting personal data, account numbers, or sensitive context, protects you while still getting help, because helpers need the problem, not your life story. That redaction habit is especially important in public forums, where a post seeking help can expose personal details to strangers, and where those details persist long after the problem is solved. Before posting or sending a help request, ask what the helper genuinely needs, and remove the rest, because minimum necessary is a principle for outgoing information as well as for incoming permissions. Helpers appreciate concise, relevant requests, so minimum necessary also makes help faster and better, as well as safer. Share the problem, not the person, and you will get help without exposure. Section 43: Documenting Who Has Access to What

Documenting who has access to what is a simple record-keeping habit that makes every other account practice in this chapter workable, because you cannot review what you cannot see. A document, even a simple spreadsheet or notebook page, listing each system, each person with access, their role, and the date access was granted, turns a scattered set of permissions into a visible map. That map lets you answer questions quickly: who can access this folder, who is an administrator on this computer, which apps are connected to this account, and whose access should change when someone leaves. For households, the document can be simple: a page listing each device, each account, and who may use it, kept in a safe place, not in a shared file that everyone can edit. For small teams, the document should be a little more formal, with a named owner who keeps it current, and a review date, so it does not become stale. Documentation also helps during incidents, because a responder who can see the access map can revoke compromised access quickly, rather than hunting through settings under pressure. It helps during audits, because an auditor can verify that permissions match roles, and it helps during onboarding, because a new member's access can be copied from a similar role rather than invented. The document itself must be protected, because it reveals who has access to what, and it should be stored securely, with access limited to those who need it. Documenting access is not bureaucracy for its own sake; it is the map that makes least privilege navigable, and a map kept current is worth far more than a perfect map made once and forgotten. Start with a simple list today, and improve it as you review. Access documentation also needs a simple update rule, because a document that is hard to update will not be updated, and a document that is not updated becomes misleading, which is worse than no document at all. Make updating easy: keep the document in a place you already use, use a simple format, such as a table with a few columns, and update it immediately when access changes, rather than saving updates for a monthly batch. That immediacy ensures the document reflects reality, because memory fades quickly, and a change recorded today is accurate, while a change remembered next month may be forgotten or misremembered. For teams, assign the update to the person who makes the access change, so the change and its record happen together, rather than relying on a separate person to notice. A document that is easy to update is a document that stays true, and a true document is a useful one, while a stale document is a trap that misleads incident responders and auditors alike. Easy updates are honest updates, and honest updates are the foundation of access control. Check: What should an access document list, and why must the document itself be protected?

Section 44: Teaching Least Privilege to Someone Else

Teaching least privilege to someone else is best done with a concrete, physical analogy, because the principle is abstract until it is tied to something a learner can picture. Start with keys: imagine a building where every person carries only the keys they need, a cleaner carries keys to the rooms they clean, a teacher carries keys to their classroom, and only the manager carries the master key, and ask the learner what would happen if everyone carried the master key. The answer, that a lost key or a dishonest person would compromise everything, is least privilege in a sentence, and it transfers easily to computers, where accounts are keys and permissions are doors. Next, show the learner a real example on their own device: open an app's permissions, or a shared folder's settings, and ask, for each permission, whether the app or person genuinely needs it, and what would happen if it were removed. That hands-on review turns the analogy into a skill, because the learner practises the central question, what is the minimum necessary, on a real system. Encourage a first action, such as moving daily work to a standard account, or revoking one connected app, because a single action teaches more than a lecture. Be patient with the learner's existing habits, especially if they have always used an administrator account or shared passwords, because those habits were formed for convenience, and changing them requires understanding, not scolding. Frame least privilege as precision and care, not as restriction, because people adopt practices that feel empowering more readily than practices that feel punishing. Teaching least privilege well is itself an act of least privilege, because you give the learner exactly what they need to decide well, and no more jargon than necessary. Teach with keys, practise with real settings, and celebrate the first narrowed permission, and the learner will carry the principle forward. Teaching least privilege also benefits from a second analogy, using a kitchen, because kitchens illustrate permissions in a familiar, non-technical way. In a kitchen, everyone can use the plates and the sink, but only the cook uses the sharp knives, only the manager opens the safe, and guests use the guest bathroom, not the staff areas, and those boundaries feel natural, not restrictive, because they reflect jobs and safety. Transferring that picture to computers, where everyone uses their own files, only administrators change system settings, and guests use guest accounts, makes least privilege feel equally natural, because it mirrors a system people already accept. That familiarity helps learners who find computers abstract, because kitchens are concrete, and concrete analogies anchor abstract principles. Use kitchens, keys, and buildings together, and least privilege will feel like common sense rather than like a technical rule, because it is common sense, applied to a new domain. Familiar analogies are bridges, and bridges carry learners further than definitions alone. Check: How would you explain least privilege to a beginner using only keys and doors?

Section 45: Least Privilege Myths That Mislead

Myths about least privilege mislead people into either ignoring it or applying it badly, and correcting the common ones helps you apply it wisely. One myth is that least privilege slows work to a crawl, but in practice, well-fitted permissions rarely block legitimate work, because they are fitted to the job, and the occasional prompt for elevation is a small price for contained damage. Another myth is that least privilege is only for large organisations, but households and individuals benefit just as much, because a standard account, a guest account, and a revoked app are all least privilege in miniature. Another myth is that least privilege means distrusting people, but it means protecting people, because a person with narrow permissions cannot accidentally cause wide damage, and cannot be blamed for damage they could not cause. Another myth is that once permissions are set, they stay correct, but jobs change, projects end, and people leave, so permissions must be reviewed, or they drift into creep. Another myth is that administrator access is a status symbol, but it is a responsibility and a risk, and the most security-conscious people often hold the least privilege for daily work, elevating only when needed. Another myth is that least privilege is all or nothing, but it is a spectrum, and narrowing one permission, revoking one app, or moving to a standard account are all valuable steps, even if other permissions remain broad. The antidote to each myth is the central question: what is the minimum necessary for this job, at this time, and does the current permission match it? When you ask that question regularly, myths lose their power, because they depend on exaggeration, while least privilege depends on precision. Learn the myths, replace them with the question, and your permissions will become calmer, narrower, and more honest. Myths about least privilege also include the myth that it is only about preventing malicious insiders, when in fact its greatest value is preventing accidents, because accidents are far more common than malice. A well-meaning colleague who deletes the wrong folder, a child who changes a critical setting, or a user who installs a harmful program by mistake all cause damage that narrow permissions would have contained, and those accidents happen daily in every organisation and household. Framing least privilege as accident prevention, rather than as insider-threat prevention, makes it more relatable, because everyone has made a mistake, while few have faced a malicious insider. That framing also reduces defensiveness, because a permission limit framed as accident protection feels caring, while one framed as distrust feels accusatory. Least privilege protects people from their own mistakes as much as from others' malice, and that protection is its most everyday value. Accidents are universal, and a principle that contains them is a principle for everyone, not only for the security-conscious. Section 46: Your Access Checklist

An access checklist turns this chapter into a short list you can follow and revisit, and building your own version is more valuable than copying a generic one, because it reflects your accounts, your devices, and your household or team. Start with accounts: one account per person, strong unique passwords, two-factor authentication on important accounts, and recovery options current. Then check account types: standard account for daily use, administrator only for maintenance, guest account for visitors, and children's accounts fitted to age. Then check permissions: app permissions reviewed, file and folder sharing narrowed, cloud shares set to named people with minimum levels, and third-party access revoked where no longer needed. Then check reviews: connected apps and devices reviewed twice a year, access reviewed when roles change, and old and dormant accounts closed or revived. Then check special cases: service accounts separate and narrow, temporary access expired, emergency access planned and logged, and shared passwords replaced by proper sharing features. Write the checklist down, keep it short enough to fit on one page, and revisit it twice a year, alongside your firewall, encryption, and privacy checklists, because those checklists together cover much of your digital safety. Share the checklist with your household or team, and invite improvements, because a checklist owned by everyone is followed by everyone. A checklist does not make you an expert, but it makes you consistent, and consistency is what turns good intentions into a system that actually protects you. Build it now, keep it visible, and let it carry the lessons of this chapter into your daily routine, one checked box at a time, until the checks themselves become habit. A checklist also benefits from a review of its own, because a checklist that never changes can become outdated as your accounts, devices, and household change, and an outdated checklist can mislead as surely as an outdated access document. Review your checklist twice a year, alongside the access review it supports, asking whether each item still applies, whether new items are needed for new devices or services, and whether any item can be simplified or removed. That meta-review keeps the checklist lean and relevant, rather than growing into a long, intimidating list that no one follows. A checklist that fits on one page, because it is reviewed and pruned, is a checklist that will be used, while a checklist that grows unchecked becomes a document that is admired and ignored. Prune your checklist as you prune your permissions, and both will remain useful, because both are living tools, not monuments. A living checklist is a used checklist, and a used checklist is a protective one. A living checklist, pruned regularly, is a checklist that will actually be used when it matters. Section 47: Least Privilege at Home in Practice

Least privilege at home in practice is a set of small, household-sized habits that together create a home where each person and each device has only what it needs. Each person has their own account, with a standard account for daily use and an administrator account held by the household's tech keeper for maintenance. Guests use the guest account or the guest network, never a family member's account, so a visitor's brief use does not become access to private files. Children have accounts fitted to their age, widening as they grow, with purchase approval and content limits that are explained, not merely imposed. Shared folders are few and deliberate, holding only what the household genuinely wants to share, while personal folders remain private, and cloud shares are set to named family members with view access unless editing is genuinely needed. Smart devices are inventoried, with per-person control where offered, and shared access revoked when a visitor, cleaner, or former housemate no longer needs it. Connected apps are reviewed twice a year, with tried-once apps revoked, and old accounts are closed rather than left dormant. These habits are not onerous, because each is a small decision made once and reviewed occasionally, and together they create a home where a single mistake, a single compromised account, or a single curious visitor cannot reach everything. A home practising least privilege feels normal, not restricted, because its members can still do everything they need, but nothing more, and that precision is invisible when it works. It becomes visible only when it prevents a problem, which is exactly when you will be glad it was there. Home least privilege is hospitality with boundaries, family life with privacy, and convenience with control, all at once. Home least privilege also includes a habit for visitors' devices, because a guest's phone or laptop joining your home network gains a position from which it could probe your devices, even if the guest has no malicious intent. That is why the guest network matters, as you learned in the firewall chapter, because it separates guest devices from your main network, giving them internet access without access to your computers, your shared folders, or your smart devices. Offering the guest network password, rather than the main network password, is a small hospitality habit with a large security benefit, and it also protects the guest, whose device is separated from your household's devices as well. Make the guest network the default offer for visitors, and keep the main network password for household members only, because that separation is least privilege applied to networks, giving each device only the network access it needs. A guest network is a boundary that feels welcoming, because it offers full internet access, while quietly protecting everything behind it. Section 48: Least Privilege in a Small Business

Least privilege in a small business follows the same principles as at home, but with more people, more systems, and more formal processes, because the stakes and the scale are larger. Start with roles: define a small set of roles, such as viewer, editor, and administrator, for each system, and assign each employee the lowest role that lets them do their job, rather than making everyone an administrator for convenience. Next, handle joiners, movers, and leavers with checklists, so accounts are created narrowly, changed when roles change, and disabled promptly on departure, with shared passwords changed when a leaver knew them. Next, separate duties for sensitive processes, such as payments, so no single person controls a process end to end, and add a review step where full separation is impractical. Next, review access twice a year, comparing permissions with current roles and removing creep, and document the review, so the business can show, if asked, that access is controlled. Next, manage service accounts and third-party access with the same care, giving each service its own narrow account and revoking access when the service ends. Next, train staff briefly on least privilege, explaining it with the keys analogy and showing them how to request access when they need more, because a process for requesting access prevents people from seeking workarounds. A small business that does these things, even informally, is ahead of many larger ones, because its permissions reflect its present, its processes survive individual absences, and its damage from any single compromise is contained. Least privilege in business is not bureaucracy; it is the discipline that lets a small team grow without its permissions growing out of control, and that discipline pays for itself the first time it prevents a mistake or contains an incident. Small-business least privilege also includes a habit for former employees' personal devices, because a former employee's phone or laptop may retain access to work systems through saved passwords, connected apps, or synced files, even after their work account is disabled. That residual access is easy to overlook, because disabling the account feels complete, yet the device may hold offline copies, cached credentials, or linked services that persist. Addressing it requires a leaver process that includes asking the leaver to remove work data from personal devices, revoking device-specific access, and changing shared secrets the leaver knew, because those steps close the device-level doors that account disabling alone does not. That process should be handled respectfully, as a routine part of departure, not as an accusation, because most leavers will cooperate gladly when the request is framed as standard practice. A leaver process that covers devices, as well as accounts, is a complete process, and completeness is what prevents a friendly departure from becoming a lingering exposure. Section 49: Common Access Mistakes to Avoid

Common access mistakes are the quiet errors that undermine otherwise good account habits, and knowing them helps you avoid the traps that catch even careful people. The first mistake is using an administrator account daily, which amplifies every program and every mistake, and the fix is a standard daily account with elevation only when needed. The second mistake is sharing passwords, which destroys accountability and complicates departure, and the fix is personal accounts plus proper sharing features. The third mistake is leaving old and dormant accounts active, which creates invisible doors, and the fix is a yearly account audit with closure or revival. The fourth mistake is granting broad permissions for convenience, such as full photo library access for a simple app, and the fix is the narrowest grant that serves the job. The fifth mistake is forgetting temporary access, which becomes permanent by neglect, and the fix is expiry dates and calendar reminders. The sixth mistake is ignoring connected apps and devices, which accumulate silently, and the fix is a twice-yearly review of connected lists. The seventh mistake is neglecting recovery options, which become stale and turn into back doors, and the fix is a review whenever you change email or phone number. The eighth mistake is assuming permissions stay correct, which leads to creep, and the fix is regular access reviews tied to role changes. Each mistake is ordinary, each fix is simple, and the pattern across them is the same: permissions drift towards broadness and permanence unless someone actively narrows and expires them. That active tending is the whole job of account management, and it is a job that rewards consistency more than expertise. Avoid these eight mistakes, and your accounts will be among the best-guarded of any household or small team, not because you are extraordinary, but because you tended them. Access mistakes also include a mistake that is easy to make under pressure, which is granting broad access to solve an urgent problem quickly, and then forgetting to narrow it when the urgency passes. Urgency is a poor time to make permanent decisions, because pressure narrows thinking and broadens grants, and the broad grant, made in haste, often persists long after the emergency ends. The habit that counters this is to treat urgent grants as temporary by default, with an expiry or a reminder set at the moment of granting, so that the urgency does not create a permanent exposure. When you grant access under pressure, say aloud, or note, that the grant is temporary and when it will be reviewed, because that spoken commitment makes follow-up more likely. Urgent grants are sometimes necessary, but permanent urgent grants are rarely so, and distinguishing the two at the moment of granting is a discipline that protects you from your own haste.
