Chapter 21: Public Wi-Fi, VPNs and Safe Remote Work

Section 1: What Public Wi-Fi Really Is

Public Wi-Fi is a wireless network that anyone nearby can join, usually without a password or with a password that is printed on a wall for everyone to see. You find it in cafés, airports, hotels, libraries, shopping centres and buses. It feels convenient because you can get online without using your mobile data, and it usually works the moment you connect. Behind that convenience, however, the network is shared with strangers. Your device and their devices are on the same local network, even though you do not know each other and have no reason to trust each other. That shared setting is the key fact to understand. A home network is normally used by people you know, and it is protected by a password that only your household uses. A public network has no such circle of trust. Anyone who walks in can join, and that includes people who are curious, careless or dishonest. This does not mean every public network is dangerous or that you must never use one. It means you should treat it differently from your home network. Think of it like a public noticeboard in a busy market. You can read the notices and leave a message, but you would not pin your bank details to it. In the same way, you can use public Wi-Fi for ordinary browsing, but you should be careful with sensitive accounts. The network itself may be run honestly by the café or hotel, yet the other users on it are unknown. Some public networks also have weak settings because they were set up for ease rather than safety. They may use old security standards, or they may not separate one guest from another. You cannot check all of that from your phone screen, so the safest habit is to assume the network is open and act accordingly. Use it for low-risk tasks, keep sensitive work for a safer connection, and learn the simple protections covered in this chapter. Good habits matter more than fear, and they cost nothing.

It also helps to think about who runs the network and why. A café offers Wi-Fi to keep customers comfortable, not as its main business, so the router may be an ordinary home model with default settings that nobody has reviewed in years. A hotel network may be run by an outside company, which is better, but you still share it with hundreds of guests. In both cases, the network owner is not watching every user or vouching for them. Your safety therefore depends on your own habits rather than on the kindness of strangers. That is not a reason to distrust every café owner; it is a reason to keep your own protections switched on. Updated software, HTTPS websites, a VPN for sensitive work, and a habit of forgetting networks afterwards together give you a safety net that does not depend on anyone else. When you understand the shared nature of public Wi-Fi, the rest of the advice in this chapter stops feeling like a list of rules and starts feeling like common sense.

Section 2: Why Open Networks Carry Extra Risk

An open network is one that needs no password at all, and its extra risk comes from how easily traffic can be observed. When your device sends information over Wi-Fi, that information travels through the air as radio signals. On a well-protected private network, those signals are encrypted between your device and the router, so a stranger nearby cannot easily read them. On an open network, that layer of protection is often missing or much weaker. That means someone with simple, freely available tools could see which websites you visit, and in older or badly set up cases, could see more. Modern websites help a lot here, because most now use HTTPS, which encrypts the connection between your browser and the website itself. That is a strong second layer, and it protects a great deal of your browsing even on an open network. Still, an open network leaves gaps. A stranger may be able to see the names of the sites you visit, even if the pages themselves are encrypted. They may also try to trick your device into connecting to a fake network or a fake login page. They may watch for unencrypted traffic from older apps that do not use HTTPS. The risk is not that every café is full of attackers waiting for you. The risk is that the opportunity is there, the cost of trying is low, and you have no way of knowing who else is connected. That is why security advice about public Wi-Fi sounds cautious. It is not about panic; it is about removing easy opportunities. If you keep your software updated, use HTTPS sites, avoid sensitive accounts on open networks when you can, and use a trusted VPN for work, you close most of those easy gaps. The rest of this chapter shows you how each of those habits works in plain words.

Consider also what an attacker actually gains from watching an open network. They may collect email addresses, session details, or login information from apps that fail to encrypt properly. They may learn about your habits, such as which bank you use or which company you work for, and use that knowledge later in a phishing email that looks convincing because it mentions real details. They may also inject false content into unencrypted pages, replacing a genuine download link or advert with a harmful one. These attacks do not require expensive equipment or rare skill; the tools are widely known and the barrier to trying is low. That is why defenders focus on removing easy wins. When your traffic is encrypted end to end by HTTPS or by a VPN, watching the network yields very little of value, and most opportunistic attackers move on to easier targets. Your goal is not to become impossible to attack, which no one can promise, but to become a poor use of an attacker's time. Strong defaults, careful site choices, and a VPN for sensitive tasks do exactly that, quietly and without changing how you browse.

Section 3: The Fake Hotspot Trick

One of the oldest tricks on public Wi-Fi is the fake hotspot, sometimes called an evil twin. An attacker sets up a wireless network with a name that looks exactly like a trusted one, such as the name of the café you are sitting in, the airport lounge, or the hotel. Your phone may even connect to it automatically if it has connected to a network with that name before, because phones often remember network names and rejoin them without asking. Once you are on the fake network, the attacker controls the router your traffic passes through. They can show you a fake login page that asks for your email, your social media password, or even payment details, pretending it is the normal Wi-Fi sign-in page. They can also redirect you to lookalike websites. The defence is simple and mostly about pausing before you connect. Always ask staff for the exact network name, and check the spelling carefully. A network called "Cafe_Free_WiFi" is not the same as "Cafe Free WiFi" if the real one uses spaces, and a small difference is a warning sign. Turn off automatic joining for public networks, and forget public networks after you leave, so your phone does not silently rejoin a copy later. If a login page asks for more than a simple agreement or a voucher code, be suspicious, especially if it asks for a social media or email password. A real Wi-Fi portal should never need your email password. If something feels wrong, disconnect, forget the network, and ask a member of staff. You can also use your mobile data instead for anything important. The fake hotspot trick works because people are in a hurry and trust familiar names. Slowing down for ten seconds and confirming the name breaks the trick completely, and that is a good trade for the small effort involved.

The fake hotspot trick also works in places you might not expect, such as conference centres, trains, and even outside office buildings, where a network name promises free internet to passers-by. Attackers choose names that people already trust or that sound official, and they may add words like free, secure, or official to make the name more tempting. Your device cannot judge trustworthiness from a name alone, which is why human confirmation matters so much. If you are at an event, check the programme or ask the organisers for the exact name and password. If you are in a hotel, check the welcome card in your room rather than trusting the strongest signal. Another useful habit is to disable Wi-Fi auto-join entirely and to connect manually each time, so no connection happens while your attention is elsewhere. It takes only a few seconds longer, and it puts you back in control. If you do connect to the wrong network by mistake, do not panic and do not type any passwords into its pages. Disconnect, forget the network, and switch to mobile data. Then, if you entered any password, change it from a safe connection as soon as you can.

Section 4: What HTTPS Does and Does Not Do

HTTPS is the small padlock you see in your browser address bar, and it is one of the most important protections on the modern web. When a site uses HTTPS, the connection between your browser and that site is encrypted, which means the contents of the pages and the information you type into forms cannot easily be read by someone watching the network. That is why banks, email providers and shopping sites all use it, and why browsers now warn you loudly about sites that do not. On public Wi-Fi, HTTPS is your best friend, because it protects you even when the network itself is open. However, HTTPS has limits that are worth knowing. It does not hide which website you are visiting from everyone; the domain name may still be visible to the network operator and to anyone watching traffic patterns. It does not prove that a site is honest. A scam site can also use HTTPS and show a padlock, because a padlock only means the connection is encrypted, not that the business behind it is trustworthy. You still need to check the address carefully and be wary of lookalike domains. HTTPS also does not protect you if you download a harmful file from a site, or if you type your password into a fake page that happens to use HTTPS. Finally, HTTPS only protects traffic inside the browser. Some older apps send data without encryption, and those apps are riskier on public networks. The practical lesson is simple: prefer sites with HTTPS, never ignore a browser warning about a broken certificate, and do not treat the padlock as a guarantee that a site is safe. Use it as one layer among several, together with updated software, careful checking of addresses, and a VPN when you are working with sensitive information.

A further limit of HTTPS is that it protects the journey, not the destination. Once your encrypted traffic arrives at the website, the site itself handles your data according to its own practices, which may be good or poor. A site with HTTPS can still store passwords badly, share your data with advertisers, or be breached by attackers who steal its database. That is why strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication matter even on HTTPS sites, and why you should be careful about what personal information you hand over. Browser warnings deserve respect too. If your browser says a certificate is invalid or expired, it may mean the site is misconfigured, or it may mean someone is interfering with the connection. Either way, do not click through the warning to reach a login page or a payment page. Close the tab and try again later from a trusted network. You can also check whether a site is genuinely the one you expect by looking at the full address, not just the padlock. Scammers register domains that differ by one letter, and HTTPS does nothing to stop that trick. Used together, HTTPS, careful addresses, and respect for warnings form a solid habit.

Section 5: Your Phone Remembers Too Much

Your phone and laptop are helpful in a way that can quietly work against you: they remember Wi-Fi networks and reconnect automatically. This is convenient at home and at work, where you want to join instantly. In public, it creates a risk. Your device may broadcast the names of networks it has joined before, looking for them, and it may join a network with a familiar name without asking you. An attacker can exploit that by creating a network with a common name, such as a popular café chain or an airport network, and waiting for passing devices to join. The fix is to take control of this memory. Go into your Wi-Fi settings and look at the list of saved networks. Remove the ones you no longer need, especially old hotel, airport and café networks. Turn off the setting that joins networks automatically, or at least turn it off for public networks. On many phones you can also turn off Wi-Fi when you are not using it, which stops the device from searching and joining on its own. Another good habit is to forget a public network as soon as you leave the place. It takes a few taps, and it means your phone will not silently reconnect next time you walk past a fake copy. Some devices also have a setting to use a random hardware address when joining networks, which makes it harder for strangers to track your device across places. Turn that on if your phone offers it. None of this requires technical skill, only a few minutes in settings and a habit of tidying up afterwards. Think of it like clearing your pockets of old receipts: the fewer old network names your phone carries around, the fewer chances a stranger has to impersonate one of them.

Device memory also affects laptops, tablets, and even smartwatches, not just phones, so it is worth checking each device you travel with. A laptop that automatically joins open networks can connect before you even open the lid fully, and it may start syncing email and files in the background over a network you have not approved. Turning off auto-join on your laptop and setting it to ask before joining new networks prevents that silent sync. Some operating systems also let you mark a network as public, which tightens the firewall and stops file sharing with nearby devices. Use that setting whenever you join a network outside your home or office. It is also wise to turn off file sharing, printer sharing, and screen sharing features when you are on a public network, because those features are designed for trusted environments. If you share files with colleagues while travelling, use your company's approved cloud drive or an encrypted messaging tool instead of local sharing. A few minutes spent reviewing these settings before a trip can prevent your device from volunteering information to strangers, and the settings only need to be changed once.

Section 6: VPNs in Plain Words

A VPN, or virtual private network, is a tool that creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a server run by the VPN provider. All of your internet traffic travels through that tunnel, so someone watching the local network, such as a stranger on public Wi-Fi, sees only scrambled traffic going to one server, not the individual sites you visit. Think of it like sending your letters inside a sealed courier van instead of handing them out one by one in a crowded room. The courier still knows where the van goes, but the crowd cannot read your letters. That is the core benefit, and it is why VPNs are recommended for remote work and for sensitive browsing on untrusted networks. A VPN does not make you anonymous on the internet. The VPN provider itself can see your traffic, so choosing a trustworthy provider matters a great deal. It does not protect you from phishing, malware, or a scam site, because those dangers sit beyond the tunnel, at the website or in the file you download. It does not make a dishonest site honest. What it does well is protect your traffic from local snooping and from some forms of network interference. Many employers provide a company VPN for staff working away from the office, and you should use it whenever it is offered, because it also gives you safe access to internal systems. If you choose a personal VPN, look for a provider with a clear privacy policy, a no-logs claim that has been independently audited, and support for modern protocols. Avoid free VPNs that make money from advertising or selling data, since that defeats the purpose. A VPN is one layer, not magic, but on public Wi-Fi it is a strong and worthwhile layer.

It is worth understanding where the tunnel ends, because that shapes what a VPN can and cannot do for you. Your traffic is encrypted between your device and the VPN server, and from there it travels to the website in the normal way. If the website uses HTTPS, the second part of the journey is encrypted too. If it does not, the traffic between the VPN server and the website is visible to others, though not to people on your local café network. This is why a VPN and HTTPS work best together. A VPN also changes your visible location, because websites see the VPN server's address instead of yours, which can be useful for privacy but can also confuse services that check your location for security, such as banks. You may need to confirm your identity more often when a VPN is on, and that is normal. Some streaming and shopping sites block known VPN servers, which is another normal side effect. None of these quirks mean the VPN is broken. They are signs that it is doing its job of hiding your real network location. For work, always prefer your employer's VPN, because it is configured for company systems and monitored by people whose job is to keep it safe.

Section 7: Choosing a Trustworthy VPN

Choosing a VPN is a trust decision, because the provider sits in the middle of your traffic. A good provider earns that trust with clear answers, not with loud advertising. Start with the privacy policy. It should say in plain language what data is collected, how long it is kept, and what happens if the company receives a legal request. A claim of "no logs" is common, but it is stronger when an independent audit has checked it, so look for audit reports and read their dates. Next, look at the technology. Modern protocols such as WireGuard and OpenVPN are well regarded, and a good app lets you choose or explains its default. Check whether the app has a kill switch, which blocks all internet traffic if the VPN connection drops, so your traffic never leaks out unprotected by accident. Check the company's home country and ownership, because laws about data retention differ, and a company that hides its owners is a warning sign. Be careful with price. A VPN costs money to run, with servers and staff, so a completely free service must pay for itself somehow, often through advertising, data collection, or limits that push you towards a paid plan. Some free plans from reputable paid providers are honest, with clear limits, but a free VPN you have never heard of, with no company details, is a risk. Read independent reviews from security publications rather than only the provider's own website, and avoid providers that promise total anonymity or claim to make you invisible, because no VPN can do that. Finally, test the app yourself: it should connect quickly, stay stable, and show you clearly when protection is on or off. A VPN you understand and trust is worth paying for; one you do not trust is worse than none, because it gives false confidence.

Another factor in choosing a VPN is how the company handles pressure from governments or advertisers. A provider based in a country with strong data retention laws may be forced to collect more than its policy promises, while a provider that owns its own servers rather than renting them has fewer third parties involved. These details are hard for a beginner to judge, which is why independent audits and the opinions of respected security researchers matter. Look for providers that publish transparency reports, which describe how many legal requests they received and how they responded, even if the numbers are small. Look also for open-source apps, because open code can be inspected by anyone, which makes hidden data collection harder to conceal. Be wary of review websites that rank VPNs mainly by affiliate payments, and instead check a few independent sources and look for agreement between them. A provider that is consistently praised for privacy, speed, and honest marketing is a safer bet than one that tops a single paid ranking. Finally, remember that a VPN subscription is a relationship: you will trust this company for months or years, so a little research at the start is time well spent.

Section 8: Setting Up a VPN Step by Step

Setting up a VPN is usually simpler than people expect, and you only need to do the careful part once. First, choose your provider using the checks from the previous section, and sign up on its official website, never through a link in a random email or advert. Download the app only from the provider's official site or from your device's official app store, and check that the developer name matches the provider. Install it, sign in with your account, and look through the settings before your first trip. Turn on the kill switch if the app offers one, so a dropped connection cannot leak your traffic. Choose a server location that makes sense: for everyday privacy, a nearby server is usually fastest, while for work you should use the server your employer tells you to use. Some apps offer automatic protocol selection, which is fine for most people. Connect once at home and test that everything still works: browse a website, check that your email loads, and confirm that the app shows a clear "connected" state. Learn how to disconnect and reconnect quickly, and learn what the app's warning looks like when the connection drops. If your employer provides a VPN, follow their instructions exactly instead of mixing in a personal VPN, because company systems may only accept the company one. Keep the app updated, just like any other security tool, and do not share your VPN account with strangers. If the VPN slows your connection a little, that is normal, because encryption takes work, but a large slowdown may mean you picked a distant server. A few minutes of setup at home saves fumbling in an airport later, when you are in a hurry and most likely to make a mistake.

If you travel often, it helps to practise the whole routine before you need it under pressure. Connect the VPN at home, open your work email, join a video call, and open a shared document, checking that each one works through the tunnel. If something fails, you can contact support calmly from home instead of troubleshooting in a noisy airport. Write down the steps for connecting, or save them in a note on your phone, so you do not have to remember them when you are tired. Some VPN apps also offer a setting that connects automatically whenever you join an untrusted network, which is a useful safety net if you forget. Turn that on if it is available and if it does not conflict with your employer's rules. Keep your login details safe, and use a strong, unique password for the VPN account itself, protected by multi-factor authentication where possible. If your VPN account were stolen, an attacker could use your subscription, so treat it like any important account. Practising at home also teaches you what normal looks like, so you will notice quickly if something changes while travelling, such as a missing VPN icon or an unexpected warning.

Section 9: When Not to Use Public Wi-Fi

There are times when the safest choice is simply not to use public Wi-Fi at all. If you need to do online banking, move money, access medical records, or handle confidential work documents, a public network is the wrong place unless you are protected by a trusted VPN and you have no alternative. Your mobile data connection is usually a better choice for sensitive tasks, because it is harder for a stranger nearby to intercept, and it does not put you on a shared local network with unknown users. Another time to avoid public Wi-Fi is when a network behaves strangely: the login page asks for a password you would never give to a café, the connection keeps dropping, or your browser shows certificate warnings. Those are signals to stop, disconnect, and use mobile data instead. You should also avoid public Wi-Fi for downloading software or updates if you can, because a tampered network could interfere, and it is better to update on a network you trust. The same goes for signing into important accounts for the first time on a new device. None of this means you must buy expensive data or avoid cafés. It means matching the task to the network. Use public Wi-Fi for reading news, watching videos, checking a map, or browsing a shop. Use mobile data or a trusted VPN for money, health, work secrets, and account recovery. That simple split removes most of the danger without removing the convenience. If you are travelling and mobile data is costly, plan ahead: download maps, documents and entertainment before you leave, do sensitive tasks before you go or after you return, and keep a small data allowance for emergencies. Good security is often just good planning, done before you are standing in a crowded airport with a low battery.

Planning ahead also helps when you travel abroad, where mobile data may be expensive or restricted. Before you leave, check whether your phone plan includes roaming or whether a local SIM or travel data plan would be cheaper. Download offline maps, translation packs, and any documents you expect to need, so you are not forced onto an untrusted network for basic tasks. Save important phone numbers and addresses offline, including your hotel and your employer's IT help desk. If you must use public Wi-Fi abroad, a trusted VPN becomes even more important, because network rules and monitoring differ between countries. Some countries restrict VPN use, so check the local rules before you travel and follow your employer's guidance. Keep copies of travel documents in an encrypted form rather than as plain photos in your gallery, and avoid logging into sensitive accounts on shared hotel computers. Shared computers may keep copies of what you typed or may carry malware from previous users. Your own device, your own data plan, and your own VPN are a much safer combination, and a little preparation makes that combination easy to use wherever you land.

Section 10: Working Remotely Without Leaking Secrets

Remote work adds a new layer to public Wi-Fi safety, because your work may include information that belongs to your employer or to customers, not just to you. The first rule is to use the tools your employer provides. If they offer a company VPN, use it for all work, not only for some tasks, because partial use leaves gaps. If they provide a managed laptop, use it rather than a personal device when you can, because it usually has encryption, updates and security software already configured. Keep work files in approved places, such as the company cloud drive, rather than copying them to personal USB sticks or personal email. Be careful with screens in public places. Someone sitting behind you in a café can read a document, an email, or a password you type, and this is called shoulder surfing. Sit with your back to a wall when possible, lower your screen brightness, and consider a privacy screen filter. Lock your device every time you step away, even for a minute, and set it to lock automatically after a short time. Do not discuss confidential work on speakerphone in a public place, and be careful with printed documents, which should be shredded or taken home rather than left behind. If you must print in a hotel, collect the pages immediately. Finally, know how to report a problem. If you think you connected to a fake network or entered a password on a suspicious page while working, tell your IT team quickly, so they can change passwords and check for misuse. Reporting early is not punished in a healthy workplace; hiding a mistake is far more costly. Remote work can be safe and flexible when these habits become routine.

Remote work security also includes how you handle your working environment at home, not only in cafés. A home office can leak information too, if family members or visitors can see your screen, or if a smart speaker overhears a confidential call. Keep work documents in work accounts, lock your screen when you leave the room, and use headphones for calls that discuss sensitive topics. Separate work and personal browsing where you can, for example by using a work browser profile or a separate user account on a shared computer. Be careful with personal smart devices that may record audio, and mute them during confidential meetings if your employer allows it. Back up work files only through approved company tools, because a personal backup could create an uncontrolled copy of company data. If you work from home on your own network, make sure your home router uses a strong password and current security settings, as covered in the home Wi-Fi chapter. These habits protect both you and your employer, and they show professionalism. Remote work gives you freedom, and with that freedom comes responsibility for the space you work in, whether it is a kitchen table, a hotel room, or a busy café.

Section 11: Split Tunnelling Explained Simply

Split tunnelling is a VPN feature that lets you choose which apps or websites go through the VPN tunnel and which ones use your normal internet connection. By default, a VPN usually sends everything through the tunnel, which is the safest and simplest setting. With split tunnelling turned on, you can make exceptions. For example, you might send your work email and company systems through the VPN while letting a local video streaming app use the direct connection so it runs faster or still sees your real location. That sounds convenient, and sometimes it is, but it needs careful thought, because every exception is a gap in the tunnel. Traffic that goes outside the VPN is visible to the local network again, just as if the VPN were off for that app. If you accidentally exclude a sensitive app, such as your browser or your cloud drive, you may believe you are protected when you are not. That is the main danger of split tunnelling: it adds complexity, and complexity creates mistakes. For most people, especially beginners, the safest choice is to leave split tunnelling off and send everything through the VPN. The small speed cost is worth the simplicity, because you never have to wonder which app is protected. If you do use split tunnelling, keep the exception list very short, write down what you excluded and why, and review it regularly. Never exclude your browser, your email, your banking app, or any work tool. Use it only for a specific, low-risk reason, such as a local service that blocks VPNs and contains no personal data. Some employers forbid split tunnelling on company devices, because it can leak company traffic, so always follow your employer's rules first. If you are unsure, ask your IT team rather than guessing. A VPN is most useful when its behaviour is predictable, and split tunnelling trades some of that predictability for convenience. Understand the trade before you make it.

A useful way to judge split tunnelling is to ask who benefits from the exception and what is at stake. If the exception only helps a music app load a little faster, the benefit is small and the risk, while also small for that app, still adds complexity you must manage. If the exception helps a work system that blocks VPN traffic, the benefit may be real, but you should ask your IT team for an approved solution rather than creating your own hole in the tunnel. Some VPN apps show a small icon or colour change when split tunnelling is active, which helps you notice the state, but not all do, so check how your app displays it. Review your exception list every few months, because apps change purpose: a game you excluded last year may now handle payments or messages. If you share a device with family, remember that your split tunnelling choices affect everyone who uses it. When in doubt, the default of sending everything through the tunnel is the choice that needs no review, no memory, and no worry, and that simplicity is itself a security feature worth keeping.

Section 12: VPN Kill Switches and Leaks

A kill switch is a VPN safety feature that blocks all internet traffic if the VPN connection drops unexpectedly. Without it, your device would quietly switch back to the normal connection, and your traffic would suddenly travel unprotected across the public network. You might not notice, because your browsing would keep working, and that silent switch is exactly the danger. With a kill switch turned on, the internet simply stops until the VPN reconnects, which is annoying for a moment but far safer. It is like a door that locks itself when the guard steps away, rather than swinging open. Most good VPN apps include a kill switch, but it is not always turned on by default, so check your settings and switch it on. There are different kinds of leaks to understand too. An IP leak happens when your real address is exposed despite the VPN. A DNS leak happens when the names of the sites you visit are sent to an unprotected server instead of through the tunnel. A WebRTC leak can happen in a browser, revealing your real address through a feature used for video calls. Good VPN apps protect against these, and there are simple test websites that check for leaks, though you should use well-known ones and understand what the results mean. If a test shows a leak, update your app, check your settings, and contact support. Do not ignore a leak and carry on as if nothing happened. Also remember that a kill switch only helps while the VPN app is running. If you quit the app entirely, the kill switch usually stops too, so do not quit the VPN and keep browsing on public Wi-Fi as if you were still protected. The habit to build is simple: VPN on, kill switch on, and if either fails, stop sensitive browsing until both are working again.

Testing for leaks does not need to be a daily ritual, but an occasional check is worthwhile, especially after you change settings, update the app, or switch providers. Connect the VPN, visit a leak test site from a reputable security publication, and compare the results with what you expect: the visible address and DNS servers should belong to your VPN provider, not to your home internet provider or the café network. If you use a browser with WebRTC enabled for video calls, check that test too, or disable WebRTC if you do not need it, though some call apps depend on it. Keep a note of what a clean result looks like for your provider, so a future change stands out. If a leak persists after updating and checking settings, stop using that provider for sensitive work until support resolves it, and consider whether a provider with repeated leaks deserves your trust at all. A VPN that quietly leaks is worse than no VPN in one respect: it encourages you to behave as though you are protected when you are not, which is why honest apps and occasional tests matter.

Section 13: DNS Leaks in Plain Words

DNS is the internet's address book. When you type a website name, your device asks a DNS server to translate that name into a number address that computers use. Normally this question travels in the open unless it is protected, which means someone watching the network could see which sites you are asking about, even if the sites themselves use HTTPS. A DNS leak happens when you are connected to a VPN but your device still sends those address-book questions to an outside server, such as your internet provider's server or the café router's server, instead of sending them safely through the VPN tunnel. The result is that part of your browsing history leaks out around the tunnel, like water finding a crack in a pipe. The tunnel still protects the contents of your pages, but the names of the sites you visit become visible. Good VPN apps prevent this by sending all DNS questions through the tunnel to the provider's own DNS servers, and many also offer encrypted DNS, which scrambles the questions themselves. You can check for leaks with a DNS leak test website while connected to the VPN. If the test shows servers belonging to your internet provider or to an unknown company rather than your VPN provider, you have a leak. Fix it by updating the app, turning on any DNS protection setting, and, if needed, asking support for help. On your own devices you can also choose an encrypted DNS service in your system or browser settings, which adds another layer. DNS sounds technical, but the idea is simple: the questions your device asks about where to go should be just as private as the journey itself. A VPN that leaks DNS is doing only half its job, and it is worth testing yours occasionally.

Encrypted DNS, sometimes called DNS over HTTPS or DNS over TLS, is a related improvement worth knowing about. It scrambles the address-book questions themselves, so even someone watching the network cannot easily read which names you are asking about. Many browsers now offer encrypted DNS as a setting, and some VPN providers include it automatically. Turning it on in your browser adds protection even when you are not using a VPN, though it does not hide your traffic from your internet provider entirely, since the provider can still see the addresses you connect to. The combination of a VPN and encrypted DNS is strong, because the tunnel hides the traffic and the encryption hides the questions. If you set up encrypted DNS yourself, choose a provider with a clear privacy policy, just as you would for a VPN, because that provider will see your questions. For most beginners, the simplest path is to rely on a good VPN's built-in DNS protection and to leave browser DNS settings at their defaults unless a trusted guide suggests otherwise. The key point remains: your device's questions about where to go deserve the same privacy as the journey that follows.

Section 14: Hotel and Airport Wi-Fi Habits

Hotels and airports are the places where most people use public Wi-Fi, and they deserve their own habits because the risks are slightly different. Hotel networks serve many guests, often for days at a time, and they may use a portal page where you enter your room number and surname. That information is not very secret, but it is still personal, so only enter it on the hotel's genuine portal, and never enter a full password for an unrelated account there. Airport networks are busier and more rushed, which makes fake hotspots more tempting, because travellers in a hurry connect to the strongest signal with a familiar name. In both places, start by confirming the exact network name with staff or with signs, and prefer the official network over a random open one with a similar name. Use a VPN for anything beyond casual browsing, and use mobile data for banking or sensitive work if the Wi-Fi feels slow, strange, or overloaded with login demands. Be careful with hotel room devices too. A hotel television or a shared business-centre computer may remember what previous guests typed, and may itself be compromised. Do not log into important accounts on them. If you must print a boarding pass, collect it immediately and log out of everything. Another good habit is to check your device after you leave. Forget the hotel and airport networks, so your phone does not rejoin a fake copy later, and review your accounts for anything unusual. Travelling is tiring, and tired people make quick decisions, so decide your rules before you travel: VPN on, mobile data for money, forget networks afterwards. With those three rules, hotel and airport Wi-Fi becomes a convenience you can enjoy rather than a worry you carry through your trip.

Hotel and airport networks also deserve attention after you leave, not only while you are connected. Some hotel portals keep your room number and surname on record, and some airport networks track devices by their hardware address across visits. Forgetting the network stops automatic reconnection, but it does not erase records the provider already holds. If a hotel asks for more personal detail than seems reasonable for Wi-Fi, such as a passport number, ask whether a simpler option exists, such as a voucher code. Be careful with loyalty programmes and Wi-Fi sign-ups that link your travel history to marketing profiles. Another habit for hotels is to check what the room itself offers: a wired socket may be more stable than Wi-Fi, but it is still a shared network, so the VPN rule stands. If you use the hotel's business centre to print, remember that printed pages can sit in output trays and that shared computers may keep copies of files. Collect your pages immediately and delete any file you uploaded. These small afterthoughts turn a hotel stay from a series of minor exposures into a tidy visit with nothing left behind.

Section 15: Tethering and Mobile Hotspots

Tethering means sharing your phone's mobile data connection with another device, such as a laptop, so the laptop can get online through the phone. Your phone becomes a small personal hotspot, creating its own Wi-Fi network that only your devices join. This is often safer than public Wi-Fi, because the connection between your phone and the mobile network is harder for a nearby stranger to intercept, and your hotspot is protected by a password you control. It is a good option when you need to do sensitive work in a café or hotel and do not fully trust the local network. There are costs to consider. Tethering uses your mobile data allowance, which may be limited or expensive, especially abroad. It also drains your phone battery faster, because the phone is running both its own connection and a Wi-Fi network. Video calls and large downloads will eat data quickly, so plan what you need to do and keep heavy tasks for a cheaper connection. Security still matters. Give your hotspot a strong, unique password, not a simple word, and change it occasionally. Use a network name that does not identify you, because strangers nearby can see it in their Wi-Fi lists. Turn the hotspot off when you are finished, so it is not left running for strangers to find. Check how many devices are connected, and disconnect any you do not recognise. Some phones also let you share data over a cable or Bluetooth, which can be safer than Wi-Fi in a crowded place, because the connection is not broadcast to the room. Tethering is not a perfect solution, but it puts you in control of the network, and that control is exactly what public Wi-Fi lacks. For short, sensitive tasks, it is often the best choice available.

Tethering also has a social side worth managing. When your phone broadcasts a hotspot, nearby strangers can see its name, and a name that includes your full name or company tells them something about you before any connection is made. Choose a neutral name, and avoid names that joke about hacking or security, which attract attention. If you tether in a shared workspace, be aware that others may ask to use your hotspot, and sharing it with strangers means sharing your data allowance and trusting their devices on your small network. It is reasonable to decline politely. Also check your phone plan: some plans limit tethering speed or charge extra, and some throttle video streaming over a hotspot. Knowing your plan avoids a surprise bill. If you tether a laptop for work, your employer's VPN still applies, because the hotspot only replaces the local network; it does not encrypt traffic beyond the phone. The hotspot and the VPN work together: the hotspot keeps strangers off your local link, and the VPN protects the journey beyond it. That layered thinking is the heart of this chapter, and tethering is one of its clearest examples.

Section 16: Using Your Phone as a Safer Hotspot

Setting up your phone as a hotspot takes only a minute, and doing it well is worth that minute. First, open your phone's hotspot settings and check the password. If it is still the default or a simple number, change it to a strong password you can remember, mixing letters and numbers. Give the hotspot a neutral name that does not include your full name, your company, or your phone model, because that name is visible to everyone nearby. Turn on the strongest security type your phone offers, which is usually labelled WPA2 or WPA3. If your phone offers a setting to hide the network or to limit the number of devices, use it when you can, though hiding alone is not real security. Connect your laptop, and check that it is using the hotspot rather than a nearby public network, because a laptop may prefer a network it already knows. While you work, keep an eye on the connected devices list on your phone. If a device you do not recognise appears, disconnect it and change the password. When you finish, turn the hotspot off straight away. Leaving it on in your pocket means your phone keeps broadcasting a network, using battery, and offering an entry point to strangers. If you tether often, consider a data plan with a generous allowance, and learn how to check your data usage so you are not surprised by a bill. A cable connection is an even quieter option: connecting your laptop to your phone with a cable shares data without broadcasting Wi-Fi at all. The principle is the same in every case. You control who can join, you control the password, and you switch it off when you are done. That control is the difference between your own hotspot and a stranger's network.

A hotspot also needs care when you use it with more than one device. A tablet, a laptop, and a colleague's phone can all join, and each one adds data use and a possible weak point. Check the connected list regularly, and disconnect devices that are idle, because an idle device may still sync email or cloud files in the background, using data you did not plan to spend. If you lend your hotspot to a family member, agree on what they will use it for, so a video stream does not consume the allowance you need for work. Some phones let you set a data limit for the hotspot or warn you as you approach your allowance; turn those warnings on. Battery is another shared resource: tethering while charging from a laptop or a power bank keeps the phone alive longer, but a hot phone in a bag can overheat, so keep it ventilated. When you finish, turn the hotspot off and check that your phone has returned to its normal state, with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth as you left them. These small checks take seconds, and they keep a useful tool from quietly becoming a drain on your battery, your data, and your privacy.

Section 17: Public Computers and Shared Devices

Public computers, such as those in libraries, hotels, and internet cafés, are convenient, but they are among the least safe devices you can use. You do not know who used the computer before you, what software is installed on it, or whether it carries malware that records keystrokes. A keylogger is a harmful program that quietly records what you type, including passwords, and a public computer is a plausible place to find one. You also do not know whether the browser is set to save passwords or to stay signed in. For those reasons, the rule is simple: do not use a public computer for banking, email, shopping, or any account that matters to you. Use it only for low-risk tasks, such as looking up a map, reading news, or printing a document that contains no personal details. If you must sign into something, use a private browsing window, do not let the browser save your password, and sign out fully when you finish, closing the browser afterwards. Clear the browsing history if the option is available, though this does not remove malware. Never plug in a personal USB stick to a public computer and then use it on your own devices afterwards, because the stick could pick up malware. The same caution applies to shared devices at work or at home when you are not sure who else uses them. Use separate user accounts, do not save passwords in a shared browser profile, and sign out every time. If you borrow a friend's device for a quick task, ask before saving anything, and sign out before handing it back. Public computers are useful for public information, and that is where their use should stop. Your own device, even a modest phone, is almost always safer than a stranger's computer.

Shared devices also include devices you lend or borrow within a family or a team, where trust is higher but the risks are not zero. A child using a parent's tablet may open the parent's email or make a purchase, and a colleague using your laptop may see notifications or open files you meant to keep private. Separate user accounts solve most of this: each person gets their own profile, their own browser data, and their own sign-ins, and switching profiles takes seconds. On a phone, a guest mode or a second profile can serve the same purpose. If separate accounts are not possible, sign out of sensitive apps before handing a device over, and turn off notification previews on the lock screen. When you borrow a device, respect the same rules in reverse: do not save your passwords on it, do not stay signed in, and delete any file you downloaded. If you must enter a password on a borrowed device, change that password afterwards from your own device, because you cannot be sure what the borrowed device records. These courtesies protect both people, and they are easy to explain: everyone deserves a clean device, and everyone deserves to have their own accounts left alone.

Section 18: Screen Privacy in Public Places

Screen privacy is about who can see your screen, and in a public place the answer is often "more people than you think." Someone sitting behind you on a train, standing in a queue behind you, or passing your table in a café can read emails, messages, documents, and passwords. This is called shoulder surfing, and it needs no technology at all, only eyesight and opportunity. It is also easy to underestimate, because you are focused on your own work and not on the people around you. The simplest defence is position. Sit with your back to a wall when you can, so nobody can stand behind you unseen, and avoid working on sensitive documents in crowded queues or packed trains. Lower your screen brightness, which makes the screen harder to read from a distance while still readable for you. A privacy screen filter is a thin sheet that sticks to your screen and narrows the viewing angle, so people to the side see only darkness. They cost little and work well on laptops and phones. On your phone, be careful when typing passwords or viewing codes in public, and shield the screen with your hand if needed. Set your device to lock quickly when idle, and to hide notification contents on the lock screen, because a message preview can reveal a code or a private detail to anyone glancing at your phone. Be careful with video calls too, because your camera may show documents or whiteboards behind you. Choose a plain background or blur it if your app offers that. Screen privacy is not about being secretive for its own sake. It is about remembering that a screen is a window, and in public, windows are meant to be looked through. A few small habits close that window to everyone except you.

Screen privacy also extends to what your device shows when you are not actively using it. Lock screen notifications can display message previews, email subjects, and verification codes to anyone who picks up or glances at your phone. Go into your notification settings and choose to hide sensitive content on the lock screen, showing only that a message arrived, not what it says. The same applies to smartwatches, which mirror phone notifications onto your wrist where others can read them. On a laptop, be careful with desktop notifications during presentations or shared screens, because a private message can appear in front of an audience. Most systems let you pause notifications while presenting or sharing. Another overlooked area is reflections: a window behind you, a mirror, or even a glossy poster can reflect your screen to someone seated elsewhere. Position matters as much as brightness. Finally, remember that photographs can leak screens too. A photo taken in an office or café may capture a colleague's screen, a whiteboard, or a document in the background, and posting that photo shares those details widely. A quick glance at the background before you post is a habit worth building.

Section 19: Safe Video Calls Away From Home

Video calls from a hotel room, a café, or an airport need a little extra care, because both your connection and your surroundings are less private than at home. Start with the connection. Join calls through a trusted network or a VPN, and keep your video app updated, because updates fix security flaws that could let strangers join or listen in. Use the meeting's password or waiting room features when they are available, and do not share the meeting link publicly. If a stranger joins a call, the host should remove them promptly and check the settings. Next, think about your surroundings. Check what your camera shows behind you. A whiteboard with project details, a document on a desk, or a family photo with personal details can all be visible to everyone on the call. Choose a plain wall, or use your app's background blur if it offers one. Use headphones rather than a speaker, so your side of the conversation stays private, and keep your voice low in public places. Be careful about screen sharing. Before you share, close tabs and documents you do not intend to show, and hide notifications, because a private message popping up during a shared screen can be seen by the whole meeting. If you need to show a document, open only that document, share only that window rather than your whole screen, and stop sharing as soon as you finish. Finally, be polite about recording. Do not record a call without telling everyone, and check your employer's rules. Video calls are a normal part of remote work, and with a trusted connection, a tidy background, and careful sharing, they can be just as safe away from home as they are in the office.

Calls away from home also raise the question of what others in the room can hear, not only what the camera shows. In a hotel room, walls are thin and housekeeping may enter, so keep confidential calls short and use headphones. In a café, other customers can overhear, and a stranger does not need to be listening deliberately to catch a name, a figure, or a password spoken aloud. Avoid saying passwords, account numbers, or personal details on any call in public, even with headphones, because your own voice carries. If a call turns sensitive unexpectedly, ask to continue it later from a private place, or move to a quieter corner and lower your voice. Check your microphone settings too: some apps pick up background noise clearly, including conversations near you that are not part of the call. Mute when you are not speaking, which protects both your privacy and the meeting's clarity. If you record a call, tell everyone at the start and check the law and your employer's rules, because recording without consent can be illegal in some places. Good call habits are mostly courtesy and awareness: know who can see and hear you, and adjust accordingly.

Section 20: Cloud Drives and Remote File Access

Cloud drives let you reach your files from anywhere, which is wonderful for remote work, but it also means your files are reachable from anywhere, including by anyone who steals your password. The first defence is therefore your account itself: a strong, unique password plus multi-factor authentication on your cloud account, whether it is for work or personal use. Without that, every other precaution is weakened. Next, think about which files you actually need while travelling. Do not sync your entire life to a laptop you carry through airports. Sync only the folders you need, and remove sensitive folders from a travel device when you return. Most cloud apps let you choose which folders sync, and some let you mark files as available online only, so they are not stored on the device at all. Be careful with sharing links. A link set to "anyone with the link" can be forwarded far beyond the person you meant, so use named sharing where possible, set expiry dates on links, and remove access when a project ends. Avoid public Wi-Fi for uploading or downloading highly sensitive files unless you are on a VPN, and prefer your employer's approved drive over a personal one for work files. Check the sharing settings on your folders occasionally, because a folder shared months ago for one project may still be open. If your device is lost or stolen, you can usually revoke its access from your cloud account's settings on another device, and you should do that immediately. Cloud storage is safe when its account is strong, its sharing is deliberate, and its contents are reviewed. Treat it like a filing cabinet with a good lock, and do not leave the drawers open.

Remote file access also includes files you receive from others while travelling, which deserve the same care as files you send. A document shared by a colleague or client may contain sensitive data, and downloading it to a travel laptop creates a copy you must protect and later delete if it is no longer needed. Open shared files in the cloud app where possible, rather than downloading them, so no local copy remains. If you must download, store the file in an encrypted folder or an encrypted drive, and delete it securely when the task is done, emptying the trash afterwards. Be careful with automatic downloads in messaging apps, which may save photos and documents to your device without you noticing. Review your messaging app settings and turn off automatic saving for media from unknown contacts. Also check version history and comments on shared documents, because they may contain older, more sensitive drafts. When you return home, review what you downloaded during the trip and clean up anything that should not stay on a travel device. Files accumulate quietly, and a tidy return routine keeps your devices lean and your sensitive data contained.

Section 21: Securing Remote Desktop Access

Remote desktop tools let you control a computer from somewhere else, which is useful when you work away from the office, but they also create a doorway into that computer that must be guarded carefully. If remote access is left open with a weak password, an attacker on the internet can try to walk straight through it, and they do not need to be near you to try. The first rule is to use remote access only when you genuinely need it, and to switch it off when you do not. A door that is closed cannot be tried. When you do need it, use the method your employer provides, because company tools are usually set up with encryption, strong login checks, and monitoring. Avoid installing a random remote access app you found online, especially a free one with unclear ownership, because you would be handing control of your computer to an unknown company. Strong authentication is essential. Use a long, unique password for remote access, and turn on multi-factor authentication wherever the tool offers it, so a stolen password alone is not enough. Keep the software updated, because remote tools are popular targets and flaws in them are fixed regularly. Limit who can connect, ideally to your own account only, and check the access logs or connection history occasionally for logins you do not recognise. Be careful with the computer you connect from as well. A shared or public computer should never be used to open a remote session into your work machine, because anything typed there could be recorded. If you finish a session, log out properly rather than just closing the window, and check that the remote computer locks its screen. Remote access is a powerful convenience, and like all powerful tools it deserves respect. Used through approved tools, with strong login checks and switched off when idle, it lets you work from anywhere without leaving the office door standing open.

Remote access also needs attention to the network it travels over. A remote session opened from public Wi-Fi should always run through a VPN or through the remote tool's own encryption, and you should verify that encryption is actually on, not merely assume it. Some older tools offer an unencrypted mode for compatibility, and that mode should never be used outside a trusted office network. Check the tool's settings for encryption and for login alerts, and turn on alerts that tell you when a new session starts, so an unauthorised session is noticed quickly. Limit session length where possible, so a forgotten session does not stay open all day. If you share your screen rather than giving full remote control, prefer that narrower option, because it exposes less. When a support technician asks for remote access to help you, verify their identity through a known channel before accepting, because scammers impersonate support staff and ask for remote access to steal data. A genuine support team will never rush you or ask you to disable security software. Remote access is a bridge into your computer, and bridges need gates, guards, and a habit of closing them.

Section 22: Two Devices, One Trip: Keeping Both Safe

Most travellers carry at least two devices, usually a phone and a laptop, and keeping both safe takes a little coordination, because a weakness in one can affect the other. Your phone often holds the keys to your laptop's world: it receives login codes, it may be your hotspot, and it may be signed into the same cloud accounts. If your phone is stolen or compromised, the attacker may gain stepping stones into your other accounts, so protect the phone at least as carefully as the laptop. Start before you leave. Update both devices, back both up, and make sure each one has a screen lock with a strong code or biometric check. Use different unlock codes for each device where possible, so learning one does not open both. Check that device-finding features are switched on, such as Find My Device, and practise locating and remotely locking a device from another one, so you know the steps before you are stressed. While travelling, keep the devices together in your bag when you are not using them, and never leave a laptop visible in a parked car or unattended in a hotel room if you can avoid it. Use a cable lock in shared workspaces when one is available. Be careful about charging too: charge both devices from your own charger and a trusted socket, not from a stranger's cable. At the end of each day, glance at both devices for anything odd, such as unfamiliar apps, changed settings, or login alerts you did not cause. When you return home, review both devices again, forget public networks on each, and change any password you entered on a network you did not trust. Two devices double your convenience, and with a small routine they do not need to double your risk. Think of them as travelling companions that watch out for each other, with you as the one in charge of both.

Two devices also means two sets of notifications, and notifications can leak information across devices. A message preview on a watch or a tablet can reveal a verification code or a private detail to someone nearby, even when your phone is safely in your pocket. Review notification settings on each device and hide sensitive content on lock screens. Also consider which accounts are signed in on both devices: if a streaming or shopping account is signed in on a tablet you lend to a child, purchases or messages may be accessible. Use separate profiles or sign out of sensitive apps on shared devices. Keep a single list of your devices, including serial numbers, in a safe place at home, so a loss can be reported accurately. If one device is stolen, change passwords for accounts signed in on it, and revoke its sessions from another device promptly. Think of your devices as a small team: each one should be locked, updated, and backed up, and the team is only as safe as its least-protected member. A few minutes of coordination before a trip keeps the team strong.

Section 23: Software Updates on the Road

Software updates are easy to postpone while travelling, because they arrive at inconvenient moments and use data you may want to keep, but postponing them on a trip is riskier than at home. Updates fix security flaws, and attackers know that travellers often run older software, so an unpatched device on public Wi-Fi is a tempting target. The best approach is to prepare before you leave. Update your operating system, your browser, your VPN app, and your work apps at home on a trusted network, so you start the trip fully patched. While you are away, allow important security updates to install, but be thoughtful about large ones. If your connection is slow or expensive, a huge update can eat your data and your patience. Most devices let you choose to download updates over Wi-Fi only, and you can decide case by case. Never download an update from a pop-up on a website or from a message claiming your device is infected, because those are common tricks. Real updates come through your device's own settings or your app store. Be careful about updating over public Wi-Fi if you can avoid it, since a tampered network could interfere, though updates from official stores are normally signed and checked by your device. If an update fails repeatedly on a strange network, wait until you are on a trusted connection and try again. Keep automatic updates switched on for your browser and your security software, because those protect you every day. A device that is up to date is a harder target, and on the road, where you face more unknown networks and more strangers, that hardness matters. A few minutes of updating before departure is one of the highest-value security habits a traveller can build.

Updates on the road also include the apps you rarely think about, such as your password manager, your authenticator app, and your cloud storage app. These are security-critical, so their updates deserve priority even when data is limited. Check for updates manually in your app store before a long trip, and read what each update changes if the store shows it. Be wary of fake update prompts inside a browser, which often lead to malware; genuine app updates come only from the official store or the device's settings. If a device prompts you to update its operating system while you are on public Wi-Fi, it is usually safe to allow a small security update, because updates are signed and verified, but a large feature update can wait for a trusted network. Keep a note of your devices' current versions before travel, so you can tell whether an update succeeded. If an update fails, try again on a better connection rather than ignoring it. Staying current is a quiet, continuous defence, and travellers who keep it up are measurably harder targets than those who postpone every update until they return home.

Section 24: Backing Up While Travelling

A trip is exactly when a backup proves its worth, because travel is when devices are lost, stolen, broken, or dropped. A phone that falls into water or a laptop left in a taxi can take years of photos and important documents with it, unless a copy exists somewhere else. The rule is simple: back up before you leave, and keep backing up while you are away if the trip is long. Before departure, run a full backup of each device to your usual place, whether that is a cloud service or an external drive at home, and check that the backup actually completed. Do not assume it worked; open the backup settings and look for a recent successful date. While travelling, automatic cloud backup over Wi-Fi is convenient, but be careful on public networks. Let photos and documents back up over a VPN or over mobile data where possible, and avoid backing up highly sensitive work files to a personal cloud account if your employer forbids it. If your trip involves important new work, such as photos from an event or notes from meetings, copy those files to a second place each evening, such as an encrypted USB drive or a separate cloud folder. Encryption matters for any backup you carry with you. An unencrypted USB drive full of your files is a gift to a thief, while an encrypted one is useless without the password. Keep the backup separate from the original device, so a single theft does not take both. When you return, verify that everything is still there, and keep the travel backup until you are sure your normal backup has caught up. Backups are boring until the day they save you, and travellers have more of those days than most people.

Backing up while travelling also means thinking about what you would do if both your device and your backup were taken together. Keep at least one backup copy separate from your luggage, such as a cloud backup that you can reach from any device, so a single theft cannot take everything. Test restoring a single file from your backup before the trip, because a backup you have never restored is only a hope, not a guarantee. If you use an external drive for travel backups, encrypt it and keep it in a different bag from your laptop. Label it neutrally, without your name or the words backup or private, which attract attention. For irreplaceable items such as travel documents and photos, consider a second cloud copy in a different service, so a problem with one provider does not leave you stranded. Remember that backups themselves need protection: a cloud backup account should have a strong password and multi-factor authentication, just like any important account. A backup is your safety net, and a safety net with a hole in it is worse than none, because it encourages you to take risks you would otherwise avoid.

Section 25: What to Do If a Network Feels Wrong

Sometimes a network just feels wrong, and learning to trust that feeling is a genuine security skill. Perhaps the login page asks for information it should not need, your browser shows certificate warnings, pages redirect to strange addresses, or the network name is almost but not quite the same as the official one. Perhaps your device connects and immediately shows warnings, or your VPN refuses to connect at all. In any of these cases, the right move is to stop and disconnect. Do not try to push through the warning to get your work done, because warnings exist for exactly these moments. Disconnect from the network, forget it in your settings, and switch to mobile data. If you already typed a password into a suspicious page, change that password as soon as you are on a safe connection, and turn on multi-factor authentication if it is not already on. If it was a work account, tell your IT team, describing what happened and when, so they can check for misuse and help you reset safely. If you entered payment details, contact your bank promptly and watch your statements. It also helps to note down details while they are fresh: the network name, the time, the place, and what the page asked for. That note helps IT staff and helps you remember what to change. Do not reconnect to the same network to investigate, and do not download any tool the network offers you. A network that feels wrong may be harmless and badly configured, or it may be hostile, and you cannot tell the difference from your side of the screen. Treating every suspicious network as hostile costs you a few minutes and a little mobile data. Treating a hostile network as harmless could cost you an account. When in doubt, disconnect first and ask questions later.

A network that feels wrong can also show subtler signs, such as pages loading with the wrong language, adverts that do not match the site, or a login page that appears for a site you are already signed into. These can indicate interference, though they can also be caused by a misconfigured network or a browser issue. The safe response is the same: disconnect, and try again from mobile data. If the problem disappears on mobile data, the network was the cause, and you should avoid it. If the problem follows you to mobile data, the cause may be your device or the site itself, and you should update your browser and check the site's official status. Keep a note of suspicious networks and tell the venue if you can, because staff may not know their network has been impersonated. A venue that learns about a fake hotspot can warn other guests and investigate. You are not obliged to investigate yourself, and you should not confront anyone you suspect. Simply disconnect, protect your accounts, and report what you observed to the right people. Caution, combined with a calm report, protects both you and the next person who walks in.

Section 26: Recognising Captive Portals

A captive portal is the login or agreement page that appears when you join many public networks, before you get full internet access. It might ask you to accept terms, enter a voucher code, or type your room number. Captive portals are normal, and most are genuine, but they are also easy to fake, because anyone running a network can show any page they like. The trick is learning to recognise a plausible portal and to spot the implausible ones. A genuine portal usually matches the place you are in: it shows the hotel, airport, or café name, it uses reasonable language, and it asks for only what is needed, such as agreement to terms or a code from your receipt. A suspicious portal asks for your email password, your social media login, your bank details, or a full payment card for a supposedly free network. No genuine free Wi-Fi needs your email password. Another warning sign is a portal that appears on a network you did not expect, or one that keeps reappearing after you have already accepted. Check the address bar if your browser shows it, and look for spelling mistakes or a domain that has nothing to do with the venue. Be careful with portals that ask you to download an app or a profile to get online, because that download could be harmful. If you are unsure, ask staff whether the portal is theirs and what it should ask for. Once you are past the portal, your browsing should still be protected by HTTPS and, for sensitive work, by a VPN, because the portal only controls entry to the network, not safety on it. Captive portals are a normal part of public Wi-Fi, and with a little scepticism about what they ask for, they are easy to handle safely.

Captive portals also appear on networks that charge for access, such as some hotels and airports, where the portal asks for payment. Treat payment portals with extra care: check that the address is genuine, that the page uses HTTPS, and that the amount matches what you were told. Prefer paying through a trusted method, and keep the receipt. Be suspicious of a portal that asks for a full card number for a free network, or that shows a payment form with spelling mistakes and mismatched branding. Some portals ask you to install a profile or certificate to get online, claiming it improves the connection; installing a certificate from an untrusted network can let the operator inspect your encrypted traffic, so decline such requests unless your employer explicitly instructs you and provides the certificate through official channels. If a portal will not let you proceed without installing something suspicious, use mobile data instead. A genuine portal's job is simply to let you in, not to reconfigure your device. Knowing that boundary helps you spot portals that overreach, and declining them costs you nothing but a little mobile data.

Section 27: Cookies and Sessions on Shared Networks

Cookies are small pieces of data that websites store in your browser to remember you, such as keeping you signed in or remembering what is in your shopping basket. A session is the period during which a website treats you as signed in, based on a session cookie. On a shared network, these matter because an attacker who can capture your session cookie might be able to impersonate you on that site without knowing your password, a trick sometimes called session hijacking. Modern websites protect against this with HTTPS, which encrypts cookies in transit, and with flags that mark cookies as secure, so they are only sent over encrypted connections. That protection is strong, but it is not perfect, especially for older sites or apps that do not use it properly. Your habits can help. Sign out of important accounts when you finish using them on a public network, rather than just closing the tab, because signing out usually invalidates the session. Avoid staying signed into banking or email on a shared device or an untrusted network for longer than needed. Keep your browser updated, and consider clearing cookies after using public Wi-Fi if you are concerned, though this will sign you out of sites. Use a VPN, which encrypts all your traffic, including cookies, so a watcher on the local network cannot capture them. Be careful with the "remember me" option on public networks, because it creates a long-lived session that is more valuable if stolen. On your own devices, a password manager and multi-factor authentication mean that even a stolen session is less useful, because the attacker still faces extra checks for sensitive actions. Cookies are not evil; they make the web usable. Understanding that they are keys to your signed-in state helps you treat them with the care they deserve on shared networks.

Sessions also matter on devices you share with others, because a signed-in session left open on a shared device is an open door. If you use a family tablet or a colleague's computer, sign out of every account when you finish, and close the browser. Some sites offer a 'sign out of all devices' option, which is useful after using a shared or public device. Be careful with single sign-on, where signing into one service signs you into many: convenient, but a single open session can expose several accounts at once. On your own devices, you can reduce session risk by setting browsers to clear cookies on exit, though this means signing in more often, which is a fair trade for sensitive accounts. Pay attention to 'remember this device' prompts on public networks: declining them means the site will ask for multi-factor authentication again next time, which is exactly what you want. If a site offers trusted device management, review the list occasionally and remove devices you no longer use. Sessions are invisible keys, and managing them deliberately is a quiet but powerful habit.

Section 28: Bluetooth Risks in Public Places

Bluetooth lets your devices talk to nearby devices without cables, which is convenient for headphones, watches, and car systems, but in a public place it also means your device is announcing itself to strangers. Most modern Bluetooth is reasonably secure when it is set up well, but there are still risks worth knowing. An attacker nearby may try to connect to a device that is in pairing mode, or may exploit a flaw in older Bluetooth software. Some attacks try to pair without your knowledge, while others simply collect information about which devices are nearby. The defences are straightforward. Keep Bluetooth switched off when you are not using it, especially in crowded places. When you do use it, make sure your device is not in discoverable mode permanently; most phones only become discoverable for a short time when you open the pairing screen, which is the right behaviour. Only pair with devices you recognise, and check the pairing code shown on both devices matches before confirming. If a pairing request appears from a device you do not recognise, decline it. Remove old pairings you no longer need, such as a rental car's system or a hotel speaker, because a forgotten pairing is a standing invitation. Keep your phone's software updated, because Bluetooth flaws are fixed in updates. Be careful with Bluetooth accessories that ask for access to your contacts or messages; a headphone does not need your address book, and granting that access to a cheap unknown device is a needless risk. Bluetooth is a useful tool, and these habits do not make it hard to use. They simply stop your devices from chatting with strangers when you are not looking, which is a sensible rule for any wireless technology in a public place.

Bluetooth security also depends on the accessories you choose. Cheap, unknown-brand headphones or speakers may have outdated Bluetooth software with known flaws, and some ask for broad permissions during setup, such as access to contacts or call history, that they do not need. Buy accessories from reputable makers, keep their firmware updated where the maker provides updates, and decline permissions that do not match the device's purpose. A speaker does not need your messages, and a fitness tracker does not need your call log. When pairing a new accessory, do it at home rather than in a crowded place, so the pairing process is not observed or interrupted. If an accessory behaves strangely, such as connecting on its own or asking to pair repeatedly, remove it and check for updates or a replacement. Turn off Bluetooth on devices you are not actively using, such as a tablet in your bag, because a silent device can still be probed. Bluetooth is designed for convenience, and these habits preserve that convenience while removing the easiest opportunities for misuse. A few settings and a little scepticism about permissions are all it takes.

Section 29: NFC and Public Charging Dangers

NFC, or near-field communication, is the short-range wireless technology behind tap-to-pay cards and phone payments. It only works over a few centimetres, which limits the risk, but it is still worth understanding. A payment terminal you tap is normally safe, because payments use encryption and your bank checks the transaction. The risk comes more from unfamiliar readers and from social tricks, such as someone holding a reader close to your pocket in a crowd, though modern cards and phones have limits and checks that make large-scale theft this way difficult. You can reduce the risk by keeping cards and phones in a front pocket in crowds, and by using your phone's payment app, which usually requires you to unlock the phone or confirm with a fingerprint before paying, rather than a card that pays on tap alone. Public charging is a separate danger that is often confused with NFC. A public USB charging port or a stranger's cable could, in theory, do more than charge: a compromised port could try to access data on your phone, an attack nicknamed juice jacking. The risk in practice is low, but the defence is cheap and simple. Carry your own charger and plug it into a normal power socket, or use a USB data blocker, a small adapter that allows power through but blocks data. A power bank charged at home is another safe option. If you must use a public USB port, choose charge-only mode on your phone if it offers one, and decline any prompt on your phone asking whether to trust the connected computer. Public charging stations are convenient, and most are harmless, but your own charger removes the question entirely. Small, cheap habits like these are the heart of travel security.

NFC payments are protected by several layers that are easy to overlook. Your card or phone does not transmit your full card number; it sends a one-time token that is useless if captured, and transactions above a certain amount usually require a PIN or biometric confirmation. That is why tap-to-pay is generally safe at genuine terminals. The greater risk is social: a stranger may try to distract you at a payment point, or a fake terminal may be placed over a real one. Check that a terminal looks normal and is firmly attached, and shield your PIN when you enter it. For phones, keep the payment app locked behind your fingerprint or face, and review your bank's transaction alerts promptly. If your bank offers instant notifications for every payment, turn them on, because they reveal misuse within seconds. If a card is lost, freeze it from your banking app immediately rather than waiting to call. These habits make NFC payments one of the safer ways to pay, especially compared with carrying cash or handing a card to a stranger. Technology helps, but attention at the payment point remains your best defence.

Section 30: Juice Jacking: Charging Safely

Juice jacking is the name for an attack where a compromised public charging port or cable tries to steal data from your phone or install harmful software while it charges. The idea works because a normal USB cable carries both power and data on different wires. When you plug your phone into a port, the phone may ask whether to trust the connected device, and if you accept, or if your phone is set to trust automatically, data could flow as well as electricity. Security researchers have shown this attack in demonstrations, though real-world cases are rare, and modern phones have added protections, such as asking before trusting a new computer. Even so, the defence is so easy that it is worth doing every time. The simplest defence is to use your own wall charger plugged into a mains socket, because a mains socket supplies only power, not data. If no socket is available, use a power bank that you charged at home. If you must use a public USB port, use a USB data blocker, sometimes called a USB condom, which is a small, cheap adapter that passes power but blocks the data wires. Alternatively, carry a charge-only cable, which is built without data wires at all. When your phone asks whether to trust a connected device, always say no unless you are at your own computer. Also, lock your phone before plugging it in, because a locked phone shares less. Keep your phone's software updated, since updates strengthen these protections. Juice jacking is a good example of a low-probability risk with a low-cost defence, and that combination makes the defence worthwhile. You do not need to fear every airport charging station, but you also do not need to trust one with your data when your own charger does the same job safely.

Safe charging also includes the cables you borrow. A cable is not just a wire; a compromised cable can contain a tiny chip that acts like a keyboard or a storage device when plugged in. This is rare, but it is a reason never to borrow a cable from a stranger or to pick up a cable left behind in a public place. Carry your own cable, and if you must borrow one, use it only with a USB data blocker, or charge a power bank rather than your phone directly. Inspect your own cables occasionally for damage or unusual bulk near the connector, which can indicate tampering, though most damage is ordinary wear. Keep a spare cable in your travel kit so you are never forced to borrow one. The same caution applies to charging from another person's laptop or a shared computer: decline any trust prompt, and prefer a wall socket. Charging is a daily necessity, and daily necessities deserve safe routines. Your own charger, your own cable, and a data blocker for emergencies form a simple routine that removes juice jacking and malicious cables from your list of worries entirely.

Section 31: Working From a Café: A Checklist

Working from a café can be pleasant and productive, but it deserves a small checklist, because cafés combine public Wi-Fi, strangers nearby, and easy distraction. Before you open your laptop, choose your seat with care. Sit with your back to a wall if possible, so nobody can read your screen from behind, and avoid seats near the door where passers-by brush past. Connect to the network only after confirming its exact name with staff, and turn on your VPN before opening any work app. Check that your screen lock is set to a short timeout, and consider a privacy filter if you work in cafés often. Keep your bag and devices close, and never leave your laptop on the table while you go to the counter. A few seconds is enough for a theft. When you order, take your valuables with you or ask a trusted companion to watch them. Use headphones for calls, keep your voice low, and avoid discussing confidential details where nearby tables can hear. Do not print sensitive documents on a café printer, and do not plug into a stranger's charger. Save your work often to your company's approved drive, so a sudden disconnection or a dead battery does not lose an hour of effort. When you finish, sign out of work accounts, disconnect from the Wi-Fi, and forget the network in your settings. Pack up everything, including cables and small items that are easy to leave behind. A checklist like this sounds formal, but after a few visits it becomes automatic, and it turns a café from a risky place into a pleasant office. The goal is not to be suspicious of everyone around you, but to make good habits so routine that you can relax and enjoy the coffee while your work stays safe.

A café checklist also benefits from thinking about time. A short visit for email is different from a full working day, and the longer you stay, the more exposure you accumulate. For a long session, take breaks away from the screen, lock the device each time, and re-check that you are still on the right network and that your VPN is still connected, because networks drop and devices reconnect to the wrong network silently. Bring water and keep your belongings in sight, because a comfortable worker is less alert to a bag sliding away. If the café is busy and noisy, consider whether a call is appropriate at all, or whether it can wait for a quieter place. Some cafés also offer wired sockets or a quiet corner; choosing the quiet corner reduces both shoulder surfing and eavesdropping. Finally, be a good guest: buy something, clear your table, and leave your seat tidy. A worker who is welcome is a worker who can return, and a regular café where staff know you is a place where a fake network is more likely to be noticed. Good habits and good manners reinforce each other.

Section 32: Working From a Hotel Room

A hotel room feels private, but it is not your home, and a few habits make it safer for work. The room's Wi-Fi is shared with other guests, so treat it like any public network: confirm the name, use a VPN for work, and use mobile data for anything highly sensitive. Housekeeping staff, maintenance workers, and previous guests may all have had access to the room, so do not leave sensitive papers lying around, and use the room safe for your laptop and documents when you go out, if the safe is large enough and you trust it. If the safe is small or unreliable, carry your laptop with you rather than leaving it in the room. Be careful with the hotel's own devices. A smart television, a voice assistant, or a phone in the room could record or display information, so avoid discussing confidential work near them, and do not sign into personal accounts on the television. If the room has a wired internet socket, it is often a reasonable option, but it is still a shared hotel network, so a VPN is still wise. Check the door lock when you enter, and use the extra latch or chain at night. When you check out, do a final sweep: check drawers, the safe, power sockets for chargers, and under the bed for anything left behind. Log out of any hotel system you used, and forget the hotel Wi-Fi network on all your devices. A hotel room is a good temporary office when you treat it as a semi-public space rather than a private one. Lock the door, guard your screen, use your VPN, and keep your devices with you, and the room will serve you well without exposing your work.

Hotel rooms also contain small risks that are easy to overlook. The room's telephone, television, and smart devices may be connected to the hotel network, and some have microphones or cameras for voice control. If a device makes you uneasy, you can usually unplug it or cover a camera, though you should be careful not to damage hotel property. Check for forgotten items from previous guests, such as USB sticks left in a television or charger, and do not plug them into your own devices, because a found USB stick is a classic way to spread malware. If you use the hotel's laundry or concierge services, avoid leaving sensitive papers in pockets or bags you hand over. When working late, remember that room service or housekeeping may knock and enter, so lock your screen before opening the door. If you leave the room for dinner, take your laptop or lock it in the safe, and close sensitive documents. A hotel room is a temporary home, and treating it with the same tidy caution you use at home, plus a VPN, is enough to keep your work safe without spoiling your stay.

Section 33: Airport Lounges and Conference Wi-Fi

Airport lounges and conference venues offer Wi-Fi that feels more exclusive than a café's, because access is limited to ticket holders or registered attendees, but that feeling can be misleading. A lounge still serves many strangers, and a conference network may serve thousands of people, including security researchers, journalists, and attackers who attend precisely because the network is rich with interesting targets. At conferences especially, the network is a place where skilled people test things, and not all of them have good intentions. The habits that protect you are the same as elsewhere, applied with extra care. Confirm the network name from official signs or staff, not from a fellow attendee, and be suspicious of a network with a slightly different name. Use a VPN for all work, and avoid banking or account recovery on conference Wi-Fi unless you have no alternative and your VPN is on. Be careful with file sharing and device discovery features, which should be switched off, because a conference network is the worst place to advertise your laptop to strangers. Watch your screen, because conference seating is often dense and people sit close behind you. Keep your badge visible only as required, because a badge may show your name and company, which helps a stranger craft a convincing phishing message later. If you give a talk, be careful about what your slides show, such as internal addresses or credentials in screenshots. When the event ends, forget the network on every device, and review your accounts for odd activity. Lounges and conferences are valuable places to work and meet people, and a VPN, a careful screen, and a tidy forget-the-network habit let you enjoy them without offering your data to the crowd.

Conferences add a further twist: the audience itself is part of the risk, because talks often include live demonstrations on the venue network, and attendees may scan the network out of curiosity or for learning. Some conferences even run capture-the-flag competitions, which are legitimate learning events, but they increase background network activity. If you attend such an event, keep your devices' sharing features off, and avoid joining the competition network with a device that holds sensitive work. Use a separate, clean device for hands-on sessions if you can, or a virtual machine provided by the organisers. Be careful with QR codes on badges and posters, because a code can lead to a lookalike site; type important addresses yourself or use a link from the official programme. Networking is valuable, so exchange contact details through official channels rather than by joining an unknown file-sharing service a stranger suggests. After the conference, review your devices for new apps, profiles, or certificates installed during the event, and remove anything you do not recognise. Conferences are stimulating places, and a little extra caution lets you enjoy them fully.

Section 34: Client Data and Confidential Work

When your work involves other people's data, such as client records, medical details, or financial information, your responsibility grows. You are not only protecting yourself; you are protecting people who trusted your employer with their information. That trust carries legal and professional consequences if the data leaks, so the rules for handling it while travelling are stricter. First, know what you are allowed to carry. Some employers forbid taking certain data outside the office at all, or require it to be accessed only through approved systems that do not store copies on your device. Follow those rules even when they are inconvenient. If you are allowed to work with client data remotely, use only approved devices and approved cloud systems, never personal email or personal drives. Keep the data encrypted, both on your device and in transit, and avoid downloading large sets of it to a travel laptop when you only need a small part. Be careful with conversations: do not discuss client details on a phone call in a public place, and do not leave printed client material where others can see it. If you work on a train or plane, use a privacy filter and avoid opening the most sensitive files in crowded seating. Know your employer's incident process before you travel, so that if a device is lost or a network feels compromised, you can report it immediately rather than searching for a phone number in a panic. Handling other people's data is a privilege, and the habits in this chapter protect that privilege. When in doubt, leave the sensitive file at the office and work on something else until you are back on a trusted network.

Client data rules also vary by industry, and it is worth learning the basics of your own field. Health data, financial data, and children's data are all protected by specific laws in many countries, and breaching those rules can lead to fines and loss of trust, not only embarrassment. If you are unsure whether a file counts as sensitive, treat it as sensitive until someone qualified tells you otherwise. Ask your employer or client what classification a set of data has, and follow the handling rules for that level. Keep a record of where sensitive data lives, so you can answer honestly if asked. When a project ends, return or securely delete client data according to the agreement, rather than keeping a copy just in case. Personal devices that once held client data should be wiped before they are sold or given away. These steps sound bureaucratic, but they are the practical form of respect for the people whose data you handle. A client who trusts you with their information is trusting your habits, your devices, and your judgement, and that trust is one of the most valuable assets a professional can hold.

Section 35: Reporting a Security Incident While Travelling

If something goes wrong while you are travelling, reporting it quickly matters more than fixing it perfectly yourself. A security incident might be a lost device, a password entered on a fake page, a strange login alert, or a file you suspect was exposed. The natural reaction is embarrassment or a hope that it will sort itself out, but both reactions make things worse, because attackers act fast and early action limits damage. Tell your employer's IT or security team as soon as you can, using the contact method they provided, even if you are unsure whether it counts as an incident. Describe what happened in plain words: what you did, when, on which network, and what information might be involved. Do not try to investigate a suspicious network yourself by reconnecting to it, and do not delete evidence such as messages or screenshots, because those help the team understand what happened. If the incident involves a personal account, change the password from a safe connection, turn on multi-factor authentication, and check recent activity for anything you do not recognise. If payment details were involved, contact your bank. Keep a note of who you reported to and when, so you can follow up. A good employer will thank you for reporting early, because early reports save them from larger breaches. Even if you are self-employed, the same principle applies: tell affected clients or services promptly and honestly, and get help from a trusted professional if needed. Reporting is not an admission of failure; it is the responsible action of someone who understands that mistakes happen and that speed limits harm. The worst incident is the one nobody hears about until it is too late.

Reporting is also easier when you prepare the contact details in advance. Before a trip, save your IT help desk's phone number and email address offline, and note the steps for reporting a lost device, such as the device-finding website and your account recovery options. If you travel abroad, check whether your company's reporting line works from another country, and whether there is a local contact. Keep a written note of your device serial numbers, which help support teams identify and lock a stolen device. If you are self-employed, prepare a short list of who to tell in different situations: your bank for payment issues, your clients for data issues, and a trusted technician for device issues. Practising the reporting step, even just by reading it once, makes it far less daunting when you are stressed. Remember that early reporting also protects you personally, because it creates a record that you acted responsibly. The goal is not to assign blame but to limit harm, and every hour of delay gives an attacker more time. Preparation turns reporting from a dreaded task into a simple phone call.

Section 36: Changing Passwords After a Risky Connection

After using a risky connection, changing your passwords is a sensible precaution, but it should be done in the right order and from a safe place. If you think a password was exposed, do not change it while still connected to the suspicious network, because the new password could be exposed the same way. Disconnect, switch to mobile data or a trusted network, and then change it. Start with the most important accounts: your email first, because email is often the key to resetting other accounts, then banking, then work accounts, then social media. Use a strong, unique password for each one, and let a password manager generate and store them so you do not reuse a weak one under stress. Turn on multi-factor authentication for each account as you go, if it is not already on, because that protects the account even if a password leaks again. Check the account's recent activity or login history for sessions you do not recognise, and sign out of all other sessions where the account offers that option. This forces any session an attacker captured to end. Be careful about password reset emails: only follow reset links you requested yourself, and check the sender address carefully, because attackers sometimes send fake reset emails after an incident. If a work account is involved, tell your IT team before or as you change it, because they may want to check for misuse or reset it centrally. Changing passwords after a risky connection is not an admission that something definitely went wrong. It is cheap insurance, and it turns an uncertain worry into a concrete action you have completed. Do it calmly, in order, from a safe connection, and then move on.

Changing passwords also raises the question of which accounts share a password, because a single exposed password may unlock several accounts if you reused it. This is why unique passwords matter so much, and why an incident is a good moment to fix reuse, starting with the most important accounts. Open your password manager and look for accounts marked as reusing a password, then change those one by one, giving each a fresh, unique password. If you do not use a password manager, an incident is the right time to start, because it turns a stressful chore into a lasting improvement. Be patient with yourself: changing many passwords takes time, and it is better to do the important ones well than to rush through all of them badly. Watch for phishing emails in the days after an incident, because attackers sometimes follow up with fake reset messages that prey on your worry. A genuine reset email arrives only after you requested it, and it will not ask you to reply with your password. Take the incident as a lesson, not a verdict on your competence, and let it leave you with stronger, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication everywhere it matters.

Section 37: Checking Your Accounts After Travel

When you return from a trip, a short account check is a wise habit, because travel exposes you to more networks, more strangers, and more chances for something to go wrong unnoticed. Set aside fifteen minutes in the first day or two after you return. Start with your email: look at the login history or recent activity page, and check for logins from places or devices you do not recognise. Do the same for your bank and payment apps, scanning recent transactions for anything small and unfamiliar, because attackers sometimes test a stolen card with a tiny purchase first. Check your social media accounts for posts, messages, or setting changes you did not make. Review your cloud drive's sharing settings and recent file activity, in case a folder was shared or a file was changed while you were away. If anything looks wrong, change that account's password from your home network, turn on multi-factor authentication, and sign out of other sessions. Tell your bank immediately about unfamiliar transactions, and tell your IT team about anything work-related. Even if everything looks fine, the check is worthwhile, because it confirms your habits worked and it teaches you what normal activity looks like, which makes future oddities easier to spot. While you are at it, forget the public networks from your trip, remove temporary apps you installed for travel, and update any device that fell behind on updates. Returning home is a natural moment to tidy up digitally, just as you unpack a suitcase. A short, calm review closes the trip properly and leaves you ready for the next one with clean accounts and good habits intact.

An after-travel check should also include your devices themselves, not only your accounts. Look through recently installed apps for anything you do not recognise, and check your device settings for changes, such as a new VPN profile, a new certificate, or a changed DNS setting that you did not make. These are signs that a network or a download interfered with your device. If you find something suspicious, remove it, update the device, and run a security scan with your trusted antivirus app. Check your browser extensions too, because a malicious extension can survive long after a trip. Review your phone's permissions for apps you installed while travelling, and remove apps you no longer need. Also check your data usage for unexplained spikes, which can indicate background activity you did not approve. If anything remains unclear, ask a trusted technician or your IT team for help, describing what you found. A device check completes the account check, because accounts and devices protect each other. Together they give you a clean, confident start after travel, and they make the next trip's preparation shorter, since you begin from a known-good state.

Section 38: Family Travel: Protecting Everyone's Devices

Travelling with family means protecting several devices at once, including children's tablets and older relatives' phones, and the habits need to be simple enough for everyone to follow. Before the trip, update every device, turn on screen locks, and make sure device-finding features are on. Show children and older relatives how to connect to Wi-Fi safely: ask an adult or staff member for the real network name, and never connect to a random open network just because it is free. Explain in plain words why they should not enter passwords on Wi-Fi login pages, and why a game or video app asking for unusual permissions should be shown to an adult. Set up separate user profiles or parental controls on shared tablets, so a child's game cannot reach a parent's banking app. Agree on a family rule for the trip: use mobile data or the hotel's confirmed network for important things, and ask before downloading new apps on holiday, because holiday boredom is a common reason for risky downloads. Keep chargers and cables in one bag, and use your own chargers rather than public USB ports. If a child loses a device, knowing the finding feature is on and knowing the lock code means you can locate or lock it quickly. Talk about what to do if something feels wrong online: tell an adult, disconnect, and do not keep tapping. These conversations work best before the trip, not during a crisis. Family travel security is not about turning a holiday into a lesson; it is about a few shared rules, set up in advance, that let everyone enjoy the trip while their devices stay reasonably safe. When everyone knows the simple rules, nobody has to be the expert.

Family travel also means planning for lost or damaged devices, which happen more often with children. Before the trip, agree on who carries which device, and keep a note of each device's model and serial number. Teach children never to leave a device on a restaurant table or a beach towel while they play, and to hand it to an adult when they are not using it. If a device is lost, use the finding feature promptly, lock it remotely, and if it cannot be recovered, sign out its accounts from another device and change important passwords. For older relatives, write down the steps for connecting to Wi-Fi and for spotting a suspicious page in large, clear text, and keep that note in the travel bag. Patience matters more than technical detail with older travellers, and a calm explanation repeated once is better than a rushed lecture. Family security is a team effort, and the team works best when everyone knows their part and no one is afraid to ask for help.

Section 39: VPNs and Streaming: What Changes

Many people first meet VPNs through streaming, because a VPN can make it appear as though you are in another country, which sometimes changes which shows a service offers. This is a side effect of how VPNs work: the streaming service sees the VPN server's location instead of yours. It is worth understanding what changes and what does not. A VPN does not give you a legal right to content that is licensed only for another region, and streaming services actively block many VPN servers, so the trick often fails or stops working without warning. Your account is still yours, your payment details are still handled by the service, and the VPN does not make your streaming account more secure in itself. What does change is your privacy from the local network: your viewing habits are hidden from the café Wi-Fi, which is a genuine benefit. Speed may change too, because your traffic takes a longer route through the VPN server, which can cause buffering, especially for high-definition video. If streaming matters to you while travelling, choose a nearby VPN server for speed, and accept that some services will block you or ask for extra verification. Do not install a free VPN purely for streaming, because the privacy cost of an untrustworthy provider outweighs the benefit of a show. Also, be careful with "free streaming" sites that claim to need a VPN: many are piracy sites carrying malware and scams, and a VPN does not make them safe or legal. Streaming through a VPN is a reasonable everyday use, but it is not the reason VPNs exist. Their real value is protecting your traffic on untrusted networks, and streaming is just a visible side effect of that protection.

Streaming through a VPN also interacts with your location-based services in ways worth anticipating. A bank app may flag a login from a VPN server's country as unusual and ask for extra verification, and a shopping site may show prices in another currency. Some news sites and sports services restrict content by region, and a VPN may change what you can watch, sometimes for the better and sometimes for the worse. If you rely on location for maps, weather, or local search results, a distant VPN server can confuse those services too. For everyday use, a server in your own country avoids most of these quirks while still protecting you from the local network. If you travel and want local content, a server in your current location is usually the sensible choice. Remember that streaming services update their VPN blocking regularly, so a server that works today may fail tomorrow, and that is normal rather than a fault in your setup. Do not pay extra for a VPN purely on streaming promises, because those promises change often. Judge a VPN on its privacy, its security features, and its reliability, and treat streaming as a bonus that sometimes works.

Section 40: When a VPN Slows You Down

A VPN almost always slows your connection a little, because encryption takes time and your traffic travels further, through the VPN server. Usually the slowdown is small and unnoticeable, but sometimes it is large enough to cause buffering, slow downloads, or laggy calls. When that happens, there are sensible steps to try before giving up on the VPN. First, try a nearer server. A server in your own country or a neighbouring one is usually much faster than one on the other side of the world. Second, try a different protocol in the app's settings, because some protocols are faster than others on certain networks, and your provider's support pages often recommend one. Third, check whether the slowness is really the VPN's fault by briefly testing your speed with the VPN off on a safe network, such as your home network, and comparing. If the connection is slow even without the VPN, the problem is the local network, not the VPN. Fourth, close bandwidth-heavy apps you do not need, such as cloud backups or video streams running in the background. Fifth, restart the VPN app or your device, which clears temporary problems. If the VPN is still very slow, contact the provider's support, because a faulty server or an outdated app could be the cause. What you should not do is switch the VPN off permanently on public Wi-Fi just for speed, because that trades your privacy for convenience on exactly the network where protection matters most. A small slowdown is the price of encryption, and it is a fair price. Most of the time, a nearer server or a protocol change restores comfortable speed without giving up protection.

Persistent slowness can also be a sign of a deeper problem, such as an overloaded server, a congested local network, or an outdated app. If changing servers and protocols does not help, check whether other people on the same network are also slow, which points to the network rather than your VPN. Try connecting at a different time of day, because public networks are often slowest at peak hours. Make sure your device is not running a large backup or update in the background, which competes for bandwidth. If you use a free VPN tier, slowness may be deliberate, because free tiers are often speed-limited to encourage upgrading; that is a business decision, not a fault, but it is a reason to consider a paid plan from a trusted provider if you rely on a VPN daily. Keep your app updated, because providers regularly improve speed and fix bugs. If slowness continues across networks and servers, contact support with details: your location, the servers you tried, and the speeds you measured. Good support can often suggest a better configuration. Patience and methodical testing solve most speed problems without sacrificing protection.

Section 41: Free Wi-Fi Deals That Cost You Privacy

Some free Wi-Fi is not really free: you pay with your personal information instead of money. A network might ask you to sign in with a social media account, provide your email address, phone number, date of birth, or agree to marketing messages before you can get online. That information can then be used for advertising, sold to data brokers, or kept for far longer than you expect. The venue may be honest about this in its terms, but few people read terms on a phone screen in a busy shop. The question to ask is whether the Wi-Fi is worth the data you are handing over. For a quick map check, probably not. For a long work session, your own mobile data may be a better deal. If you must use such a network, consider what you share. Use an email address you reserve for low-importance sign-ups rather than your main email, and do not connect a social media account, because that can share your friends, likes, and profile details as well. Read the terms briefly, looking for how long data is kept and whether it is shared with third parties, and decline marketing consent where the form allows it. Be careful with networks that ask for your phone number and then send a code, because that number may be used for marketing or linked to your browsing. After using such a network, you can sometimes ask the provider to delete your data, though this is not always easy. The broader lesson is that free services are paid for somehow, and on Wi-Fi the payment is often your attention and your data. Knowing that lets you choose deliberately: sometimes the trade is fair, and sometimes your mobile data is the cheaper option after all.

Paid-for-with-data Wi-Fi also creates a record that can follow you. A venue that collects your email or phone number may link your visits over time, building a picture of when you visit, how long you stay, and which other venues you visit if the same provider runs them. That profile may be used for advertising or shared with partners, and it may persist long after you have forgotten the visit. You can reduce this by using a secondary email address for such sign-ups, by declining marketing consent, and by avoiding social logins, which link far more data than an email alone. Some venues offer a time-limited code on a receipt instead of a sign-up, and that is usually the more private option; choose it when available. If you travel, the same provider may operate networks in many cities, so one sign-up can follow you across a whole trip. Asking for data deletion is your right in many places, though exercising it takes effort and is not always honoured promptly. The practical balance is to treat each sign-up as a small transaction: ask what you gain, what you give, and whether your mobile data would serve just as well. Often it would, and choosing it keeps your visit private.

Section 42: Terms and Tracking on Free Networks

When you accept the terms of a free Wi-Fi network, you may also accept tracking that follows you beyond that visit. Some networks use tracking technology to record which sites you visit, how long you stay, and which shops you walk past afterwards, building a profile of your movements and interests for advertising. Others share your data with partners you have never heard of. This is legal in many places when it is disclosed in the terms, but disclosure in long legal text is not the same as genuine understanding. You can reduce this tracking with habits you already know. Use a VPN, which hides your browsing from the local network. Use your browser's private mode for casual browsing on public networks, though private mode does not hide you from the network itself. Block third-party cookies in your browser settings, and consider a browser extension from a reputable maker that blocks known trackers, checking its permissions carefully. Use a separate email address for Wi-Fi sign-ups, as suggested in the previous section. After leaving, forget the network, so your device does not rejoin and continue to be tracked silently. If you are concerned about a particular provider, you can look for its privacy policy later from home and ask for your data to be deleted. You cannot eliminate all tracking on a network you do not control, and that is an honest limit. What you can do is make tracking harder, less complete, and less linked to your real identity. That is a worthwhile goal. The key insight is that accepting terms is a choice, even when it feels like a formality, and a VPN, careful sign-ups, and forgetting networks afterwards are practical ways to make that choice count in your favour.

Tracking on free networks also includes a technique called device fingerprinting, where a network or website identifies your device by a combination of its characteristics, such as screen size, browser version, and installed fonts, even without a cookie. Fingerprinting is harder to block than cookies, and it can persist across visits. A VPN does not prevent fingerprinting by websites, because it hides your network location, not your device's characteristics. Browser settings that reduce fingerprinting, such as limiting the information your browser shares, can help, and some privacy-focused browsers include such protections by default. Using a common, up-to-date browser also helps, because standing out from the crowd makes fingerprinting easier. Private browsing mode prevents local storage of history and cookies on your device, but it does not stop the network or the website from seeing you during that session. Understanding these limits keeps your expectations honest: no single tool makes you invisible, but layers of tools make you harder to track and less valuable as a target. Combine a VPN, sensible browser settings, careful sign-ups, and forgetting networks, and you have a practical privacy posture that works on real networks without requiring expert knowledge.

Section 43: A Travel Security Kit in Plain Words

A travel security kit is a small set of items and settings that makes safe habits easy while you are away, and putting one together before a trip saves repeated decisions later. The physical items are simple: your own wall charger and cable, a power bank charged at home, a USB data blocker if you ever use public USB ports, and a privacy screen filter for your laptop if you work in public. A small cable lock can help in shared workspaces. The digital items matter just as much: a password manager with strong, unique passwords, multi-factor authentication on your important accounts, a trusted VPN app installed and tested, device-finding features switched on, and automatic updates enabled. Before you leave, run through a checklist: update everything, back everything up, forget old public networks, and download offline maps and documents you will need. Save important numbers offline, including your bank's phone number and your employer's IT help desk, so you can reach them even without internet access. Tell someone you trust where you are going and how to reach you, which is good safety in general, not only online. Pack your kit in the same place every time, so you do not forget an item. The point of a kit is not to carry a bag of gadgets, but to make the safe choice the easy choice. When your own charger, your VPN, and your password manager are all ready before you leave, you will not be tempted by a stranger's cable, an unprotected network, or a reused password in a moment of tiredness. Good travel security is mostly preparation, and a kit turns preparation into a habit you can repeat every trip.

A travel kit should also include a plan for what to do when something is lost or stolen, written down where you can find it without the lost device. Keep a paper note, or a note on a second device, with your bank's phone number, your employer's IT contact, and the web address for locating your devices. Without this note, a stolen phone can leave you unable to look up the very numbers you need. Include a spare charging cable in your kit, because cables fail and hotel shops charge heavily for replacements. A small notebook can be useful too, for writing down network names, confirmation numbers, and incident details without relying on a device. If you travel internationally, add a travel adapter and check that your chargers support the local voltage, which most modern chargers do. Review your kit after each trip and replace anything you used or lost. A kit that is missing its key item is not a kit at all. The discipline of packing the same items, checking them before departure, and restocking afterwards is what makes the kit reliable. Over time, your kit becomes personal: you will know exactly what you need, and packing it will take minutes rather than an evening of worry.

Section 44: Practising Safe Habits Before You Go

Safe habits are easier to build at home than under travel stress, so practising before you go is one of the best investments you can make. Set aside half an hour a few days before departure and walk through the situations you expect. Connect your VPN and use it for an evening, so you learn what its connected state looks like and how to reconnect if it drops. Practise tethering your laptop to your phone's hotspot, so you know the steps and the password without fumbling. Try locking and locating your devices with the finding feature, so you know how it works if a device goes missing. Check that your password manager opens on each device, and that you can sign into your important accounts with multi-factor authentication. If you will work while away, test your work apps through the VPN and confirm that documents open and calls work. Show travelling companions, especially children, how to ask for the real network name and how to spot a suspicious login page. Run a backup and confirm it completed, and download the offline content you will need. This practice session also reveals problems while they are cheap to fix: a forgotten VPN password, an expired app, or a device that will not update can all be sorted at home. By the time you travel, the habits feel familiar rather than new, and familiarity is what survives tiredness, queues, and low batteries. Security advice often fails because it is learned in a crisis, when attention is scarce. Learning it calmly at home, a few days early, is the difference between a habit you actually use and a tip you remember too late.

Practising before travel also helps you discover which of your devices and apps you actually depend on. During a practice evening, you may find that a work app only works on office Wi-Fi, that a password manager needs an update, or that your VPN blocks a service you need. Each discovery is a gift, because it arrives while you have time to ask support or to find an alternative. Practise with the exact devices you will carry, not with a different computer, because settings differ between devices. If you travel with family, practise together, so children learn the rules and adults learn how to help them. Make the practice fun rather than a test: connect the VPN, watch a video through it, and confirm that everything still works. Confidence grows from successful practice, not from reading warnings. If you find a step confusing, write it down in your own words, because your own words are easier to remember under stress than a manual's. Keep that note with your travel kit. A short practice session turns security from a list of advice into a set of actions you have already performed, and performed actions are the ones you repeat when it matters.

Section 45: Teaching Colleagues About Public Wi-Fi

If you work with others, teaching them about public Wi-Fi safety multiplies the benefit, because one colleague's mistake can expose shared systems. Teaching does not require a formal presentation or technical language. Start with the simple ideas from this chapter: public networks are shared with strangers, a VPN protects traffic on them, and mobile data is safer for sensitive tasks. Share a short checklist rather than a long policy document, because people actually use short checklists. Offer to help colleagues set up the company VPN and test it before a trip, and show them how to forget a network afterwards. Use real examples without embarrassing anyone: describe a fake hotspot in general terms, and explain how asking staff for the exact network name defeats it. Encourage reporting, and make clear that reporting a mistake early is praised, not punished, because a culture of blame drives incidents underground. Celebrate good habits when you see them, such as a colleague using a hotspot for a payment task, because positive reinforcement works better than warnings alone. Sharing three clear rules, offering practical help, and supporting early reporting will protect your team far more than a single long training session that everyone forgets by the following week.

Teaching colleagues also means being honest about your own mistakes, because stories of real errors teach better than abstract warnings. If your organisation runs phishing simulations or security training, connect the lessons to travel: a phishing email that arrives while someone is on hotel Wi-Fi is doubly dangerous, because the victim is tired and the network is untrusted. Keep teaching moments short and frequent, such as a two-minute reminder before a team trip, rather than a single annual lecture. People retain small, repeated messages, and they act on advice from a colleague they trust more readily than from a distant policy document. Teaching is therefore not an extra task; it is part of working safely together.

Section 46: Myths About Public Wi-Fi

Several myths about public Wi-Fi cause either unnecessary fear or false confidence, and clearing them up helps you make balanced decisions. The first myth is that all public Wi-Fi is extremely dangerous and must never be used. In reality, with HTTPS websites, updated software, and a VPN for sensitive work, ordinary browsing on public Wi-Fi is reasonably safe. The second myth is the opposite: that public Wi-Fi is completely safe because nothing bad has happened to you yet. Past luck is not protection, and a network you used safely yesterday could be impersonated today. The third myth is that a Wi-Fi password means the network is private. A password shared with everyone in a café, printed on a receipt, protects you no more than an open network against other guests, because anyone with the password can join. The fourth myth is that a VPN makes you anonymous and invincible. It protects your traffic from local watchers, but it does not stop phishing, malware, or a dishonest website, and the VPN provider itself can see your traffic. The fifth myth is that only important people are targeted. Most attacks on public networks are opportunistic, aimed at anyone who happens to connect, not at a chosen victim. The sixth myth is that Apple or Android devices are automatically safe. Good default security helps, but outdated software, weak passwords, and careless habits can undermine any device. The truth sits between fear and carelessness: public Wi-Fi carries real but manageable risks, and a handful of habits manage them well. Believing the myths leads either to avoiding useful networks entirely or to trusting dangerous ones blindly, and neither extreme serves you well.

Another myth is that security software alone can protect you on public Wi-Fi, so habits do not matter. Antivirus and firewalls are valuable, but they cannot stop you from typing a password into a fake login page, connecting to a fake hotspot, or sharing a sensitive file over an unprotected connection. Security is a partnership between tools and behaviour, and neither half works alone. A related myth is that a strong password makes everything safe. Strong passwords matter enormously, but they do not protect traffic on an open network, and they do not help if you are tricked into entering the password on a fake site. Multi-factor authentication helps there, because a stolen password alone is not enough. A further myth is that public Wi-Fi attacks require a skilled hacker in a dark room. In reality, the tools are widely available and the attacks are often automated, which is why opportunistic protection matters. Finally, some people believe that turning off Wi-Fi entirely is the only safe option, but that sacrifices a useful convenience for little gain, since mobile data and VPNs already manage the risk well. Balanced habits beat both extremes.

Section 47: Public Wi-Fi and Children

Children use public Wi-Fi in cafés, hotels, schools, and friends' houses, and they need guidance suited to their age, not just adult rules repeated loudly. Young children should use devices with parental controls and content filters switched on, and should only join networks an adult has approved. Explain the rule simply: ask a grown-up before connecting to any new Wi-Fi, and never type a password into a Wi-Fi page. Older children can learn more: why a network name should be checked, why personal details should not be entered on login pages, and why a stranger offering free internet or a free game download should be treated with suspicion. Set up their devices with the same basics as yours: updates on, screen lock on, and app downloads only from the official store with an adult's approval. Talk about what to do if something feels wrong: stop, disconnect, and tell an adult, without fear of punishment. Children are often afraid to report because they think they will lose the device, so make clear that telling you is always the right move. Be careful with location sharing on children's devices, and review which apps can see their location. On family trips, agree on screen time and Wi-Fi rules in advance, so the rules do not feel like a sudden punishment. Model the behaviour yourself: if children see you checking a network name and using a VPN, they learn that caution is normal, not paranoid. The goal is not to frighten children about the internet, but to give them a few clear rules and a trusted adult to turn to. With those in place, public Wi-Fi can be a useful part of family life rather than a source of worry.

Children's use of public Wi-Fi also includes school and library networks, which are usually better managed than café networks but still shared with many users. Teach children to follow the school's acceptable use rules, to keep passwords private even from friends, and to tell a teacher if a page asks for personal details or shows something uncomfortable. Friends can be a source of risk too: a friend may suggest a free Wi-Fi app, a game download, or a shared account, all of which can carry malware or expose personal data. Encourage children to check with an adult before installing anything or sharing an account, and explain that real friends will not mind waiting. On shared family devices, use separate profiles so a child's browsing does not affect a parent's accounts, and review the device together occasionally, looking at installed apps and permissions as a shared activity rather than a secret inspection. Talk about kindness as well: children should not use a network to harass others or to access someone else's account, even as a joke. Security and kindness overlap here, because both are about respecting other people's privacy. A child who learns both early will carry them into adult life naturally.

Section 48: Remote Work Policies in Plain Words

A remote work security policy is a set of rules an employer writes to keep work safe when staff are away from the office, and understanding it in plain words helps you follow it without guessing. A typical policy covers which devices you may use, which networks are acceptable, and which tools you must use, such as a company VPN and approved cloud storage. It may say that certain systems can only be accessed from the office, or that sensitive data must not be downloaded to personal devices. Add a reporting rule: who you tell, and how quickly, if a device is lost or an account is compromised. Section 49: Building Your Personal Travel Routine

A personal travel routine turns everything in this chapter into a sequence you follow without thinking, which is exactly what you need when you are tired, rushed, or distracted. Build it in three phases: before, during, and after. Before you leave, update and back up your devices, install and test your VPN, download offline maps and documents, and pack your charger, power bank, and data blocker. Everything else is optional polish. A routine that you actually follow beats a perfect routine that you ignore, and a routine that improves after each trip stays useful for years. Safe travel is a skill, and like any skill, it grows with practice and reflection.

Finally, test the routine on a practice evening at home: connect through the VPN, tether your laptop to your phone's hotspot, and locate one of your devices with its finding feature. Keep the routine with your travel kit and refine it after your next real trip.
