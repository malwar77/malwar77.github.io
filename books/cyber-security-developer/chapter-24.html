Chapter 24: Safe Online Payments and Shopping

Section 1: How Online Payments Work in Plain Words

When you pay online, your money does not travel directly from your device to the shop. Instead, a chain of trusted parties moves it for you: the shop's website, a payment gateway that handles the card details securely, the card network that routes the payment, and your bank, which checks that you have the funds and approves the transfer. Each party handles only its own step, and your full card number is usually not stored by the shop itself. Instead, the shop receives a token, a reference that stands for your card without revealing its number, which is safer if the shop is later breached. Understanding this chain helps you spot problems. A genuine checkout involves a recognised payment page, a secure connection, and a confirmation from your bank, such as a text code or an app approval. If a shop asks you to pay by an unusual method, such as a direct bank transfer to a personal account, a gift card, or cryptocurrency, that chain is broken, and you lose the protections that cards and reputable gateways provide. If a checkout page looks different from the shop's usual design, or if it asks for details a normal checkout would not, such as your full bank password, stop. Legitimate payments never ask for your bank password at checkout. Knowing the normal flow, shop, gateway, bank approval, confirmation, lets you recognise when something is abnormal, and abnormal is the moment to pause, check, and, if in doubt, walk away from the purchase.

The payment chain also includes fraud monitoring systems that watch for unusual patterns, such as a sudden purchase in another country, a large amount, or a rapid series of transactions. When a system flags a payment, your bank may text you, call you, or ask you to approve the payment in its app. Those checks are a protection, not an inconvenience, and you should respond to them promptly and honestly. If a payment you did not make is flagged, telling the bank immediately helps it block further misuse. If a genuine payment is flagged, confirming it promptly avoids a declined purchase. Be careful with messages claiming to be fraud alerts: a genuine alert will not ask you to share your full password or to move money to a safe account, and it will direct you to the bank's official app or number. The chain works best when every party does its part: the shop provides a secure checkout, the gateway protects card details, the bank monitors for fraud, and you verify the shop and check your statements. A weak link anywhere, especially a shop that asks for unusual details or a payment method without protection, weakens the whole chain. Knowing the chain helps you spot the weak link before you pay.

Section 2: Cards, Wallets and Bank Transfers

There are several ways to pay online, and each has different safety features. A card payment, whether credit or debit, goes through the card network, which offers fraud protection and, in many places, a right to dispute a charge if goods never arrive or are not as described. A digital wallet, such as a phone's wallet app or an online wallet service, stores your card details securely and lets you pay without typing them on every site, which reduces exposure to fake checkout pages. A direct bank transfer moves money straight from your account to the seller's, usually with little or no fraud protection, because the transfer is treated as authorised by you. That is why scammers love bank transfers: once the money is sent, it is very hard to recover. Cash-like methods, such as gift cards and cryptocurrency, offer even less protection, because they are designed to be irreversible. For everyday shopping, a card or a reputable wallet is usually the safest choice, because both offer dispute routes and fraud monitoring. Keep a separate card or account for online shopping if you can, with a spending limit, so a breach affects only that account. Never pay by bank transfer to a seller you do not know and trust, and never pay by gift card for goods or services, because no genuine shop asks for gift cards as payment. The payment method you choose is itself a safety decision, and choosing one with protection built in is one of the simplest ways to shop safely.

Payment methods also differ in how they handle refunds and disputes in practice, not only in theory. A credit card dispute is usually handled by the card network, with a formal process and a deadline, and the bank may issue a provisional credit while investigating. A wallet dispute goes through the wallet provider first, which may have its own rules and deadlines, and which may require you to contact the seller first. A bank transfer dispute is often limited to cases of fraud or error, and a mistaken payment to the wrong account can be very hard to recover, because the recipient's bank may not be obliged to return it. Understanding these practical differences helps you choose: for a large or uncertain purchase, a method with a formal, well-tested dispute process is worth more than a small saving from paying by transfer. Keep records of the payment method used for each purchase, so a dispute, if needed, starts with clear evidence. If a seller offers a discount for paying by transfer, weigh that discount against the protection you give up. A five percent saving is poor compensation for losing the ability to recover the full payment if the goods never arrive.

Section 3: Recognising a Safe Checkout Page

A safe checkout page has several recognisable features, and learning them helps you shop with confidence. First, the address should use HTTPS and show a padlock, which means the connection is encrypted, as covered in earlier chapters. Second, the address should match the shop you think you are buying from, with no misspellings, extra words, or strange domains. Third, the checkout should be part of the shop's own site or a recognised payment gateway, with branding that matches, rather than a bare page with a single form. Fourth, it should ask only for the details a payment needs: card number, expiry, name, and a security code, plus a billing address where required. It should never ask for your bank password, your full PIN, or a verification code sent to your phone, because those belong to your bank's own approval step, not to the shop. Fifth, it should show the total clearly, including delivery and taxes, before you confirm, with no surprise charges added afterwards. Sixth, it should offer a confirmation page and an email receipt after payment, so you have a record. If any of these features is missing, or if the page pressures you to pay quickly, offers a discount for paying by an unusual method, or redirects you through several unrelated sites, stop and check. A genuine shop wants you to feel confident enough to complete the purchase, and it designs its checkout accordingly. A fake shop wants you to hurry past the warning signs.

A safe checkout page also includes details that are easy to overlook, such as the language and spelling used. A genuine shop's checkout is usually written in clear, professional language, with consistent branding and no spelling mistakes. A fake checkout may contain awkward phrasing, mismatched fonts, or logos that look slightly wrong, because it was copied hastily. Check the page's behaviour too: a genuine checkout validates your card details and shows clear error messages for mistakes, while a fake may accept any input, including an obviously invalid card number, because its goal is to collect details, not to process a payment. Be careful with checkouts that open in a new window or tab with a different address, especially if that address is unrelated to the shop. Some genuine gateways do open separately, but their addresses should still be recognisable payment providers, not random domains. If a checkout asks you to disable a security feature, such as your browser's warning or your bank's approval step, stop immediately, because no genuine checkout needs you to weaken your own protection. Small details, language, validation, and consistent branding, are the signs of a shop that invested in a real checkout, rather than a copied one.

Section 4: HTTPS and Padlocks at Checkout

HTTPS and the padlock icon are especially important at checkout, because that is where you type your most sensitive financial details. HTTPS encrypts the connection between your browser and the website, so your card number cannot easily be read by someone watching the network. Without it, your details travel in the open, and anyone on the same network could capture them. Modern browsers warn loudly about non-HTTPS pages, and you should never enter card details on a page without the padlock. However, as covered earlier, a padlock does not prove a shop is honest. A scam shop can also use HTTPS, because obtaining a certificate is cheap and automatic. The padlock proves only that the connection is encrypted, not that the seller is trustworthy. You still need to check the shop's identity, its reviews, and its reputation, and to prefer payment methods with fraud protection. Another point is that the padlock should appear on the checkout page itself, not only on the shop's homepage. Some shops load their homepage over HTTPS but their checkout over an insecure connection, which is a serious flaw. Check the address bar on the exact page where you type your card details. If your browser shows a warning about a broken or mismatched certificate, do not proceed, even if the shop looks familiar, because the warning may indicate interference. HTTPS is a necessary condition for safe checkout, but it is not a sufficient one. Combine it with shop verification and a protected payment method.

HTTPS at checkout also interacts with your bank's own approval step, which is a separate layer worth understanding. After you enter card details on a secure checkout, your bank may ask you to approve the payment in its app, by fingerprint, or by a one-time code. That step, often called strong customer authentication, exists because card details alone are no longer considered enough. It protects you even if a shop's checkout is compromised, because the attacker would also need your bank approval. Never share that approval code with the shop, a caller, or anyone in a message, because the code is meant only for your bank's own app or page. Be careful with checkout pages that ask you to enter a bank approval code directly on the shop's page, rather than on your bank's page or app, because that is a phishing pattern. A genuine checkout redirects you to your bank or opens your bank's app for approval, and the shop never sees the code. If a payment completes without any bank approval step, that is not necessarily a problem for small amounts, where banks may waive the check, but for a large purchase, the absence of approval deserves a second look. HTTPS plus bank approval is a strong combination.

Section 5: Fake Shops and Lookalike Stores

Fake shops are websites designed to look like genuine retailers, often copying a real shop's design, logo, and product photos, in order to steal your payment details or your money. They usually appear through social media adverts, search results, or messages offering a popular product at a dramatic discount. Once you pay, the goods never arrive, or a counterfeit arrives, and the shop disappears. Spotting a fake shop takes a few checks. First, look at the address: a fake often uses a misspelled brand name, an extra word, or an unusual domain ending. Second, check the shop's contact details: a genuine shop lists a real address, a phone number, and an email, while a fake may list none, or only a contact form. Third, check its reviews elsewhere, not only testimonials on its own site, which can be faked. Fourth, be suspicious of prices far below every other seller, because a genuine shop cannot sell a popular product at a fraction of its normal price and stay in business. Fifth, check the payment options: a fake shop often insists on bank transfer, gift cards, or cryptocurrency, because those cannot be disputed. Sixth, check how long the site has existed, using a domain lookup if you know how, because a shop created days ago selling a famous brand is suspicious. If several of these checks fail, do not buy, however tempting the deal. A real bargain is a small saving from a trusted seller, not a miracle price from a stranger.

Fake shops also exploit search engines and social platforms by buying adverts that appear above genuine results. An advert at the top of a search page is paid placement, not an endorsement, and a fake shop can buy the same position as a genuine one. To protect yourself, do not trust search ranking alone. Click through to the shop, check its address, contact details, and reviews independently, and compare its prices with other sellers. Be especially careful during sales events and holidays, when fake shops multiply and shoppers are in a hurry. Some fake shops also impersonate well-known brands by using their logos and product images, and they may even copy the genuine shop's return policy and about page. The details that betray them are usually small: a domain registered recently, a contact email from a free service, a phone number that never answers, and a payment page that only accepts irreversible methods. If you discover a fake shop after paying, report it promptly, as covered later, and contact your bank, because early reporting improves recovery. Fake shops thrive on speed and trust in familiar branding. Slowing down and checking independently breaks both advantages.

Section 6: Too-Good-To-Be-True Deals

A deal that is far cheaper than everywhere else should trigger scepticism, not excitement, because it is one of the commonest lures in online shopping scams. Scammers know that a dramatic discount short-circuits careful thinking: the fear of missing out pushes people to pay before checking the seller. Genuine discounts exist, especially during sales, but they are usually modest, from a known retailer, and consistent with the product's normal price range. A new phone at a tenth of its price, a designer bag at a twentieth, or a holiday at a fifth of the usual cost are not bargains; they are bait. The goods, if they arrive at all, are usually counterfeit, broken, or nothing at all. To judge a deal, compare the price across several reputable sellers, and check the product's normal price from the manufacturer. If the deal's price is wildly out of line, walk away. Be especially careful with deals that arrive by social media advert, message, or email, rather than from a shop you sought out yourself, because those channels are where fake deals spread. Be careful with countdown timers and claims that only one item remains, because those are pressure tactics designed to stop you checking. A genuine seller will still be there tomorrow, and a genuine deal will survive a ten-minute check. If you miss a real bargain by checking, you lose a small saving. If you fall for a fake bargain by not checking, you lose the whole payment.

Too-good-to-be-true deals also appear in a subtler form: a genuine product at a plausible discount, but from a seller who adds hidden costs, ships a different model, or charges for a subscription you did not agree to. The headline price is attractive, but the total cost, including delivery, taxes, and unwanted extras, may exceed a reputable seller's price. Always check the total before confirming, including delivery and any subscription or warranty added by default. Be careful with pre-ticked boxes at checkout, which may add insurance, warranties, or memberships you do not want. Untick anything you did not choose, and read the order summary carefully before paying. A deal that requires you to act within minutes, to share the offer with friends, or to pay by an unusual method is using pressure, not value, to close the sale. Genuine sellers offer clear totals, optional extras that are genuinely optional, and time to think. If a deal's total is unclear, or if extras appear that you did not choose, abandon the purchase and buy from a seller whose checkout is transparent. A small saving is not worth a confusing checkout, because confusion is where unwanted charges hide.

A genuine seller will still be there tomorrow, and a genuine deal will survive a ten-minute check before you commit any money.

Section 7: Reading Reviews Before You Buy

Reviews can help you judge a shop and a product, but they need to be read with the same care as app reviews. Start with reviews on independent sites, not only testimonials on the shop's own website, which the shop controls and can fake. Look for a pattern across many reviews: consistent complaints about non-delivery, counterfeit goods, or poor refunds are a strong warning, even if the average rating looks acceptable. Read recent reviews, because a shop's behaviour can change, and a shop that was good last year may have been sold or may have turned to fraud. Be suspicious of reviews that are vague, overly enthusiastic, and posted in a burst, because fake reviews are often bought that way. Check whether the reviewer actually bought the product, where the platform marks verified purchases, and give those reviews more weight. Look at how the shop responds to negative reviews: a shop that answers helpfully and resolves problems is more trustworthy than one that ignores complaints or attacks reviewers. For products, check reviews that mention durability and real use over time, not only first impressions. Reviews are not proof, and they can be manipulated, but a shop with many detailed, verified, recent, positive reviews, and thoughtful responses to criticism, is a safer bet than a shop with no independent reviews at all. Ten minutes of reading before a significant purchase is time well spent.

Reviews also need to be judged for the reviewer's perspective, because a review that is helpful for one buyer may be misleading for another. A reviewer who bought a budget product and expected premium quality may rate it poorly for reasons that do not apply to you, while a reviewer who received a free product may rate it generously. Look for reviews that describe actual use over time, specific features, and comparisons with similar products, because those are more informative than star ratings alone. Check whether the platform verifies that the reviewer bought the product, and whether it marks reviews from incentivised reviewers. Be careful with review sites that rank products by affiliate commission, because their rankings may reflect payment rather than quality. Cross-check a product's reviews across more than one site, because a product with consistent praise across independent sources is more trustworthy than one praised only on the seller's own page. If reviews are polarised, with many five-star and many one-star ratings, read both extremes to understand the disagreement: it may reflect different expectations, different batches, or a genuine quality problem. Reviews are a tool for judgement, not a verdict, and reading them thoughtfully is what makes them useful.

Ten minutes of reading before a significant purchase is time well spent, and it often reveals problems a star rating alone would hide.

Section 8: Seller Reputation Checks

Checking a seller's reputation before buying is a habit that protects you across every kind of online shopping, from large retailers to individual marketplace sellers. For a retailer, check how long it has traded, whether it has a real address and contact details, and whether it appears in independent review sites and consumer forums. For a marketplace seller, check their rating, their number of completed sales, and their recent feedback, paying attention to comments about delivery speed, product accuracy, and problem resolution. A seller with thousands of sales and consistently positive feedback over years is a safer bet than a new seller with a perfect score from a handful of sales, because a new account's score is easy to fake. Check whether the seller's profile shows verified identity or business details, where the platform offers them. Be careful with sellers who only accept payment outside the marketplace's own system, because that removes the platform's protection, which is often the main reason to use a marketplace at all. Be careful with sellers who pressure you to complete a deal quickly or to move conversation off the platform, because that is a common scam pattern. If a seller's reputation cannot be checked, because they are new, anonymous, or only reachable through a message, treat the purchase as high risk and either walk away or use the most protected payment method available. Reputation is built over time, and a seller who has built it has something to lose, which is exactly why it matters.

Seller reputation also includes how a seller handles problems, which is often more revealing than how they handle successful sales. A seller who responds promptly, offers a fair solution, and follows through is trustworthy, even if a problem occurred. A seller who ignores messages, blames the buyer, or disappears when a problem arises is not, however perfect their sales record looks. When checking feedback, look specifically for comments about problem resolution, and for the seller's responses to negative feedback. Check whether the seller's rating has dropped recently, which can indicate a change in ownership or a decline in standards. For individual sellers, check whether they sell a consistent range of goods, which suggests a genuine trader, or a random assortment of high-value items, which can indicate a reseller of stolen or counterfeit goods. Be careful with sellers who have recently changed their username or shop name, because that can be a way to escape a bad reputation. Reputation is a record of behaviour over time, and behaviour under pressure is the most honest part of that record. A seller who treats problems well has earned trust that a perfect score alone cannot convey.

Section 9: Payment Methods Compared for Safety

Comparing payment methods by safety helps you choose the right one for each purchase. Credit cards usually offer the strongest protection, because the money is the bank's until you pay the bill, and card networks provide dispute rights for goods not received or not as described. Debit cards offer similar dispute rights in many places, but the money leaves your account immediately, so recovering it can take longer and leave you short in the meantime. Digital wallets add a layer, because the shop never sees your card number, and the wallet provider offers its own fraud monitoring and dispute process. Bank transfers offer the least protection, because they are treated as authorised by you and are hard to reverse. Cash-like methods, such as gift cards, cryptocurrency, and money transfer services to strangers, offer almost no protection and should never be used for shopping with an unknown seller. Payment services that hold funds until delivery, such as marketplace escrow or buyer protection programmes, add safety for marketplace purchases, because the seller is not paid until you confirm receipt. For high-value purchases, a credit card through a reputable gateway is usually the safest combination. For small, everyday purchases from trusted shops, a debit card or wallet is fine. For any purchase from an unknown seller, choose the method with the strongest dispute rights, and avoid methods that cannot be reversed. The right payment method turns a risky purchase into a recoverable one.

Payment methods also include newer options, such as pay-by-bank services, which let you approve a payment directly from your bank app without entering card details. These services can be secure, because they use your bank's own authentication and do not share card numbers with the shop, but they vary in dispute protection, so check the terms before using one for a large purchase. Some pay-by-bank services offer purchase protection similar to cards, while others offer little more than a transfer. Similarly, some wallet services offer buyer protection for goods, while others are designed for sending money to friends and offer no protection for purchases. The distinction matters: sending money to a friend is not the same as paying a shop, and using a friends-and-family option for a purchase can void your protection. Always choose the goods-and-services option when paying a seller, even if it costs a small fee, because that fee buys dispute rights. If a seller asks you to use a friends-and-family payment to avoid a fee, that request itself is a warning sign. Payment methods are tools with different guarantees, and choosing the right tool for a purchase, not merely the cheapest or fastest, is a core shopping skill.

Section 10: Credit Cards vs Debit Cards Online

Credit and debit cards look similar, but they behave differently when something goes wrong, and that difference matters for online shopping. With a credit card, you spend the bank's money up to a limit, and you repay it later. If a fraudulent charge appears, the bank's money is at stake while the dispute is resolved, and your own account is untouched. With a debit card, the money leaves your bank account immediately, so a fraudulent charge can leave you short of funds for rent, bills, or food while you wait for a refund. That practical difference makes credit cards safer for online shopping, especially for larger purchases or purchases from sellers you do not know well. Credit cards also often include additional benefits, such as extended warranties, purchase protection, and rewards, though those vary by card. Debit cards have their place: they prevent debt, because you cannot spend money you do not have, and they are fine for small purchases from trusted shops. A sensible approach is to use a credit card for online shopping, pay the balance in full each month to avoid interest, and keep a debit card for cash and everyday spending. If you do not have or want a credit card, consider a separate debit account for online shopping, funded with only the amount you need, so a breach cannot drain your main account. Whichever card you use, turn on transaction alerts, check statements regularly, and report unfamiliar charges promptly. The card you choose is part of your shopping safety, not just a payment detail.

Credit and debit cards also differ in the additional protections that may come with them, such as purchase protection, extended warranties, and travel insurance, which vary by card and by country. Check your card's benefits guide, which is usually available from your bank's app or website, and understand what is covered before you need it. Some cards extend a manufacturer's warranty by a year, cover accidental damage for a period, or insure a trip paid for with the card. Those benefits can be valuable, but they usually require you to have paid with that specific card and to keep receipts and documentation. Be careful with cards that charge high interest or annual fees for benefits you do not use; a simple card with strong fraud protection and no fee may serve you better than a premium card whose benefits you never claim. If you carry both a credit and a debit card, decide in advance which to use for online shopping, and stick to that rule, so your statements are easier to review and your exposure is limited to one account. The card you choose, and how you use it, is a financial decision as well as a security one, and a card chosen deliberately serves both goals.

Section 11: Virtual Cards and Spending Limits

A virtual card is a card number generated for online use, often for a single purchase or a single retailer, with a spending limit and sometimes an expiry date you control. It protects your real card number, because the shop never sees it, and if the virtual number is stolen, it cannot be used beyond its limit or for other retailers. Many banks and wallet services now offer virtual cards for free, and they are one of the best tools for safe online shopping. Use a virtual card for a purchase from a shop you are trying for the first time, for a subscription you may want to cancel, or for any site where you are unsure about its security. Set the limit to the purchase amount, so even a breach cannot overspend. Spending limits on your real cards are also useful: a daily or per-transaction limit on a card used for online shopping reduces the damage from a single fraudulent charge. Check your bank's app for limit settings, and set them to levels that suit your normal shopping. Virtual cards do not replace other protections, such as checking the seller and using HTTPS, but they add a powerful layer: they make your card number disposable. A stolen disposable number is a minor inconvenience, while a stolen real number can affect every subscription and payment linked to it. If your bank offers virtual cards, learn to create one before your next online purchase, because a few taps at checkout can save hours of cancelling and reissuing a compromised card later.

Virtual cards also help with subscriptions, because a virtual card set for a single retailer or a low limit can prevent an unwanted subscription from charging more than you agreed. If a trial converts to a subscription you do not want, a virtual card with a low limit or a short expiry can limit the damage while you cancel. Some virtual card services let you pause or delete a virtual number instantly, which is a powerful control: deleting the number stops future charges from that retailer without affecting your real card or other subscriptions. That control is useful, but it should not replace cancelling the subscription itself, because the retailer may still consider you subscribed and may pursue payment. Use virtual cards as a safety net, not as a substitute for managing subscriptions. Check whether your bank's virtual cards work for recurring payments, because some are designed only for one-time use and will decline a subscription renewal, which can cause a service interruption. For one-time purchases from new or uncertain shops, a one-time virtual card is ideal. For regular subscriptions to trusted services, a dedicated virtual card with a limit matching the subscription is a good balance. Virtual cards turn a single, precious card number into many disposable ones, and that disposability is a profound safety improvement for online shopping.

Section 12: Digital Wallets in Plain Words

A digital wallet is an app or service that stores your payment cards securely and lets you pay without typing your card number on every website. When you pay with a wallet, the shop receives a token, a stand-in for your card, rather than your real number, which means a breach at the shop does not expose your card. Wallets also usually require a biometric check or a PIN on your device before paying, which adds a layer that a typed card number lacks. Popular wallets include phone wallets built into your device and online wallet services that work across websites. To use a wallet safely, protect the wallet account itself with a strong, unique password and multi-factor authentication, because the wallet is now the key to all your stored cards. Keep your device locked and updated, because a wallet on an unlocked, outdated phone is less safe. Check which cards are stored in your wallet regularly, and remove cards you no longer use. Be careful with wallet features that store loyalty cards, tickets, and IDs alongside payment cards, because a wallet that holds everything is a richer target if the device is stolen. If your device is lost, you can usually suspend wallet cards remotely from another device, and you should do so promptly. Wallets are convenient, and their token system makes them safer than typing card numbers on many sites. That safety depends on the wallet account and the device being protected, so guard both as carefully as you guard the cards themselves.

Digital wallets also raise a question about what happens when you change phones or lose access to your wallet account. Wallet cards are usually tied to your device and your wallet account, and moving to a new phone requires re-adding cards, which is a good security feature, because a stolen phone's wallet cannot simply be copied to another device. Keep a record of which cards are in your wallet, so you can re-add them promptly and notice if one is missing. If you lose your wallet account password, recovery can take time, and during that time you may be unable to pay with the wallet, so keep a physical card as a backup. Be careful with wallet features that automatically add loyalty cards, tickets, and boarding passes, because a wallet full of personal items is a richer target. Review the wallet's transaction history regularly, because it shows payments across all your stored cards in one place, making it easier to spot unfamiliar charges. If a wallet payment is disputed, the wallet provider may direct you to your bank, and your bank may direct you to the wallet, so keep records of both sides of the conversation. Wallets are convenient, but they are also a single point of failure, and a backup payment method and a record of your cards are wise precautions.

Section 13: Buy Now Pay Later Risks

Buy now, pay later services let you split a purchase into several payments over weeks or months, often with no interest if you pay on time. They are convenient, but they carry risks that are easy to underestimate. First, they make spending feel smaller than it is, because a large price is presented as a few small payments, which can lead to buying more than you can afford. Second, missed payments can incur fees and damage your credit record, even though the service felt casual at checkout. Third, each service is a separate account with your personal and financial details, increasing your exposure if any one of them is breached. Fourth, returns become more complicated, because you must coordinate the refund with both the shop and the pay-later service, and payments may continue while the dispute is resolved. Fifth, some pay-later services report to credit agencies, so heavy use can affect your ability to borrow later. To use these services safely, treat them like credit, because that is what they are: only buy what you could afford to pay for in full, keep track of all active plans in one place, set reminders for payment dates, and avoid stacking several plans at once. Read the terms for fees, late charges, and credit reporting before agreeing. If a shop offers pay-later as the only payment method, or pressures you to use it for a discount, be suspicious. Pay-later can be a useful tool for a planned purchase, but it should never turn a want into a debt you did not plan for.

Buy now, pay later services also affect your credit record in ways that are not always clear at checkout. Some services perform a soft credit check that does not affect your score, while others perform a hard check or report your payment behaviour to credit agencies. Missed payments may be reported, and multiple active plans can signal to lenders that you rely on short-term credit. Before using a pay-later service, check its credit reporting policy in its terms, and consider whether the purchase is worth a potential mark on your record. Keep a single list of all active pay-later plans, with amounts and due dates, because plans from different providers do not appear together in one place, making it easy to lose track. Set up payment reminders or automatic payments from an account you monitor, so a missed date does not become a fee and a credit mark. If you struggle to keep up with pay-later payments, contact the providers early, because many offer hardship options, and prioritise essential bills over discretionary purchases. Pay-later is credit, however casually it is presented at checkout, and treating it with the same seriousness as a loan, including checking its credit effects, is the habit that keeps it useful rather than harmful.

Section 14: Subscriptions and Free Trials

Subscriptions and free trials are a common source of surprise charges, because they convert silently from free to paid and then renew automatically. A free trial usually requires your card details upfront, and if you forget to cancel before the trial ends, the first payment is taken without further warning. After that, monthly charges continue until you actively cancel, and some services make cancellation hard to find. To manage subscriptions, keep a list of every subscription you hold, with its cost, renewal date, and cancellation method. Your phone's subscription settings and your bank's app often show many of them in one place, and reviewing that list every few months catches forgotten ones. Before starting a free trial, set a reminder a day before it ends, and decide in advance whether you will keep it. Read the trial terms for how cancellation works and whether the trial converts automatically. Be careful with trials that ask for payment details for a service you only want to test briefly, and consider using a virtual card with a low limit for trials, so an unwanted conversion cannot charge much. Teach children and older relatives about trials too, because a free game or app trial can become a monthly charge on a family account. Subscriptions are legitimate, and many services are worth their price, but they should be conscious choices, renewed deliberately, not forgotten charges that drain your account month after month. A list and a reminder turn a trap into a tool.

Subscriptions also include services that bundle many features, where you may pay for a bundle but use only one part. Review bundles annually and ask whether each part is still worth its share of the cost. Be careful with subscriptions that offer a discounted annual payment, because paying yearly saves money only if you use the service all year, and a yearly charge is harder to notice than a monthly one. Check whether a subscription can be paused rather than cancelled, which is useful for seasonal services. Teach family members to check their own subscriptions, because a child's game subscription or an older relative's magazine subscription can renew unnoticed on a shared account. If a subscription is hard to cancel, document your cancellation attempts, including screenshots and confirmation emails, because that evidence helps in a dispute. Some consumer protection rules now require clearer cancellation paths, and reporting a service that hides cancellation helps enforce those rules. Subscriptions are a convenient way to pay for ongoing services, but they should be reviewed like any recurring bill, with a list, a renewal calendar, and a willingness to cancel what you no longer value. A subscription you chose and still use is a good deal; one you forgot is a leak.

Section 15: Saving Cards on Websites: Yes or No?

Saving your card on a website makes future purchases faster, because you do not need to type the number again, but it also means the website, or its payment provider, holds your card details for the future. That stored card is a target: if the site is breached, your card may be exposed, and if your account on that site is hijacked, an attacker can spend with the saved card. Whether to save a card depends on the site and on your habits. For a large, reputable retailer you use often, with strong account security, saving a card is a reasonable convenience, provided your account uses a strong, unique password and multi-factor authentication. For a small shop you use once, a site with weak security, or any site where you do not need to return, do not save the card. Enter it fresh each time, or use a wallet or virtual card instead. Review saved cards in your shopping accounts occasionally, removing ones you no longer need, and check your bank statements for charges from sites where you saved a card long ago. Be careful with the "remember me" option at checkout, which may save both your login and your card, making a hijacked account more dangerous. If a site offers to save your card, it should also offer a clear way to delete it, and you should test that deletion works. Convenience and safety are in tension here, and the balance should favour safety for sites you rarely use, and convenience only for sites you trust deeply and use often.

Saving cards on websites also interacts with account security in a way worth noting: a saved card is only as safe as the account that holds it. If your shopping account uses a weak or reused password and no second factor, an attacker who guesses that password gains not only your address and order history, but also the ability to spend with your saved card. That is why saving a card should always be paired with the strongest account protection available. Some sites offer an extra check for purchases with a saved card, such as a code or a biometric confirmation, and enabling that check is wise. Be careful with sites that allow one-click purchasing with a saved card and no further check, because that convenience also means a hijacked session can spend instantly. Review saved cards after a data breach notification from a site, and remove the card if you are unsure the site protected it. Consider using a wallet or virtual card for sites where you shop occasionally, so the site never holds your real card at all. Saved cards are a convenience that concentrates risk in one account, and that concentration should be matched by equally concentrated protection: a unique password, a second factor, and regular review.

Section 16: Autofill and Payment Safety

Autofill is the feature that completes your address, card number, and other details automatically when you check out, and it is both a convenience and a risk. On a genuine site, autofill saves time and reduces typing errors, including errors that could send a payment to the wrong place. On a fake site, autofill can be tricked into filling sensitive details into hidden fields you cannot see, exposing your card number and address to a scammer. Modern browsers have improved autofill safety, but the risk has not disappeared. To use autofill safely, keep your browser updated, and only allow autofill on sites you trust. Check what your browser stores: it may hold addresses, card numbers, and passwords, and each stored item should be protected by your device's lock and your browser's own security. Use your password manager's autofill where possible, because a good manager only fills details on the genuine website it saved, not on a lookalike, which is a powerful phishing defence. Be careful with autofill on a shared device, where another user could trigger it and see your details. Turn off autofill for payment cards if you share a device, and prefer typing the card number fresh or using a wallet. After using autofill on a public or shared device, clear the browser's stored data if you can. Autofill is a tool that trades a little safety for a lot of convenience, and that trade is reasonable on your own, trusted device, with a good password manager, but not on a device or site you do not fully trust.

Autofill safety also depends on the device you use. On your own, locked, updated phone or computer, autofill from a reputable password manager is safe and convenient. On a shared computer, a public device, or a device you do not control, autofill can expose your details to the next user, because the browser may fill them without asking for your device lock. Some browsers require a device password or biometric check before filling payment details, and enabling that check is a strong protection. Check your browser's autofill settings for what is stored, and delete addresses and cards you no longer need. Be careful with autofill for addresses, because an address filled into a fake site reveals where you live, which can be used for further scams. If you use autofill, pair it with a habit of checking the filled fields before submitting, because autofill can occasionally fill the wrong address or an old card. A quick glance at the filled form catches those errors. Autofill is a tool that should serve you, not surprise you, and a few settings, a device lock check before filling, a regular review of stored data, and a glance at the form before submitting, keep it that way.

Section 17: Public Wi-Fi and Shopping

Shopping on public Wi-Fi deserves extra caution, because the network is shared with strangers, as covered in the public Wi-Fi chapter. A safe checkout over HTTPS is encrypted, so ordinary browsing and buying on a reputable site over public Wi-Fi is reasonably safe, but the risks are higher than on a trusted network, and the safest habits still apply. Prefer mobile data for purchases, especially for high-value items or for entering card details for the first time on a site. If you must shop on public Wi-Fi, use a trusted VPN, confirm you are on the genuine network, and avoid saving a new card on a site while on that network. Be careful with checkout pages that show certificate warnings or that redirect unexpectedly, because those can indicate interference. Do not shop on a public computer or a shared device, where your card details could be recorded. After shopping on public Wi-Fi, check your bank statement for the charge and for any unfamiliar activity, and change the password of any shopping account you signed into if the network felt suspicious. Keep your shopping apps and browser updated, because updates fix flaws that could be exploited on an open network. Public Wi-Fi is convenient for browsing and comparing prices, and that low-risk use is fine. For the moment of payment itself, a trusted network, mobile data, or a VPN is worth the small effort, because payment is the moment your financial details are most exposed.

Shopping on public Wi-Fi also includes the apps you use, not only the browser. A shopping app's traffic may be encrypted, but older or poorly made apps may send data unencrypted, which is riskier on a shared network. Keep shopping apps updated, and prefer apps from reputable retailers with strong security practices. If a shopping app shows a warning about its connection, or if it behaves oddly on public Wi-Fi, stop and switch to mobile data. Be careful with shopping through a social media app's built-in browser, which may have weaker protections than your main browser, and which may not show the full address clearly. Prefer opening a shop in your main browser or its official app, where you can verify the address and the checkout. After shopping on public Wi-Fi, check your order confirmation and bank statement promptly, because early detection of a problem improves recovery. If you shop frequently while travelling, consider a VPN subscription from a trusted provider, as covered in earlier chapters, so your shopping traffic is protected on every network you join. Public Wi-Fi shopping is not forbidden, but it deserves the same layered approach as any sensitive task on an untrusted network: trusted app or browser, encrypted checkout, VPN where possible, and prompt verification afterwards.

Section 18: Shopping on a Shared Device

Shopping on a shared device, such as a family tablet, a friend's phone, or a public computer, carries risks that your own device does not. A shared device may remember your login, your card details, and your address after you finish, allowing the next user to shop with your accounts. Browsers on shared devices may save passwords and autofill data, and shopping apps may stay signed in. The rule is simple: avoid shopping on a shared device whenever you can, and if you must, take precautions. Use a private browsing window, do not let the browser save your password or card, and sign out of every shopping account when you finish, closing the browser afterwards. Do not save a card on a site from a shared device, and prefer a payment method that requires an extra approval, such as a wallet confirmation on your own phone, if that is available. Clear the browser's history and stored data if the option is available, though this does not remove malware. Never shop on a public computer for high-value items, because you cannot know what software it runs. For family devices, use separate profiles where possible, so each person's shopping accounts and payment methods stay separate, and require a password for every purchase. After using a shared device, check your shopping accounts and bank statements for unfamiliar orders, and change any password you entered if you are unsure the device was clean. Your own device is almost always safer for shopping than a shared one.

Shared devices also include smart televisions and voice assistants, which can now shop by voice or by remote control. A voice assistant that can order goods without a confirmation code is a risk, especially in a household with children or visitors, because a spoken request, a television advert, or a child's curiosity can trigger a purchase. Disable voice purchasing, or require a confirmation code for every order, in your assistant's settings. Be careful with televisions that stay signed into shopping accounts, because anyone with the remote can browse and buy. Sign out of shopping apps on shared screens, and require a PIN for purchases. For family tablets, use separate profiles and purchase approvals, as covered earlier, so a child's game or video cannot lead to a purchase on a parent's account. After a guest uses your device, check your shopping accounts for unfamiliar orders, and sign out of any account you signed into for them. Shared screens are convenient for browsing, but purchasing should be a deliberate act on your own device, with your own authentication, not a casual tap on a shared screen. That separation keeps family shopping enjoyable without turning every screen in the house into a shop till.

Section 19: Account Takeover and Shopping Accounts

Account takeover happens when an attacker gains access to your shopping account, usually by stealing your password through phishing, a data breach, or password reuse, and then uses it to order goods, change your address, or steal stored payment details. Shopping accounts are attractive targets because they often hold saved cards, addresses, and order history, and because orders placed through them may not trigger the same warnings as a direct bank transfer. Protecting shopping accounts starts with the basics: a strong, unique password for each shopping site, multi-factor authentication where offered, and transaction alerts from your bank. Be careful with password reuse, because a breach at one shop can expose the password you used at many others. Check your shopping accounts' order history and address settings occasionally for changes you did not make, such as a new delivery address added by an attacker. Sign out of shopping accounts on devices you no longer use, and remove saved cards from accounts you no longer need. If you receive an order confirmation for something you did not buy, act immediately: contact the shop, cancel the order if possible, change your password, and tell your bank. If a shop notifies you of a password reset you did not request, treat it as a warning and secure the account at once. Shopping accounts feel low-risk compared with banking, but they are gateways to your money and your address, and they deserve the same care as any financial account.

Account takeover also involves the recovery process, which attackers exploit. If an attacker gains access to your email, they can reset passwords for shopping accounts, change delivery addresses, and order goods. That is why email security is the foundation of shopping security: a strong, unique email password and multi-factor authentication protect every account linked to that email. Review your email account's recovery options, and ensure they do not depend on a phone number or an alternate email you no longer control. Be careful with password reset emails: only follow a reset link you requested yourself, and check the sender address, because attackers send fake reset emails to steal credentials. If a shopping site offers login alerts, turn them on, so you learn about a new device signing in promptly. If you receive a login alert for a device you do not recognise, change the password immediately and sign out of all sessions. Account takeover is rarely about a single weak password; it is about a chain of linked accounts, with email at the centre. Securing email, with a unique password and a second factor, protects the whole chain, including every shopping account that depends on it.

Section 20: Order Confirmations and Receipts

Order confirmations and receipts are more than paperwork; they are your evidence if something goes wrong, and they help you spot fraud early. After every purchase, you should receive a confirmation email showing what you ordered, the total charged, the delivery address, and an order number. Save that email until the goods arrive and you are satisfied with them, because it is the record you will need for a return, a refund, or a dispute. Check the confirmation carefully: does the total match what you agreed, is the delivery address yours, and is the item the one you ordered? A mismatch can indicate a mistake, a hijacked account, or a fake shop. Be careful with confirmation emails themselves, because scammers send fake confirmations for orders you never placed, hoping you will click a link to cancel or to view details, which leads to a phishing site. If you receive a confirmation for an order you did not place, do not click its links; instead, open the shop's official app or website yourself and check your order history, and contact the shop and your bank if an unknown order is real. Keep receipts for high-value purchases for longer, and note the shop's return deadline, because missing it can cost you a refund. A tidy habit of saving confirmations until delivery, and checking them promptly, turns a routine email into an early warning system for fraud and mistakes.

Order confirmations also serve as a record for warranties and returns, which is why keeping them matters beyond delivery. A confirmation email usually includes the product's serial number or order reference, the seller's details, and the purchase date, all of which a warranty claim or a return may require. Store confirmations for high-value items, such as electronics and appliances, for the warranty period, in a folder or a note you can find later. Be careful with confirmation emails that contain tracking links, because scammers send fake confirmations with malicious links, as covered earlier. Always verify a tracking link by opening the delivery company's site yourself, rather than tapping the email's link, if anything looks odd. If a confirmation email's details do not match your order, contact the seller promptly, because a mismatch can indicate a mistake, a hijacked account, or a fake shop. Keep a simple spreadsheet or note of major purchases, with dates, sellers, amounts, and warranty ends, so you can find a record quickly when you need it. Confirmations are not clutter to be deleted on arrival; they are the paper trail that proves what you bought, from whom, and for how much, and that trail is valuable long after the parcel is opened.

Section 21: Delivery Scams and Tracking Links

Delivery scams exploit the excitement and uncertainty of waiting for a parcel. A typical scam message claims a parcel is held, a delivery failed, or a fee is due, and includes a link to reschedule or to pay. The link leads to a fake delivery site that steals your personal details or payment information, or it triggers a harmful download. Genuine delivery companies do send tracking messages, but they rarely ask you to pay an unexpected fee through a link in a text, and they never ask for your full bank password. The defence is to verify independently: open the delivery company's official app or website yourself, enter the tracking number from your original order confirmation, and check the parcel's status there. Do not tap the link in an unexpected message, even if it mentions a parcel you are actually expecting, because scammers time their messages to coincide with real deliveries. Look for warning signs: a sender number that does not match the company, a web address that is slightly wrong, spelling mistakes, and pressure to act immediately. If a fee is genuinely due, such as customs duty, the delivery company will explain it through official channels and offer a verifiable payment method. Report delivery scam messages to your provider and to the impersonated company, and delete them. Waiting for a parcel is a moment of vulnerability, because you want the parcel to arrive, and scammers exploit that want. A pause to check through the official channel defeats the trick.

Delivery scams also evolve with technology, including fake delivery apps and fake tracking websites that mimic genuine ones closely. A message may direct you to download an app to track a parcel, but the app is malware that steals your data. Genuine delivery companies rarely require a new app download to track a parcel; their official app, already in the store, or their website, is sufficient. Be suspicious of any tracking link that leads to a download, a payment page for a small fee, or a form asking for extensive personal details. Check the delivery company's official website for its tracking process, and use only that. Be careful with delivery messages that arrive for parcels you did not order, because those are almost always scams, even if the message looks professional. If you run a business that receives many parcels, train staff to verify delivery messages through official channels, because a single employee tapping a fake tracking link can compromise a work device. Delivery is a routine part of online shopping, and its routine nature makes it a perfect cover for scams. Treating every unexpected delivery message as unverified until checked through the official channel is a habit that protects both personal and work deliveries.

Section 22: Returns, Refunds and Chargebacks

Returns, refunds, and chargebacks are the safety net that makes online shopping viable, and understanding them before you buy helps you use them when needed. A return is when you send goods back to the shop, usually within a stated period, for a refund or exchange. A refund is the money returned to you, which should arrive by the same payment method you used. A chargeback is a dispute raised through your bank or card network, asking them to reverse a charge because goods never arrived, were not as described, or were charged fraudulently. Chargebacks are powerful, but they should be used after trying to resolve the problem with the shop directly, because banks expect you to have given the seller a chance. Keep records for any dispute: order confirmations, delivery tracking, photos of damaged goods, and copies of messages with the seller. Note the deadlines: return windows, refund periods, and chargeback time limits vary, and missing one can cost you. Be careful with shops that offer no returns, no contact details, or only a vague refund policy, because those are warning signs. For marketplace purchases, use the platform's dispute process, which is designed for exactly these situations. If a shop refuses a legitimate refund, escalate to your bank with your evidence. Knowing these routes before you buy gives you confidence, because you know that a problem is not the end of the story. A shop that offers clear returns and honours them is a shop worth trusting with your next order.

Returns and refunds also involve the condition of returned goods and the cost of return postage, which vary by seller and by reason. If goods are faulty, the seller usually bears the return cost, but if you simply changed your mind, you may pay return postage, and the refund may be reduced if the goods are used or damaged. Read the return policy before buying, especially for clothing, electronics, and large items, where return costs can be significant. Keep the original packaging until you are satisfied with a purchase, because some sellers refuse returns without it. Photograph goods on arrival, especially high-value or fragile items, so you have evidence if they arrive damaged. If a seller refuses a legitimate return, escalate calmly: refer to the policy and to your consumer rights, keep copies of all messages, and involve your bank or the platform if needed. Be careful with return scams, where a buyer returns a different or damaged item, because sellers also need protection, and honest returns help keep the system working for everyone. Returns are a normal part of shopping, not an admission of failure, and a seller with a clear, fair return policy is a seller worth trusting. Knowing the policy before you buy prevents most return disputes.

Section 23: Your Rights as an Online Shopper

Online shoppers have legal rights that vary by country, but several principles are common and worth knowing. You generally have the right to receive goods as described, in good condition, and within a reasonable time. If goods are faulty, damaged, or not as described, you are usually entitled to a repair, replacement, or refund. In many places, you also have a cooling-off period for distance sales, during which you can return goods for any reason, though exceptions exist for personalised items, perishable goods, and digital downloads once accessed. You have the right to clear information before buying, including the total price, delivery costs, and the seller's identity. You have the right to a refund by the original payment method, not only as store credit, when a refund is due. These rights are enforced through consumer protection agencies, small claims processes, and card dispute systems, and knowing they exist helps you insist on them calmly. Keep records of every purchase, as described earlier, because rights are easier to enforce with evidence. Be careful with shops based abroad, because enforcing rights across borders is harder, and a shop that ignores your country's consumer law may be hard to pursue. For high-value purchases, prefer sellers in your own country or in a region whose consumer protections you understand. Rights are not a substitute for careful shopping, but they are a backstop, and a shopper who knows them is harder to intimidate into accepting a faulty product or a lost payment.

Consumer rights also include protection against unfair contract terms, such as a term that allows a seller to change the price after payment, to refuse all refunds, or to limit liability for faulty goods unreasonably. Such terms are often unenforceable, even if they appear in a seller's terms and conditions, because consumer law overrides them. If a seller relies on an unfair term to refuse a refund, you can challenge it, citing your country's consumer protection rules, and you can seek help from a consumer agency. Keep a copy of the seller's terms at the time of purchase, because terms can change, and a screenshot or a saved page is evidence of what you agreed to. Be careful with sellers who hide their terms, or who present them only after payment, because transparency before purchase is itself a consumer right. For digital content, rights are more complex, as covered earlier, but you still have rights to goods as described and to a remedy if they are faulty. Knowing your rights does not require legal training; it requires knowing that faulty goods, misleading descriptions, and unfair terms have remedies, and that consumer agencies and card dispute systems exist to enforce them. A shopper who knows their rights is harder to dismiss with a vague refusal.

Section 24: Marketplace Shopping Safely

Marketplaces, where many independent sellers list goods on one platform, offer choice and competitive prices, but they also require care, because the platform and the seller have different responsibilities. The platform usually provides buyer protection, a payment system, and a dispute process, while the seller provides the goods and handles delivery. To shop safely on a marketplace, stay inside the platform for every step: browse, message, pay, and resolve problems through the platform's own tools, never through a direct bank transfer or an off-platform payment a seller suggests, because that removes the platform's protection. Check the seller's rating and history, as covered earlier, and read the listing carefully for condition, delivery time, and return policy. Be careful with listings that use stock photos only, vague descriptions, or prices far below similar listings. For high-value items, prefer sellers with strong histories and clear return policies, and consider the platform's escrow or buyer protection, which holds payment until you confirm receipt. After delivery, inspect the goods promptly and report problems within the platform's deadline, because late reports may lose protection. Leave honest feedback after a transaction, because feedback is the system that protects the next buyer. Marketplaces are powerful tools for finding rare and affordable goods, and their protection systems work well when you stay inside them. Stepping outside, for a small discount or at a seller's urging, is where most marketplace fraud happens.

Marketplace protection also has limits that are worth knowing. Buyer protection usually covers goods not received or not as described, but it may not cover goods bought outside the platform, goods collected in person without a record, or purchases where you ignored the platform's warnings. Protection often has a deadline, after which a claim cannot be raised, so inspect goods promptly and report problems early. Some platforms offer protection only if you pay through their system and communicate through their messaging, which is another reason to stay inside the platform. Be careful with sellers who ask you to mark an order as received before you have inspected it, because that can void protection. If a dispute arises, provide clear evidence: photos, messages, tracking records, and a calm, factual description of the problem. Platforms usually favour buyers with good evidence, but they also consider seller history, so a single dispute against a long-standing seller may be resolved differently from a dispute against a new seller with complaints. Marketplace protection is a safety net, not a guarantee, and it works best when you follow the platform's rules, keep records, and act promptly. Section 25: Social Media Shops and Adverts

Shops that sell through social media adverts deserve extra caution, because social platforms make it easy for anyone to create a convincing shopfront, and their advertising systems do not verify every seller thoroughly. A social media advert may show a beautiful product at a low price, with glowing comments, but the shop behind it may be a fake, a dropshipper selling low-quality goods at a high markup, or a seller who never delivers. Before buying from a social advert, leave the platform and investigate the shop independently: search for its name plus reviews, check its website for real contact details and a return policy, and check how long it has existed. Be suspicious if the shop exists only on social media, with no independent website, or if its comments are all generic praise with no criticism. Check the product's real price from reputable retailers, and be wary of a dramatic discount. Prefer payment methods with dispute rights, such as a credit card, and avoid bank transfers or gift cards. Be careful with adverts that use stolen photos or videos from genuine brands, which is common. If you buy and the goods do not arrive or are not as described, report the advert to the platform, raise a dispute with your bank, and leave a public review warning others. Social media is a discovery tool, not a trusted shop, and treating every advert as a claim to be verified, rather than a shop to be trusted, is the habit that keeps social shopping safe.

Social media shopping also involves influencers and sponsored content, where a person you follow recommends a product. An influencer may be paid to promote a product, and that payment should be disclosed, but disclosure is not always clear. Treat influencer recommendations like adverts: verify the product and the seller independently, check reviews elsewhere, and compare prices with reputable retailers. Be careful with discount codes shared by influencers, because a code may be genuine, but it does not prove the seller is trustworthy. Some influencers promote fake or low-quality products unknowingly, because they did not test them, and some promote scams deliberately. If a product arrives and is not as described, report the advert and the influencer's post to the platform, and raise a dispute with your bank. Social media is a powerful discovery tool, and many genuine small businesses sell through it successfully, but discovery is not verification. A shop that exists only as an influencer's link, with no independent website, reviews, or contact details, is a high-risk purchase. A shop with a real website, a history, and a return policy, discovered through social media, is a normal purchase. The difference is verification, and verification takes only a few minutes.

Section 26: Gift Cards and Payment Scams

Gift cards are a favourite tool of scammers, because they are almost impossible to trace or reverse once the codes are shared. A typical gift card scam begins with a message claiming to be from a boss, a government office, a tech support team, or a family member in trouble, asking you to buy gift cards and share their codes, often with a story about urgency or secrecy. No genuine organisation asks for payment by gift card, and no genuine boss asks an employee to buy gift cards by text message. If you receive such a request, stop, verify through another channel, and do not buy the cards. Another version involves a fake shop or service that only accepts gift cards, which is itself a warning sign, because a legitimate shop accepts cards or reputable payment methods. If you have already bought gift cards and shared the codes, contact the card issuer immediately, because in rare cases, if the cards have not yet been spent, the issuer can freeze them, though recovery is uncommon. Report the scam to the police and to the platform where the message arrived. Teach family members, especially older relatives and young employees, that gift card requests are a universal scam signal, regardless of who appears to be asking. A gift card is a legitimate present when you choose to give one, but as a payment method demanded by a stranger, it is a red flag that should stop the transaction immediately, every time, without exception.

Gift card scams also target employees through impersonation of a boss or a senior manager. A message claiming to be from a chief executive, asking an employee to buy gift cards for clients or for a reward programme, is a well-known fraud pattern. Companies should have a clear rule: no gift card purchases are ever authorised by text or email alone, and any such request must be verified by a phone call to a known number or in person. Train staff to recognise the pattern, including urgency, secrecy, and a request to share codes by photo. If an employee does buy cards, act quickly: contact the issuer, report to the police, and review the company's payment controls. Gift cards are also used in tech support scams, where a caller claiming to fix a computer asks for payment by gift card. A genuine support service never asks for gift cards, and any caller who does is a scammer. Teach this rule widely, because gift card scams succeed through a combination of authority, urgency, and unfamiliarity. A person who knows the rule, no genuine organisation asks for gift cards, will stop the scam at the first message, however convincing the sender's name appears.

Section 27: Cryptocurrency Payments in Plain Words

Cryptocurrency is digital money that uses cryptography to record transactions on a public ledger, and paying with it differs from card payments in ways that matter for shoppers. Cryptocurrency payments are usually irreversible: once sent, they cannot be recalled through a bank dispute, because there is no bank in the middle to reverse them. They are also pseudonymous, meaning transactions are linked to wallet addresses rather than names, which makes recovering funds from a scammer very difficult. Prices can swing sharply, so the value of a payment can change between agreement and settlement. For those reasons, cryptocurrency is a poor choice for everyday shopping with unknown sellers, and scammers love it for exactly the same reasons shoppers should avoid it: no reversals, hard to trace, and easy to pressure victims into sending quickly. There are legitimate uses for cryptocurrency, such as investment or payments between parties who trust each other, but shopping should use payment methods with dispute rights unless you fully understand the risks and trust the seller completely. Be especially careful with shops that only accept cryptocurrency, or that offer a large discount for paying in cryptocurrency, because those are common scam patterns. If you do use cryptocurrency, use a reputable wallet and exchange, enable all available security features, double-check the recipient address character by character, and never send funds in response to an unexpected message. For most shoppers, the safest rule is simple: pay for goods with a card or a wallet, not with cryptocurrency.

Cryptocurrency scams also include fake investment platforms that promise high returns and show fake profits to encourage larger deposits. Those platforms are not shopping, but they exploit the same irreversible payment property: once you send cryptocurrency, it cannot be recalled. Be deeply suspicious of any investment that guarantees returns, pressures you to act quickly, or asks you to recruit others. Check whether the platform is registered with your country's financial regulator, and search for its name plus scam or review before sending anything. If you use cryptocurrency for legitimate purposes, keep most holdings in a secure wallet you control, enable all available security features, and never share your recovery phrase with anyone, including someone claiming to be support. A recovery phrase is the master key to your funds, and anyone who holds it controls them. For shopping, the rule remains: use a payment method with dispute rights. Cryptocurrency's irreversibility is a feature for some uses, but for buying goods from a stranger, it is a flaw, because it removes the safety net that cards and wallets provide. A shopper who understands that distinction will not be persuaded by a discount to give it up.

Section 28: Invoices and Payment Requests by Email

Invoices and payment requests sent by email are a common route for fraud, because email addresses can be faked and invoices look official. A typical scam involves an email that appears to come from a supplier, a landlord, or a service provider, asking you to pay an invoice to a new bank account, often with a story about changed details or an urgent deadline. The invoice may be genuine in appearance but altered, with the scammer's account details substituted. The defence is to verify independently before paying any invoice that arrives by email, especially one with changed payment details. Call the supplier on a number you already have, not on a number in the email, and confirm the amount and the account. Check the sender's address carefully, because a scammer's address may differ by a single letter from the genuine one. Be careful with invoices for goods or services you did not order, and with payment requests that arrive unexpectedly. For businesses, a simple rule helps: any change of bank details must be confirmed by a phone call to a known number, not by email alone, and large payments should require a second person's approval. For individuals, the same principle applies on a smaller scale: if a landlord, a tradesperson, or a service provider emails new payment details, confirm them by phone or in person before paying. An invoice is a request, not a proof, and verifying it through an independent channel is the habit that prevents a costly mistake.

Invoice fraud also affects individuals through fake utility bills, tax demands, and subscription renewals. A message claiming your electricity will be cut off, your tax is overdue, or your subscription has expired, with a payment link or new bank details, should be verified through the provider's official app or number, not through the message. Genuine providers send bills through established channels, offer a grace period, and do not demand immediate payment by an unusual method. Be careful with invoices that arrive as attachments from unknown senders, because the attachment may carry malware as well as a fake payment request. If you receive an invoice for a service you do not use, do not pay it and do not click its links; report it as phishing. For recurring bills, set up direct debits or standing orders through your bank's official channels, so you do not need to respond to emailed payment requests at all. That arrangement removes the opportunity for invoice fraud entirely for regular bills. For one-off invoices, a phone call to a known number is a small effort that prevents a large loss. Invoices are routine, and routine is where fraud hides, but a verification habit turns routine into safety.

Section 29: Paying Someone You Know Online

Paying someone you know online, such as a friend, a family member, or a local seller, feels safe, but it still deserves care, because accounts can be hijacked and messages can be faked. A common scam involves a hijacked social media or messaging account sending a request for money, claiming an emergency, and asking you to send it quickly to a new account. Before sending money to anyone, even someone you know, verify the request through another channel, such as a phone call or a message on a different app, especially if the request is urgent, involves a new payment method, or asks for secrecy. Check the payment details carefully: is the account name the person you expect, and does the amount match what was agreed? Use payment apps with buyer protection where available, and be careful with direct transfers to strangers, which offer little recourse. For local sellers, such as someone selling furniture or a car online, meet in a safe, public place where possible, inspect the goods before paying, and prefer a payment method that leaves a record. Be careful with advance payments for goods you have not seen, because a seller who insists on full payment upfront, without any protection, may never deliver. Paying people you know is a normal part of life, and most such payments are genuine, but a quick verification step, a phone call, a check of the account name, and a record of the payment, protects both you and the person you are paying, including from the embarrassment of a hijacked account.

Paying someone you know also includes splitting bills, crowdfunding, and group gifts, where a payment link is shared in a group chat. Before contributing, check that the organiser is who you think they are, that the cause is genuine, and that the payment link leads to a reputable platform. Be careful with links shared in a group where a member's account may have been hijacked, because a hijacked account can post a fake collection link that looks like a genuine group effort. Verify large group payments through a second channel, such as a direct message to the organiser on a different app. Keep records of group payments, including who organised, what for, and how much, because disputes over shared money can strain relationships. If a group payment is for a purchase, such as a gift, agree in advance who will buy, how much each person contributes, and what happens if the purchase falls through. For local sellers, a written record, even a simple message confirming price, condition, and delivery, protects both buyer and seller if a disagreement arises. Paying people you know is built on trust, and a few simple records and verifications protect that trust from hijacked accounts and misunderstandings alike.

Section 31: Elderly Shoppers and Scams

Older shoppers are frequently targeted by online shopping scams, not because they are careless, but because scammers exploit trust, politeness, and unfamiliarity with new tricks. Common scams aimed at older shoppers include fake shops advertising health products, fake tech support offering to fix a computer for a fee, and fake prize messages asking for a payment to release winnings. The defence is the same set of habits, taught with patience and concrete examples. Help older relatives set up safe shopping: a card with transaction alerts, a password manager or a safe notebook, multi-factor authentication on shopping and email accounts, and a rule to verify any unexpected shop, message, or payment request through a trusted person before paying. Encourage older shoppers to shop only with retailers they know, or that a trusted person has checked, and to be suspicious of dramatic discounts, urgent messages, and requests for unusual payment methods. Check in regularly, asking whether they have received strange messages or made unusual purchases, and praise them for asking for help. Patience, clear rules, and a blame-free response protect older shoppers far better than lectures about technology, because the habits that matter are simple, and the trust that lets someone ask for help is the most important protection of all.

Section 32: Tracking Spending and Statements

Tracking your online spending is a simple habit that catches fraud early and helps you stay within budget. Check your bank and card statements regularly, at least once a week for accounts you use online, looking for charges you do not recognise, including small ones, because attackers often test a stolen card with a tiny purchase before a larger one. Use your bank's app notifications for every transaction, so you learn about charges within seconds, rather than discovering them weeks later in a statement. Keep a simple record of online orders, such as a note or a folder of confirmation emails, and match charges against that record. If a charge does not match any order, contact your bank promptly, because early reporting improves the chances of recovery and limits further misuse. Be careful with subscription charges, which can be easy to overlook among many small transactions, and review them monthly. Teach family members to check statements too, especially for shared accounts, where one person's unfamiliar charge may be another's legitimate purchase. Tracking spending is not only about fraud; it also reveals your own habits, showing where money goes and where subscriptions accumulate. A few minutes a week, matching statements against orders, is one of the highest-value financial habits, because it catches problems while they are small, and it gives you a clear picture of your money that no amount of budgeting advice can replace.

Tracking spending can be made easier with a simple tool you run yourself. If your bank lets you export transactions as a CSV file, the following defensive Python script, run on your own computer and only on your own exported file, totals your spending by merchant and flags small, unfamiliar-looking charges for review. It never sends data anywhere and never moves money:

import csv
from collections import defaultdict
from pathlib import Path

def review_spending(csv_path):
    path = Path(csv_path).expanduser()
    totals = defaultdict(float)
    rows = 0
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as handle:
        reader = csv.DictReader(handle)
        for row in reader:
            rows += 1
            merchant = row.get("merchant") or row.get("description") or "unknown"
            try:
                amount = float(row.get("amount", 0))
            except ValueError:
                continue
            totals[merchant] += amount
    print(f"Reviewed {rows} transactions from your own export.")
    for merchant, total in sorted(totals.items(), key=lambda item: -abs(item[1]))[:20]:
        print(f"{merchant}: {total:.2f}")
    print("Tip: investigate any merchant you do not recognise, however small the amount.")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    review_spending("~/Downloads/transactions.csv")

Use this only with a file you exported yourself, from your own bank, and check the column names in your export, adjusting merchant and amount labels if your bank uses different headings. The script is a review aid, not a financial adviser, and it never replaces checking with your bank directly if you spot something wrong.

A review aid like this is most useful when run regularly, for example once a month, so that a new, unfamiliar merchant stands out against your normal spending pattern. It also helps you spot subscriptions you forgot, because a recurring merchant name appears month after month even when the amounts are small. Keep your exported file private, just as you would a bank statement, and delete it after review, because it contains the same sensitive information. If your bank's export uses different column names, adjust the script's labels once and reuse it, rather than retyping anything. The goal is not perfect accounting, but early detection: a merchant you do not recognise, however small the amount, deserves a check with your bank before it grows into a larger problem.

Section 33: What to Do After a Fraudulent Charge

Discovering a fraudulent charge is alarming, but acting quickly and methodically limits the damage. First, contact your bank or card provider immediately, using the number on the back of your card or in the bank's official app, and report the charge as fraudulent. The bank will usually freeze the card, issue a replacement, and begin a dispute process. Ask for a reference number and note the date and time of your call. Next, change the passwords for any shopping or email accounts linked to the card, especially if you suspect an account was hijacked, and turn on multi-factor authentication where it is not already on. Check your other accounts and statements for further unfamiliar activity, because a fraudster who holds your card details may try several charges. If the fraud involved a fake shop or a phishing message, report it to the platform and to the relevant consumer protection agency, because reports help shut down scams. Keep copies of every communication, including emails, reference numbers, and dispute forms, because a dispute can take weeks and you may need to refer back. Do not pay a fee to anyone who offers to recover your money, because recovery scams target fraud victims a second time. If the fraud was large or involved identity theft, consider reporting it to the police and checking your credit report for unfamiliar accounts. Acting quickly, documenting everything, and refusing recovery scams turns a frightening discovery into a managed process with a good chance of recovering your money.

After a fraudulent charge, also consider whether your identity, not only your card, was compromised. If a scammer holds your name, address, date of birth, and card details, they may try to open accounts in your name or to impersonate you to other services. Check your credit report for unfamiliar accounts or enquiries, and consider placing a fraud alert or a credit freeze where your country offers one, which makes it harder for anyone to open credit in your name without extra verification. Keep a record of every organisation you contacted, with dates and reference numbers, because identity recovery can take months and involve many parties. Be careful with follow-up scams: fraud victims are often contacted again by scammers claiming to offer refunds, recovery services, or compensation, for a fee. Genuine banks and authorities do not charge a fee to recover fraud losses, and any such offer is itself a scam. If the fraud involved a fake shop, keep the shop's details and your evidence, because authorities may ask for them later. Recovering from fraud is a process, not a single phone call, and patience, documentation, and scepticism about recovery offers are the habits that see it through. Most victims who act quickly and document well recover their money and protect their identity.

Section 34: Reporting a Fake Shop

Next, report it to your bank or card provider if you paid, because banks track fraud patterns and can warn other customers. Section 35: Keeping Shopping Accounts Secure

Shopping accounts deserve the same security as financial accounts, because they hold your address, your order history, and often a saved card. Start with a strong, unique password for each shopping site, stored in a password manager, and multi-factor authentication where the site offers it. Be careful with security questions, because answers like a mother's maiden name or a first pet can be found on social media; use random answers stored in your password manager where possible. Review the devices signed into each shopping account occasionally, and sign out of devices you no longer use. Check the account's address book and saved cards regularly, removing old addresses and cards you no longer need, because an attacker who hijacks the account can use those details. Turn on order and login notifications where the site offers them, so you learn about activity promptly. Be careful with public Wi-Fi and shared devices, as covered earlier, because a shopping account signed in on either is exposed. If a shopping site notifies you of a data breach, change that account's password immediately, and check whether you reused it elsewhere, changing those too. Shopping accounts feel low-stakes compared with banking, but they are a gateway to your home address, your spending habits, and your money. Treating them with the same care as a bank account, unique password, second factor, and regular review, is a habit that pays for itself the first time it blocks an account takeover.

Shopping account security also includes the devices and browsers you use to access them. A shopping account accessed from a malware-infected computer or an outdated browser is at risk even with a strong password, because malware can steal session cookies and bypass login checks. Keep your devices updated, use reputable security software, and be careful with browser extensions, as covered earlier. Sign out of shopping accounts on devices you share or no longer use, and review active sessions where the site shows them. Shopping security is a chain that includes your password, your second factor, your device, your browser, and your email account. Strengthening each link, rather than relying on one, is what keeps the chain strong. Section 36: Passwords and MFA for Shopping

Passwords and multi-factor authentication are the two locks on your shopping accounts, and using both is the standard you should aim for. A strong password should be long, unique to each site, and stored in a password manager, because reuse is the commonest reason shopping accounts are hijacked. Multi-factor authentication adds a second check, such as a code from an authenticator app or a confirmation in your bank's app, so a stolen password alone is not enough. Turn it on for every shopping site that offers it, starting with the sites where you have saved cards or addresses. Be careful with text-message codes, which are weaker than app codes because of SIM swapping, though they are still far better than no second factor at all. If a site does not offer multi-factor authentication, use an especially strong, unique password, and consider whether you need an account at all, or whether guest checkout would serve you just as well. Guest checkout, where you buy without creating an account, avoids storing your details on yet another site, and it is a good choice for one-time purchases. For sites you use regularly, an account with a strong password and a second factor is better, because it lets you track orders and manage returns. The combination, unique passwords plus a second factor, is the single most effective protection for shopping accounts, and it takes only a few minutes to set up per site. Those minutes are repaid the first time a breached password fails to open your account.

Multi-factor authentication for shopping also has a practical side: recovery when you lose your second factor. If you lose your phone with its authenticator app, you need a way back into your accounts, and that way should be prepared in advance. Save backup codes for each shopping site that offers them, storing them securely at home, and keep your email recovery options current, because email is often the fallback. If a site offers a choice between app codes and text codes, prefer app codes for security, but ensure you have a recovery plan for the app. Test recovery occasionally for your most important accounts, such as by signing in on a new device, so you know the process works before an emergency. Be careful with accounts that offer no recovery option beyond a single phone number, because losing that number can lock you out permanently. For such accounts, consider whether the account is worth the risk, or whether guest checkout would serve you better. Multi-factor authentication is a strong lock, and like any strong lock, it needs a spare key kept safely. Backup codes, a current recovery email, and a tested recovery process are that spare key, and preparing them takes only a few minutes per account.

Section 38: Price Comparison and Coupons Safely

Section 39: Loyalty Programmes and Data

Section 40: Shopping Apps vs Websites

Shopping apps and websites offer the same goods through different doors, and each has safety trade-offs. An app, installed from the official store, runs in a sandboxed environment with permission controls, and it can use biometric checks and wallet payments securely. A website runs in your browser, where you can check the address yourself, use extensions carefully, and avoid installing anything. Apps can be more convenient, with saved logins and push notifications for orders, but they also collect more data, including device information and usage patterns, and they can send promotional notifications designed to encourage spending. Websites collect data too, through cookies and trackers, but your browser's privacy settings give you more control. Fake shopping apps are a risk, as covered in the permissions chapter, so install shopping apps only from the official store, checking the developer name. Fake shopping websites are also a risk, so check the address and the shop's reputation before buying. For a shop you use regularly and trust, its official app is usually safe and convenient. For a shop you are trying for the first time, its website, checked carefully, may be safer, because you avoid installing an app that could collect data long after a single purchase. Whichever door you use, the same rules apply: verify the seller, use HTTPS, pay with a protected method, and keep records. The door matters less than the habits you bring through it.

Shopping apps versus websites also differ in how they handle updates and security patches. An app updated through the store receives security fixes automatically if you enable auto-updates, while a website's security depends on the retailer's servers, which you cannot control. That difference cuts both ways: an app you forget to update can become vulnerable, while a website's flaw can affect all its visitors at once. Keep shopping apps updated, and remove apps for shops you no longer use, because an outdated, forgotten app is a standing risk. For websites, keep your browser updated, because browser updates protect you against website flaws. Be careful with shopping apps that request permissions unrelated to shopping, such as contacts or location, because those permissions may indicate data collection beyond the purchase. Prefer the official app from the store, and be suspicious of an app offered only as a direct download, which bypasses store checks. Whether you shop by app or by website, the core habits remain: verify the seller, check the total, pay with a protected method, and keep records. The channel is a choice of convenience; the habits are the safety, and they travel with you across every channel you use.

Section 41: Cross-Border Shopping and Customs

Shopping from sellers in other countries can offer choice and lower prices, but it adds complications: customs duties, import taxes, longer delivery times, and harder dispute resolution. Before ordering from abroad, check whether the price includes duties and taxes, or whether you will be charged extra on delivery, because a bargain can become expensive after customs. Check the delivery time and the return policy, because returning goods abroad can cost more than the goods themselves, and some sellers offer no international returns at all. Be careful with customs messages: scammers send fake customs fee messages, similar to delivery scams, asking you to pay through a link. Verify any customs charge through the delivery company's official channel, not through a link in a message. Check your country's import rules for restricted items, because some goods cannot be imported legally, and ordering them can lead to seizure or a fine. For high-value cross-border purchases, prefer sellers with a clear international returns policy and payment methods with dispute rights, and keep all records, including customs forms. Consider whether a local seller offers the same goods for a slightly higher price, because the local seller's consumer protections, delivery speed, and return ease may be worth the difference. Cross-border shopping is a normal part of online life, but its extra layers, duties, delivery, and disputes, deserve a check before you buy, not a surprise after you have paid.

Cross-border shopping also involves consumer protection differences that are worth researching before a large purchase. Some countries offer strong distance-selling protections, including cooling-off periods and refund rights, while others offer little, and enforcing a right abroad can require legal action in another jurisdiction. Check the seller's country and the platform's terms for which law applies, and prefer sellers in jurisdictions whose protections you understand for high-value goods. Be careful with sellers who claim to be in one country but ship from another, because that discrepancy can indicate a dropshipper or a fake shop. Check delivery tracking carefully, because cross-border parcels pass through multiple carriers and customs, and a tracking gap does not necessarily mean a problem, though a long gap deserves a query to the seller. Keep customs documents, because they may be needed for a return or a warranty claim. If a cross-border purchase goes wrong, your card provider's dispute process is often the most practical remedy, because it operates across borders more easily than a court claim. Cross-border shopping opens a world of choice, but choice without protection is a gamble, and a quick check of duties, returns, and dispute routes before paying turns that gamble into a calculated purchase.

Section 42: Digital Goods: Games, Music, Software

Digital goods, such as games, music, films, and software, differ from physical goods because there is nothing to return in the usual sense, and refund rights are often narrower. Once you download a game or stream a film, you may lose the right to a cooling-off refund, because the content has been accessed. Before buying digital goods, check the seller's refund policy for digital content, and read reviews about quality and compatibility, because a game that does not run on your device or a film in the wrong region cannot be sent back. Buy digital goods only from official stores and reputable platforms, because unofficial sources are a common route for malware disguised as a game or software. Be careful with licence keys sold cheaply by unknown sellers, because those keys may be stolen, revoked, or region-locked, leaving you without the product and without a refund. Keep records of digital purchases, including licence keys and receipts, in a safe place, such as a password manager's secure notes, because losing a key can mean losing access. Be careful with subscriptions for digital content, which renew automatically, and review them regularly as covered earlier. For children, digital purchases need the same purchase approvals as physical ones, because a game item or a music subscription can charge a family account quickly. Digital goods are convenient and instant, but their instant nature means mistakes are harder to undo, so a pause to check the seller, the policy, and the compatibility before buying is especially worthwhile.

Digital goods also include licences and subscriptions that can be revoked or changed by the seller. A game, film, or software licence may be tied to an account, and if that account is closed or the seller withdraws the product, access can be lost even though you paid. Read the licence terms for digital purchases, especially for expensive software or game collections, and understand whether you own a copy or merely a right to access it. Keep backups of digital goods where the licence allows, such as downloaded installers or DRM-free copies, so a service closure does not erase your library. Be careful with digital goods sold as lifetime access, because lifetime may mean the product's lifetime, not yours, and a company closure ends it. For children's digital purchases, such as game items, explain that virtual items have no resale value and can disappear if the game closes, which helps set expectations. Digital goods are convenient, but their ownership is often weaker than physical goods, and that weakness should be reflected in how much you pay and how much you rely on them. A digital purchase is a licence, not a possession, and understanding that distinction prevents disappointment when a service changes or closes.

Section 43: Charity Donations Online

Donating to charity online is generous, but it is also exploited by scammers, especially after disasters and during fundraising campaigns. Fake charities, with convincing names and emotional appeals, collect donations that never reach the cause. Before donating, verify the charity independently: check its registration with your country's charity regulator, search for its name plus scam or review, and visit its official website yourself rather than following a link in a message or social post. Be careful with donation requests that arrive by text, email, or social media from unknown senders, even if they use a real charity's name and logo, because impersonation is common. Prefer donating through the charity's official website or a reputable donation platform, using a card or wallet, which leaves a record and offers dispute rights, rather than by bank transfer to an unknown account or by gift card, which no genuine charity requests. Keep the donation receipt for tax and record purposes, and check your statement for the correct amount and payee. Be careful with fundraising pages set up by individuals, which may be genuine but are harder to verify than registered charities; check the organiser's identity and the cause's details before giving. Generosity should not require abandoning caution. A few minutes verifying a charity ensures your donation helps the cause you intended, rather than funding a scammer who exploited your compassion.

Charity donations online also include fundraising for individuals, such as medical appeals or disaster relief for a named person. Those appeals can be genuine and moving, but they are harder to verify than registered charities, because there is no regulator to check. Before donating to an individual appeal, look for evidence: news coverage, verification by a reputable platform, and a clear account of how funds will be used. Be careful with appeals that use emotional photos or stories copied from elsewhere, which can be checked with a reverse image search if you know how. Prefer platforms that verify organisers and that hold funds until a goal or a verification step is met, over direct transfers to an individual's account. Keep your donation receipt and the appeal's details, so you can follow up if the appeal proves false. If you organise a fundraiser yourself, be transparent about the goal, the recipient, and the accounting, because transparency builds trust and protects you from suspicion. Generosity is a strength, and scammers exploit it precisely because it is a strength. Verifying an appeal, however briefly, does not diminish generosity; it ensures generosity reaches the person it was meant to help, which is the whole point of giving.

Section 44: Ticket Shopping Safely

Buying tickets online, for concerts, sports, travel, or events, carries specific risks, because tickets are time-sensitive, often sold out, and frequently resold at inflated prices. Buy from the official seller or an authorised reseller wherever possible, because unofficial sellers may sell fake, duplicate, or invalid tickets that are refused at the venue. Check the seller's authorisation on the event organiser's website, and be suspicious of a seller who cannot show it. Be careful with tickets sold through social media or classified adverts, where payment is often by bank transfer and there is no protection if the ticket is fake. If you must use a resale platform, choose one with buyer protection that holds payment until after the event or verifies the ticket, and read its terms carefully. Check the ticket details before paying: event, date, venue, seat, and any restrictions, such as name matching or age limits. Keep the confirmation and the ticket itself safe, and do not share photos of tickets with barcodes online, because a barcode can be copied and used by someone else before you arrive. Be careful with dynamic pricing and hidden fees, checking the total before confirming. If an event is cancelled, know your refund rights from the seller and from your card provider. Tickets are exciting purchases, and excitement makes people rush, but a pause to verify the seller and the ticket details protects both your money and your evening.

Ticket shopping also involves resale prices and fees that can be far above the face value, especially for popular events. Check the face value from the organiser, and compare the resale price, including fees, before buying, because a ticket sold at several times face value may still be valid, but it is a poor deal. Be careful with resale sites that do not verify tickets, because a ticket bought there may be a duplicate that is refused at the venue. Prefer resale platforms that guarantee entry or offer a refund if the ticket is invalid, and read that guarantee's terms. If you sell a ticket yourself, use an authorised resale platform rather than social media, because the platform protects both you and the buyer, and it prevents your ticket's barcode from being copied. Do not post ticket photos online before the event, even after the event has passed, because barcodes can sometimes be reused or can reveal personal details. After an event, keep the ticket and confirmation until any refund or dispute window closes. Tickets combine excitement, scarcity, and high prices, which is a perfect mix for scams, and the habits that protect you, official seller, verified resale, protected payment, and barcode privacy, are the same habits that protect every online purchase, applied with event-specific care.

Section 45: Travel Booking Payments

Booking travel online involves large payments, complex itineraries, and many intermediaries, which makes it a rich target for scams. Fake travel sites copy genuine booking platforms, offering flights or hotels at dramatic discounts, and then either never book anything or book a different, cheaper itinerary. Before paying for travel, verify the site independently: check its reputation, its contact details, and whether it is a member of a travel association or protection scheme in your country. Prefer booking directly with the airline or hotel where possible, because direct bookings are easier to change and refund, and the provider's own customer service can help if something goes wrong. If you use a booking platform, read its terms for cancellations, changes, and refunds, because platforms vary greatly. Pay with a credit card where possible, because travel purchases are large and dispute rights matter more. Keep every confirmation, including booking references, itineraries, and receipts, in one place, and check them against what you ordered. Be careful with payment requests that arrive after booking, claiming an extra fee or a problem, because those are a common scam; verify any such request through the airline or hotel directly. Travel is expensive and emotional, and scammers exploit both. A verified seller, a protected payment method, and a complete set of records are the habits that keep a trip safe from the moment you book.

Travel booking payments also include add-ons and extras that can inflate the total, such as seat selection, baggage, insurance, and car hire, some of which are pre-selected at checkout. Review the total carefully before confirming, unticking extras you do not want, and checking whether travel insurance offered at checkout suits your needs or duplicates cover you already have. Be careful with travel sites that show a low headline price but add large fees at the final step, a practice known as drip pricing, which is restricted in some places but still occurs. Compare the final total, not the headline, across sellers. Keep a record of every add-on you purchased, because a dispute about a missing extra needs evidence. If you book a package holiday, check whether it is protected by a financial protection scheme in your country, which refunds you if the provider fails. Package protection is a significant safety net for travel, and it is worth choosing a protected package for expensive trips. Travel booking is complex, with many parties and many extras, and complexity is where mistakes and scams hide. A careful review of the total, the terms, and the protection scheme before paying turns a complex booking into a safe one.

Section 46: Rental and Holiday Booking Scams

Holiday rentals and short stays are a frequent target for scams, because they involve large advance payments for a property you have not seen, often in another city or country. A typical scam lists a beautiful property at a low price on a classified site or social media, asks for a deposit or full payment by bank transfer, and then disappears, or the property does not exist, or it belongs to someone else. To protect yourself, book through reputable platforms with buyer protection, verified listings, and a dispute process, rather than paying a stranger directly. Check the listing carefully: reverse-search its photos, because scammers reuse photos from genuine listings, and check whether the same property appears elsewhere at a different price. Read reviews of the property and the host, looking for a history of real stays. Be suspicious of a host who pressures you to pay outside the platform, offers a discount for direct payment, or refuses a video call or additional photos. Visit the property or ask a trusted person nearby to check it for long stays or high-value bookings. Pay with a protected method, keep all messages inside the platform, and keep records of every payment. If a deal seems too good for its location and season, it probably is. A holiday should begin with excitement, not with a dispute over a property that never existed, and booking through a protected platform with a verified listing is the habit that prevents that disappointment.

Holiday rental scams also include fake reviews and fake host profiles that make a non-existent property look established. A scammer may copy reviews from a genuine listing, create a host profile with stolen photos, and respond promptly and charmingly to questions, all to build false trust. To check a listing, look for a consistent history across platforms: does the same host appear elsewhere with the same property and reviews? Does the property's address match its photos and description? Can you verify the host's identity through the platform's verification badges? Be suspicious of a host who avoids video calls, refuses additional photos, or insists on payment outside the platform for any reason, including a claimed platform fee or a last-minute discount. For a high-value stay, consider a video call with the host showing the property live, which is hard to fake convincingly. Keep all communication inside the platform, because off-platform messages lose the platform's record and protection. If you arrive and the property does not match the listing, document the differences with photos immediately and contact the platform before accepting the stay. A holiday rental is a trust transaction, and trust should be built on verifiable evidence, not on charming messages alone.

Section 47: Second-Hand Shopping Online

Buying second-hand goods online can save money and reduce waste, but it carries risks that new goods do not, because condition, authenticity, and seller honesty vary. Before buying, read the listing carefully, including the condition description, photos from multiple angles, and any mention of faults. Ask the seller questions about the item's history, condition, and reason for selling, and be suspicious of vague answers or a refusal to provide more photos. Check the seller's rating and history, as covered earlier, and prefer sellers with a record of honest descriptions. Use the platform's payment and protection system, never a direct transfer, and meet in a safe public place for local pickups, inspecting the item before paying. Be careful with high-value second-hand items, such as phones, designer goods, and electronics, because counterfeits are common and faults may be hidden. For electronics, test the item before paying where possible, and check that it is not locked to a previous owner's account. Keep records of the listing, messages, and payment, because a dispute needs evidence. After purchase, inspect the item promptly and report problems within the platform's deadline. Second-hand shopping rewards patience and inspection, and it punishes haste. A careful buyer, who asks questions, checks the seller, uses platform protection, and inspects before paying, can find genuine bargains safely, while a hurried buyer is the target every second-hand scammer hopes for.

Second-hand shopping also includes the risk of buying stolen goods, which can be seized by authorities and can involve you in a dispute even if you bought innocently. Check for warning signs: a seller offering many new, high-value items at low prices, a vague or inconsistent story about an item's origin, a refusal to provide a receipt or serial number, and pressure to pay quickly outside the platform. For high-value items, ask for the original receipt, the serial number, and a photo of the item with a handwritten note showing the date, which are hard for a thief to provide convincingly. Check the serial number against stolen-goods registers where available. Keep records of your purchase, because a record of a good-faith purchase through a platform protects you if an item's origin is later questioned. If you suspect an item is stolen, do not complete the purchase, and consider reporting the listing to the platform. Second-hand markets thrive on trust, and honest sellers welcome questions about origin and condition. A seller who resists those questions is telling you something, and listening to that signal protects you from losing both the item and the money. Buying second-hand should feel like a bargain, not a gamble, and verification is what makes the difference.

Section 48: Teaching Others to Shop Safely

Teaching others to shop safely is a gift that protects family, friends, and colleagues, because a single unsafe purchase can affect shared accounts and household finances. Keep the teaching practical and positive: show someone how to check a shop's reviews, how to recognise a safe checkout, and how to choose a protected payment method, using a real example rather than a lecture. Help older relatives set up transaction alerts, a password manager, and purchase approvals, and write the key rules down in large, clear text. Teach children that online purchases cost real money, using a concrete comparison, and agree on family rules for asking permission. Share stories of near misses, including your own, because stories are memorable and they normalise asking for help. Encourage everyone to pause before paying, to verify through official channels, and to report scams, because reporting protects the next shopper. For colleagues, share a short checklist for work purchases, covering seller verification, payment methods, and record-keeping. Be patient and non-judgmental when someone falls for a scam, because shame prevents reporting and learning. Teaching is not about making everyone an expert; it is about giving everyone a few reliable habits and a trusted person to ask. A household or a team that shops safely together is safer than any individual, because the habits reinforce each other and the questions get asked before payment, not after the money is gone.

Teaching others to shop safely also includes teaching them to handle pressure, because pressure is the scammer's main tool. A message claiming a deal ends in minutes, an account will close today, or a parcel will be returned tomorrow is designed to stop thinking. Teach a simple response to pressure: pause, breathe, and verify through an official channel, never through the message itself. Practise that response with family members, using example messages, so pausing feels natural rather than rude. Teach children that a game offer that expires in minutes is a trick, not a genuine deadline, and teach older relatives that a threat of arrest or account closure by phone or message is a scam tactic. For colleagues, include pressure tactics in any shopping guidance, because work purchases under deadline pressure are vulnerable too. Pressure affects everyone, regardless of age or experience, and the defence is not intelligence but habit: a pause, a verification, and a willingness to miss a deal rather than fall for a scam. A person who has practised pausing under simulated pressure will pause under real pressure too, and that pause is often the entire difference between a safe shopper and a victim.

Section 49: Myths About Online Payments

Several myths about online payments lead people either to fear shopping online unnecessarily or to trust it blindly, and clearing them up helps you shop with balanced confidence. The first myth is that online shopping is inherently unsafe. In reality, with reputable sellers, HTTPS checkouts, and protected payment methods, online shopping is often safer than handing a card to a stranger in a shop. The second myth is the opposite: that online shopping is completely safe because you have never had a problem. Past safety is not a guarantee, and new scams appear constantly. The third myth is that a padlock means a shop is trustworthy, which it does not, as covered earlier; it means only that the connection is encrypted. The fourth myth is that debit and credit cards offer identical protection, which they do not, because of how quickly money leaves your account. The fifth myth is that small purchases do not matter for security, but small purchases can test a stolen card, accumulate into subscriptions, and reveal your address and habits. The sixth myth is that only wealthy people are targeted, while in reality, scammers target anyone, automating their campaigns across thousands of victims. The truth is balanced: online payments are safe when you verify the seller, use a protected method, and keep records, and they are risky when you rush, trust blindly, or pay by irreversible methods. Believing the myths leads either to avoiding a useful convenience or to trusting a dangerous seller, and neither extreme serves you well.

Online payment myths also include the idea that a shop's professional appearance proves its legitimacy. A fake shop can copy a genuine shop's design perfectly, because design is easy to copy and trust is hard to verify. Appearance is a clue, not a proof, and the proofs are independent: reviews elsewhere, a real address and contact, a history of trading, and payment methods with protection. Another myth is that a small shop cannot be a scam because scammers only impersonate big brands. In reality, small fake shops appear constantly, selling niche products to small audiences, and their small size makes them harder to detect. A further myth is that paying by card makes any purchase safe, because a card dispute has deadlines and conditions, and a purchase from a shop that never delivers may still take weeks to resolve. Card protection is strong, but it is not instant, and it works best when combined with seller verification. The common thread is over-reliance on a single signal, whether appearance, size, or payment method. Safe shopping relies on several signals together, and no single signal, however reassuring, should override the others when they disagree.
