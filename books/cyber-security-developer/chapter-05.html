Chapter 5: Phishing and How to Spot It

Section 1: What Phishing Really Is

This section explains what phishing really is in plain words, using everyday examples you can picture and steps you can actually try on your own devices and accounts. Hovering over a link, or pressing and holding it on a phone, can reveal its true destination before you visit it. A link that claims to go to your bank but points to a strange domain, a shortened link with no clear destination, or a long address full of random characters should all raise suspicion. The safest habit is not to use links in unexpected messages at all: type the address yourself, use a bookmark you made earlier, or open the official app. That one habit defeats most phishing links, whatever they look like. Attachments are a common phishing tool, because opening a file can install harmful software or show a fake login page. Be especially careful with unexpected invoices, delivery notes, job offers, and documents that ask you to enable macros or extra features to view them. If you were not expecting a file, verify with the sender through a separate channel before opening it, and if you do not know the sender at all, do not open it. Your curiosity is not worth the risk, and a genuine sender will happily resend a file through a safer route. Smishing is phishing by text message, and vishing is phishing by voice call, and both use the same tricks as email phishing: authority, urgency, and a plausible story. A text about a parcel fee, a call about a bank problem, or a message from a supposed relative in trouble all deserve the same pause and independent check. Never call back a number given in a suspicious message; look up the organisation's real number yourself. Never read out a code, password, or card detail to a caller, however official they sound. Spear phishing is a targeted version, aimed at one person or a small group, using details about them to seem believable: their job, their colleagues, a recent project, or a service they actually use. Because it looks personal, it is harder to spot, and it is often used against workplaces. The defence is the same, but stricter: verify unusual requests, especially requests to move money, share data, or change payment details, through a second channel and with a second person where possible. A genuine urgent request will survive a careful check. A small habit repeated every day protects you far more than one big effort made only once in a while. When you are unsure about a message, a link, or a request, pausing before you act is always the safer choice. Write down what you changed and why, so you can check it later and help someone else understand your decision. Good security feels ordinary most of the time, because it is built from simple steps done in the right order. If a step feels rushed or secretive, treat that feeling as useful information and slow down before you continue.

def looks_suspicious(message):
    flags = []
    text = message.lower()
    for phrase in ['urgent', 'account will close', 'click now', 'verify immediately', 'prize']:
        if phrase in text:
            flags.append(phrase)
    return flags
