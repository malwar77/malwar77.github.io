Chapter 11: Backups and Recovery in Plain Words

Section 1: What a Backup Really Is

A backup is simply a second copy of something you do not want to lose. That is the whole idea, and it is worth holding on to because the world of backup tools loves complicated words. If your school essay exists only on one laptop, you do not have a backup. If the same essay also exists on a USB drive in your drawer, or in a cloud account you can reach from another device, you do have one. The second copy is what turns a disaster into an inconvenience. It does not need to be fancy, expensive, or automatic to count, although automatic copies are easier to keep up. What matters is that the copy is separate from the original, so one accident cannot destroy both at the same time.

People often confuse a backup with a shortcut, a synced folder, or a file they moved somewhere else. Moving a file is not backing it up, because the file now exists in only one place again, just a different place. Syncing can also mislead you: if you delete a file on your laptop and the sync service faithfully deletes it everywhere else, that is not a backup, it is a very efficient deletion. A real backup keeps an older copy even after you change or delete the original, at least for a while. That time delay is what saves you when you realise, two days later, that you deleted the wrong folder or saved over the good version with a bad one.

Think of a backup like a spare key. You do not make a spare key because you plan to lose the first one; you make it because losing keys is something humans do, and the cost of a spare is tiny compared with the cost of being locked out. Files are the same. Hard drives wear out, phones get stolen, laptops get dropped, and ransomware can lock everything it can reach. None of those events care how careful you usually are. A backup does not prevent any of them, but it changes what happens next. Instead of starting over, you copy your files back and carry on with your life.

A good way to test whether you really have a backup is to ask three questions. First, if this device disappeared tonight, could I still reach my important files from somewhere else? Second, if I deleted an important file last week, could I get yesterday's version back? Third, have I actually tried restoring a file, or am I just trusting that the copy exists? If the answer to any of those is no, your backup plan has a hole, and this chapter will help you close it. The rest of the chapter builds on this simple definition, one practical step at a time, until backups feel as ordinary as charging your phone.

important_files = ["school_essay.docx", "family_photos.zip", "budget.xlsx"]
backup_copies = ["school_essay.docx", "family_photos.zip"]
missing = [f for f in important_files if f not in backup_copies]
print("Files without a backup copy:", missing)

Section 2: Why Good People Lose Files

Losing files is not a punishment for carelessness, and it is not something that only happens to other people. It happens to careful, clever, organised people every day, because files live on physical things that break, and because human brains are wonderful at many things but terrible at remembering to do boring tasks on schedule. A hard drive can fail without warning, even in a laptop that worked perfectly yesterday. A phone can slip out of a pocket into water. A child can tap delete on a parent's tablet. A power cut at the wrong second can corrupt a document that was open. None of these need a villain. They only need time, and time is something we all have plenty of.

There is also a quieter way files are lost: slow, invisible change. A document gets edited a little at a time until the original wording you wanted is gone. A photo gets cropped and saved over the original. A folder gets reorganised by someone trying to help, and the file you need ends up somewhere you would never think to look. Months pass, the old version is forgotten, and by the time you want it, it is unrecoverable. This is why a backup is not only about disasters with dramatic names like fire and theft. It is also about the small, everyday edits and accidents that add up. Version history, which keeps older copies of a file, exists precisely for this kind of slow loss, and you will meet it later in this chapter.

Another common cause of loss is overconfidence in a single device or a single service. People trust a laptop because it has never failed, or trust a cloud account because a big company runs it. Both can fail, and both sometimes do. Companies close services, accounts get locked after a forgotten password or a suspicious login, and free storage tiers quietly stop syncing when they fill up. If your only copy lives in one company's cloud and you lose access to that account, your files are gone even though nobody deleted them. That is why later sections teach you to keep copies in more than one place, owned and controlled in more than one way.

The most useful attitude is to assume loss will happen eventually and plan for it calmly, the way you plan for rain by carrying an umbrella. You do not need to feel anxious about your files. You need a simple routine that runs whether or not you are thinking about it. Automatic backups are powerful for exactly this reason: they do not depend on your memory, your mood, or your free time. When you build that routine, losing a device becomes an annoying errand instead of a crisis. You buy a replacement, restore your files, and get back to work, which is a very different story from losing years of photos and schoolwork in one afternoon.

risks = ["drive failure", "theft", "accidental deletion", "power cut"]
for risk in risks:
    print("Plan for:", risk, "-> keep a second copy elsewhere")

Section 3: The 3-2-1 Rule in Plain Words

The 3-2-1 rule is the simplest backup plan that professionals actually trust, and it fits in one sentence: keep three copies of your important files, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy somewhere else. Three copies means the original plus two backups. Two kinds of storage means not putting both backups on the same type of thing, for example not two USB sticks from the same drawer, but one external drive and one cloud account, or one external drive and one second computer. One copy somewhere else means a copy that would survive if your house flooded or your bag was stolen, because a backup sitting next to the computer it protects shares the same fate as that computer.

Why three copies and not two? Because backups can fail too. An external drive can die while sitting in a drawer. A cloud account can be locked. A backup can be incomplete because it stopped halfway through, or because it silently skipped a folder you assumed it covered. With three copies, one failure still leaves you with a spare. This is the same logic as carrying both a spare key and leaving one with a trusted neighbour. Redundancy sounds wasteful until the day you need it, and on that day it feels like the smartest decision you ever made.

The two different kinds of storage matter because different storage fails in different ways. A hard drive fails mechanically. A cloud service fails through account problems, internet outages, or a company changing its rules. A USB stick fails through loss, because it is small and easy to misplace. By mixing types, you make it unlikely that one single kind of problem wipes out every copy at once. You do not need exotic technology for this. A laptop, an external drive, and a reputable cloud account already satisfy the rule for most people, and that combination is affordable and easy to maintain.

The off-site copy is the part people skip most often, and it is the part that matters most in a real disaster. If a fire, flood, or burglary takes your computer and your backup drive together, you have no backup at all, only a sad story about a backup you used to have. An off-site copy can be a cloud backup, a drive kept at a family member's house, or a drive kept at work or school, as long as it is genuinely somewhere else and updated regularly. A drive at your cousin's house that has not been updated in two years is better than nothing, but it is not a current backup. The rule works best when all three copies are kept reasonably fresh.

You do not have to build the full 3-2-1 system today. Start with one backup, then add the second type, then add the off-site copy. Each step makes you safer than you were before, and a partial plan honestly kept is far better than a perfect plan you never start. Write down where your copies live, because a backup you cannot find is almost as bad as no backup at all. Later sections will turn this rule into concrete steps for phones, computers, photos, and schoolwork.

copies = {"original": "laptop", "backup_1": "external drive", "backup_2": "cloud account"}
print("Copies:", len(copies))
print("Off-site copy:", copies["backup_2"])

Section 4: Full, Incremental and Differential Backups

Backup tools offer three main styles, and the names sound technical but the ideas are simple. A full backup copies everything you selected, every time. It is the simplest to understand and the simplest to restore from, because everything you need is in one place. Its disadvantage is size and time: copying everything every day takes longer and uses more storage, especially if you have many photos or videos. For a small set of documents, a full backup is often fine. For a large photo collection, doing a full backup every single day can become slow and wasteful, which is why the other two styles exist.

An incremental backup copies only what changed since the last backup of any kind. Imagine you have a hundred files and you change two of them today. An incremental backup copies just those two. That makes it fast and small, which is why many automatic backup tools use it for daily runs. The trade-off is in restoring: to rebuild your files, you need the last full backup plus every incremental backup since then, in order. If one link in that chain is damaged or missing, the restore can break. Good backup tools hide this complexity from you, but it is worth understanding, because it explains why a restore can sometimes fail even when most of your backup files look fine.

A differential backup sits in the middle. It copies everything that changed since the last full backup, not since the last backup of any kind. Using the same example, if you change two files on Monday and three more on Tuesday, Tuesday's differential backup copies all five changed files. It is bigger than an incremental backup, but restoring is simpler: you need only the last full backup and the latest differential backup. Many people use a weekly full backup plus daily differential backups as a sensible balance, or a weekly full backup plus daily incremental backups if storage space is tight. There is no single right answer for everyone, and the best choice depends on how much data you have, how often it changes, and how much storage you can afford.

What matters more than the exact style is that you understand what your tool is doing and that you test restores. A backup system you do not understand is hard to trust and hard to fix when it breaks. When you set up a tool, look for its settings and read what kind of backup it makes and how long it keeps old versions. Keep the schedule realistic: a daily backup that takes eight hours and slows your computer to a crawl will be switched off within a week, while a nightly backup that quietly finishes in minutes will still be running in a year. Design for the person you actually are, not the person you wish you were, and let the tool do the boring work.

all_files = ["a.txt", "b.txt", "c.txt", "d.txt"]
changed = ["b.txt", "d.txt"]
print("Full backup copies:", len(all_files), "files")
print("Incremental backup copies:", len(changed), "files")

Section 5: Choosing What to Back Up First

If you have never made a backup, the whole job can feel overwhelming, because a modern computer holds thousands of files and a phone holds thousands of photos. The trick is to start with what would hurt most to lose, not with everything at once. Sit down for ten minutes and list the files you would be upset about losing: family photos, school or work documents, a project you have spent months on, financial records, and anything that cannot be downloaded again. That short list is your first backup job. Everything else can wait, and backing up the important things today is far better than planning a perfect backup of everything next month.

A useful way to sort files is to ask whether each one can be replaced. Music and films from a streaming service can be downloaded again. Apps can be reinstalled. Operating system files can be rebuilt by reinstalling the system. But your own photos, your own writing, your own schoolwork, and records of your own life cannot be downloaded again from anywhere, because you are the only person who has them. Those irreplaceable files deserve the first and most careful backup. Replaceable files deserve backup only if you have space and time to spare, or if reinstalling everything would cost you days of work. This simple test turns a huge job into a small, clear one.

Size also matters, and it helps to be honest about it. Photos and videos usually take up far more space than documents, so a backup plan that works for documents may struggle with a decade of videos. That does not mean you should skip videos; it means you should plan storage for them deliberately, perhaps with a larger external drive, and back them up in batches if needed. Start with the most precious videos, such as family events, and work outward. If a full backup of everything would take a week, break it into evenings: documents tonight, photos tomorrow, videos over the weekend. Progress in pieces still counts as progress, and each finished batch is one less thing to worry about.

Finally, write your list down and keep it with your backup plan. When you later buy a bigger drive or set up a cloud account, the list tells you what to check first. When you help a family member back up their computer, the same question, what would hurt most to lose, gives them a place to start without panic. Backups are not about copying every byte on every device. They are about making sure the things that matter most to you exist in more than one place, and that you know where those copies are. Start small, start with what hurts most, and build outward from there.

files = [("thesis.docx", False), ("song.mp3", True), ("baby_photo.jpg", False)]
for name, replaceable in files:
    priority = "low" if replaceable else "BACK UP FIRST"
    print(name, "->", priority)

Section 6: External Drives You Control

An external drive is a hard drive or solid-state drive that plugs into your computer with a cable, and it is the most straightforward backup tool most people will ever own. You buy it once, plug it in, copy your files, unplug it, and put it somewhere safe. There is no monthly fee, no account to lose access to, and no internet connection needed. That simplicity is a real strength. It also means the drive is yours in a way a cloud account never is: nobody can change its terms, lock you out of it, or decide to close the service. For many people, an external drive is the first backup they ever make, and it is a very good first step.

There are two common kinds. A traditional hard drive, often called an HDD, stores data on spinning disks and gives you a lot of space for the price, but it is sensitive to drops and knocks. A solid-state drive, or SSD, has no moving parts, is faster, and survives being knocked around much better, but costs more for the same amount of space. For a backup drive that mostly sits in a drawer, a hard drive is usually fine and cheaper. For a drive you carry in a bag every day, an SSD is worth the extra cost because it is built for that life. Either way, buy a drive with more space than you think you need, because your files will grow and a drive that is already full cannot protect anything new.

The most important habit with an external drive is unplugging it after the backup finishes. A drive that stays plugged in all the time is convenient, but it shares the fate of your computer: ransomware that encrypts your computer can also encrypt an attached drive, a power surge can damage both, and a thief can take both. A backup drive that is unplugged and stored in another room, or another building, is protected from all three. The routine is simple: plug in, run the backup, wait for it to finish, safely eject the drive, and put it away. It takes a few minutes, and those minutes are the whole point. A backup that is always connected is only half a backup.

You should also label your drives and keep a simple note of what is on each one and when it was last updated. A drawer full of unlabelled drives is a puzzle, not a backup plan, and in a stressful moment you do not want to guess which drive holds last month's photos. A piece of tape and a pen are enough: the date, the computer it backs up, and what is on it. Check the drive from time to time, too. Plug it in every few months, open a few files, and make sure they still open correctly. Drives age, and a backup you never check is a promise you have not tested. That small check is what turns a drive in a drawer into a backup you can actually rely on.

drives = [("Photos-2026", "2026-10-15"), ("Schoolwork", "2026-10-28")]
for name, last_backup in drives:
    print(name, "last updated:", last_backup)

Section 7: Cloud Backup Basics

Cloud backup means storing a copy of your files on computers owned by a company, reached over the internet. You install a small program or use a phone setting, choose what to back up, and the service copies your files to its servers and keeps them updated as you change things. The great strength of cloud backup is distance: your copy lives far from your house, so a fire, flood, or burglary at home does not touch it. It is also automatic in a way that a drawer drive is not, because it works quietly in the background whenever you have internet. For many people, cloud backup is the off-site copy in their 3-2-1 plan, and it is the copy most likely to save them after a real disaster.

Cloud backup is not the same as cloud sync, and the difference matters. Sync services, like the folders that mirror your files across devices, are designed to keep every device identical. If you delete a file, sync deletes it everywhere. Backup services are designed to keep older versions and deleted files for a set time, often thirty days or more, so you can recover from mistakes. When you choose a service, read that detail carefully. A service that calls itself backup should tell you how long it keeps deleted files and old versions. If it deletes everything the moment you do, it is sync wearing a backup label, and you should treat it accordingly.

There are real trade-offs to cloud backup, and an honest chapter should name them. You need internet, and uploading a large photo collection for the first time can take days on a slow connection. You pay a recurring fee for most serious services, although many offer a small free tier that is enough for documents. You are trusting a company with your files, so you should choose a reputable one, use a strong password and multi-factor authentication on the account, and encrypt sensitive backups where the service allows it. Also remember that access to your cloud backup depends on access to your account. If you lose your password and your recovery options, your backup is locked away too, so keep your account recovery details safe and up to date.

A sensible approach is to use the cloud for what it does best: an automatic, off-site copy of your most important files, while keeping a local drive copy as well. Start with documents and the most precious photos, let the first upload finish, then expand to more. Test the restore early: delete a test file from your computer, or use a second device, and confirm you can download it back from the cloud. That one test teaches you more about the service than a month of reading its advertising. Cloud backup is a tool, not magic, but used with a local copy alongside it, it turns a lost or stolen device from a catastrophe into a bad day.

for role, place in plan.items():
    print(role, "->", place)

Section 8: Automatic Backups Beat Good Intentions

Almost everyone who loses files once believed they would back up soon. The intention was sincere, the plan was reasonable, and then life happened: exams, work, family, illness, or simply a busy week that became a busy month. Manual backups fail not because people are lazy, but because they depend on memory and free time, two things that are always in short supply. Automatic backups succeed for the opposite reason: they do not ask you for anything. Once set up, they run on a schedule, in the background, whether you remember them or not. If you take one idea from this chapter into your real life, let it be this one: automate your backups, and let your future self be protected by a decision your present self makes once.

Setting up an automatic backup is usually simpler than people expect. Most computers have a built-in backup tool, and most phones have a backup setting that can be switched on in a few taps. The setup asks you where to store the backup, what to include, and how often to run it. Choose a schedule you can live with: daily is ideal for documents and work files, weekly can be enough for a computer that changes slowly, and continuous backup, which copies files shortly after they change, is best if your tool offers it without slowing your machine. After setup, watch the first backup complete, and check that it actually included the folders you care about. Many people set up a tool, assume it covered everything, and discover months later that it skipped their most important folder because it was stored in an unusual place.

Automation also protects you from the most human failure of all: backing up only after a scare. People often rush to back up the day after a friend loses files, then stop again within a month. An automatic schedule does not have moods or busy weeks. It also creates regular versions over time, which matters when you need last Tuesday's version of a document rather than today's broken one. Think of it like brushing your teeth: nobody brushes because they feel inspired, they brush because it is a habit built into the day. Automatic backup is the habit version of data safety, and habits beat resolutions every time.

That said, automatic does not mean unattended forever. Check in on your backups every month or so. Look for warning messages, failed runs, or a drive that has filled up. A backup tool that failed silently for three months is worse than no tool at all, because it gives you false confidence. Set a monthly reminder on your phone to glance at your backup status, and treat any error message as something to fix that week, not someday. Automation does the daily work, and your monthly five-minute check makes sure the automation is still working. Together, they give you protection that survives real life, which is the only kind that counts.

schedule = {"documents": "daily", "photos": "daily", "whole_system": "weekly"}
for item, frequency in schedule.items():
    print(item, "backed up", frequency)

Section 9: Testing a Restore Before You Need One

A backup you have never restored from is a hope, not a proven fact. That sentence sounds harsh, but it is the most practical advice in this whole chapter. Backup tools can fail in quiet ways: a backup can stop halfway, skip a folder, store corrupted files, or require a password you have forgotten. You discover all of these at the worst possible moment if you discover them during a real emergency, when you are stressed, in a hurry, and possibly using a replacement device. Testing a restore in advance, when nothing is wrong, turns that moment of panic into a routine you have already practised. It is the difference between rehearsing a fire drill and trying to learn the escape route while the building is actually on fire.

Testing a restore is easier than it sounds. Pick a small, unimportant file, or make a test file with a silly name, and back it up. Then delete the original from your computer, or rename it, and restore it from your backup. Open the restored file and check that it is complete and correct. If you use cloud backup, try downloading the file on a different device, such as your phone, to prove the copy really lives elsewhere. If you use an external drive, unplug it, plug it back in on another day, and restore from it then. Each small test proves one link in the chain: the backup ran, the copy is readable, and you know the steps to get a file back.

You should also practise a bigger restore at least once, even if it feels like a chore. Restoring a whole folder, rather than a single file, teaches you things a single-file test cannot: how long a restore takes, how much space you need, and whether your tool restores files to their original places or to a new folder you must sort out. Knowing that a full restore takes three hours changes your plans in a good way, because you will not promise your teacher or boss that everything will be back in ten minutes. Practise on a quiet evening, not the night before a deadline, and write down the steps you took. Those notes become your recovery guide, and your future self will be grateful for them.

Make restore testing a regular habit, not a one-time event. Test a file every few months, and test again after any big change: a new computer, a new backup tool, a new cloud account, or a major system update. Each change can break something that used to work, and the only way to know is to test. A tested backup is a quiet kind of confidence. You stop worrying about your files, because you have proof, not just hope, that you can get them back. That confidence is the real product of this section, and it costs you about fifteen minutes, a few times a year.

test_file = "restore_test.txt"
with open(test_file, "w") as f:
    f.write("If you can read this after restoring, your backup works.")
print("Test file created:", test_file)

Section 10: Version History and Deleted Files

Version history is the feature that saves you when the problem is not a dead drive but a bad edit. Imagine you spend a week writing an essay, then on Friday you rewrite a section badly and save over the good version. The file still exists, so a simple backup made on Friday night would copy the bad version too. Version history solves this by keeping older copies of a file from earlier times, so you can step back to Thursday's version, or Wednesday's, and recover the wording you lost. Many cloud services, office programs, and backup tools keep versions automatically, and learning to use them is one of the highest-value skills in this chapter, because bad edits happen far more often than drive failures.

Deleted files have a similar safety net, but only for a limited time. When you delete a file, most systems do not destroy it immediately. On a computer it often goes to a recycle bin or trash folder, where it sits until you empty it. In a cloud service it often goes to a trash area kept for thirty days or so. In a backup system, deleted files may be kept for as long as your retention settings say. The key word in all of this is time: these safety nets expire. A file deleted six months ago is usually gone from trash, and a file deleted a year ago may be gone from backups too, depending on your settings. When you realise you deleted something important, act quickly, check the trash first, then your backup versions, and do not save lots of new files to the same drive first, because new data can overwrite the space where the deleted file still sits.

You should check what version history your tools actually offer before you need it. Open a document you use often, look for a version history or previous versions option, and try restoring an older version of a test file. Note how far back versions go and how many are kept. Some tools keep every version forever, others keep versions for thirty days, and some only keep versions while the file stays in a synced folder. Knowing your limit helps you make decisions: if versions expire after thirty days, you should back up finished work to a separate archive folder as well, so a mistake discovered two months later is still recoverable.

Version history also teaches a good working habit: save milestones deliberately. When you finish a major section of a project, save a copy with the date in its name, such as essay-2026-10-20, in addition to the working file. That dated copy is a version you control, independent of any tool's retention rules. It costs almost nothing and it has saved countless students and workers from the pain of a bad edit. Combine tool version history with your own dated copies, and you have two safety nets for the most common kind of loss there is: not a disaster, just a human mistake on an ordinary day.

versions = ["essay-2026-10-18.docx", "essay-2026-10-20.docx", "essay-2026-10-22.docx"]
print("Available versions:", len(versions))
print("Latest good version:", versions[-1])

Section 11: Backing Up a Phone

Your phone probably holds more of your life than your computer does: photos, messages, contacts, notes, and app data, much of it created on the phone and stored nowhere else. Yet phones are the devices people back up least, because they feel less like computers and because their backup settings are buried in menus. The first step is to find your phone's backup setting and switch it on. On most phones this is in the settings under backup, cloud, or your account, and it can copy contacts, messages, photos, app data, and settings to your cloud account automatically, usually when the phone is charging and on Wi-Fi. Switch it on, connect to Wi-Fi, plug in the charger, and let the first backup finish. That first run can take a while if you have years of photos, so be patient and leave the phone alone overnight.

Photos deserve special attention, because they are usually the largest and most precious thing on a phone. Most phones offer a photo backup option that uploads pictures and videos to a cloud service as you take them. Turn this on, and check that it is actually uploading by looking at the service from another device, such as a computer browser, and confirming that recent photos appear there. Also check the storage limit: many free accounts include only a small amount of space, and when it fills up, backups quietly stop. If your photos stop uploading, the usual cause is a full account, not a broken phone. You can free space by deleting things you do not need, pay for more space if you can afford it, or copy photos to a computer or external drive as a second copy, which is good practice anyway.

Messages and contacts are smaller but easy to lose in a confusing way. Contacts usually sync with your account and reappear when you sign in on a new phone, but only if sync was actually on. Messages are more fragile: some phones back them up to the cloud, others do not, and switching between phone brands can lose message history entirely. If your messages matter to you, check what your phone backs up, and for truly important conversations, such as agreements or receipts, take screenshots and save them with your photos, so they are backed up with everything else. The same applies to notes and voice memos: check whether your notes app syncs, and if it does not, export important notes to files you control.

Finally, prepare for the day you lose the phone itself. Write down, somewhere safe, which account your phone backs up to and what your recovery options are, so you can sign in on a replacement. Practise the restore in a small way: sign in to your cloud account from a computer and confirm your photos and contacts are visible there. A phone backup is only real if you can reach it without the phone, because the whole point is surviving the loss of the phone. Once that is true, a stolen or broken phone becomes an annoying expense and a trip to a shop, not the loss of years of memories.

phone_backup = {"photos": True, "contacts": True, "messages": "check settings"}
for item, status in phone_backup.items():
    print(item, "->", status)

Section 12: Backing Up a Computer Step by Step

Backing up a computer sounds technical, but it is really a short list of decisions followed by one long wait while files copy. Start by deciding what to back up, using the priority list from section five: your documents, photos, school or work files, and anything irreplaceable. Then decide where the backup will go: an external drive you own, a cloud service, or both, which is best. If you are using an external drive, plug it in and check it has enough free space. If you are using a built-in backup tool, open it and look at its settings before you press start, because the default settings sometimes skip folders stored outside the usual documents and pictures locations, and your important files might live somewhere unusual.

Next, run the first backup and let it finish without interrupting it. The first backup is always the slowest, because it copies everything, and for a computer full of photos it can take hours. That is normal. Plug the laptop into power, connect to a stable network if you are using the cloud, and do something else. Do not cancel halfway because it seems slow, and do not unplug the drive mid-copy, because an interrupted backup can leave a half-written copy that looks complete but is not. When the tool says the backup is complete, do not just believe it: open the backup and look inside. Check that your most important folders are there and that a few files open correctly. That five-minute check is the difference between a backup and a guess.

After the first backup, set the schedule and let automation take over, as section eight explained. Daily backups for documents, weekly for a full system image if your tool offers one, and a reminder to check the backup status monthly. A system image is a complete copy of everything, including the operating system and programs, which lets you restore a computer to a working state quickly after a drive failure. It is larger and slower than a file backup, so not everyone needs one, but if you have the space, a monthly system image plus daily file backups is a strong combination. If space is tight, file backups of your own data matter far more than a system image, because programs can be reinstalled but your files cannot be recreated.

Finally, keep a simple written record: what tool you used, where the backup lives, how often it runs, and how to restore a file. Store that note somewhere you can reach without the computer, such as on paper or in your phone. In an emergency you may be stressed and working on an unfamiliar replacement machine, and a short note turns a confusing recovery into a series of steps you can follow. Backing up a computer is not a single heroic act. It is a setup you do once, a schedule that runs itself, and a monthly glance to confirm it is still working. Do those three things and your computer's files are safer than most people's.

Section 13: Photos and Memories First

Photos are different from other files, because they are memories, not just data. A lost essay can be rewritten, badly and painfully, but a lost photo of a grandmother who has died cannot be recreated at any price. That is why photos deserve the first and most careful backup in any plan, and why this section treats them separately from documents. If you do nothing else after reading this chapter, back up your photos. Everything else can follow, but photos are the files people regret losing most, and the files they most often assume are safe because they can see them on their phone screen. Seeing a photo is not the same as having a copy of it somewhere else.

The best photo backup plan is boring and redundant: an automatic cloud upload from your phone, plus a regular copy to a computer or external drive. The cloud copy protects you if the phone is lost or stolen. The local copy protects you if the cloud account is locked, the service changes, or your internet is too slow to download everything in a hurry. Copying photos to a drive is simple: connect the phone or use a computer, copy the photo folder, and keep the drive somewhere safe. Do this monthly at least, and more often after big events like holidays, weddings, or a baby's first year, when dozens of irreplaceable pictures are taken in a few days.

Organisation matters as much as copying, because a backup of ten thousand photos with no order is hard to use. You do not need a perfect system. Sorting photos into folders by year, and by event within the year, is enough. Keep original files, not only edited or compressed versions sent through messaging apps, because messaging apps often shrink photos and strip detail. If someone sends you a precious photo through a chat app, save the original file, not a screenshot of it, and back that up too. Also keep an eye on duplicates: backing up the same photos three times wastes space, but a few duplicates are harmless, while a missing photo is not. When in doubt, keep the copy.

Finally, think about who else should have copies. Sharing an album with family is not only kind, it is also a form of backup: if your copy is lost, a sibling or parent may still have theirs. Shared family albums, printed photos of the most important pictures, and a drive kept at a relative's house all add layers of safety that no single service can provide. Print the handful of photos that matter most, because a printed photo survives dead drives, dead accounts, and dead formats. Photos are the reason most people start caring about backups, and they are the reason backups are worth doing properly. Protect them first, protect them twice, and check that the copies are real.

Section 14: Documents, Work and School Files

Documents, work files, and schoolwork have a special property: they change often, they have deadlines attached, and losing them costs not just the file but the time and stress of recreating it under pressure. A student who loses an essay the night before it is due faces a different kind of disaster from someone who loses old photos. The essay can be rewritten, but not well, not calmly, and not before the deadline. That is why documents need frequent, automatic backup, ideally continuous or daily, rather than a monthly copy to a drive. The more often a file changes, the more often it should be backed up, and documents change more than almost anything else you own.

The simplest strong setup for documents is to keep them in a folder that backs up automatically, whether that is a cloud-synced folder with version history or a folder covered by your computer's backup tool. Working directly in such a folder means every save is protected within minutes, without you remembering anything. Add version history to that, and a bad edit becomes a minor annoyance instead of a crisis, because you can step back to yesterday's version. Also make dated milestone copies of major work: when you finish a draft, save a copy with the date in the name in a separate archive folder. That habit costs seconds and has saved many students from the nightmare of a corrupted file on submission day.

School and work files also include things people forget to back up because they do not look like documents. Spreadsheets, presentations, PDFs of certificates, reference letters, application forms, and research notes all count. So do files stored inside apps, such as notes apps, design tools, or code editors, which sometimes keep data in places your backup tool does not cover by default. Once a term or so, make a list of the apps you use for important work and check where each one stores its data, and whether your backup includes that location. If an app stores data only on the device, export a copy regularly to a backed-up folder. A backup that covers your documents folder but misses your notes app is a backup with a hole in it.

Finally, protect finished work differently from work in progress. Finished essays, submitted applications, certificates, and completed projects should be archived: copied to your backup drive and your cloud account, and left alone. Work in progress should be backed up frequently and automatically. Both matter, but they need different habits, and mixing them up leads to either a bloated daily backup full of old files or a missing copy of the one file you need today. Keep it simple: an automatic folder for daily work, dated milestone copies for big steps, and an archive for finished work. That trio covers almost every document disaster a student or worker will face.

Section 15: Keeping Backups Safe from Ransomware

Ransomware is malicious software that locks your files by encrypting them, then demands payment for the key to unlock them. You will learn more about malware in the next chapter, but ransomware matters here because it specifically attacks backups. Attackers know that a victim with a good backup does not need to pay, so modern ransomware tries to find and destroy backups before locking the main files. It looks for attached external drives, network folders, and cloud accounts it can reach, and encrypts or deletes what it finds. A backup that is connected and reachable at the moment of attack is not safe from ransomware, no matter how recently it was made. This section is about making your backups hard for ransomware to reach.

The single most effective defence is the habit from section six: disconnect the backup after it finishes. A drive sitting unplugged in a drawer cannot be encrypted by software running on your computer, because the software cannot reach a drive that is not connected. The same logic applies to cloud backups with version history: even if ransomware encrypts your synced files, the cloud service keeps older versions you can restore, as long as the attacker cannot also delete those versions. That is why you should protect your backup accounts with strong passwords and multi-factor authentication, and why you should never store your backup account password in a file on the same computer it protects. If the attacker can sign in as you, your cloud backup is reachable too.

Another defence is keeping more than one backup generation. If ransomware encrypts today's files and today's backup, yesterday's backup on a disconnected drive is still clean. This is another reason the 3-2-1 rule matters: copies in different places, on different media, cannot all be reached by one attack at one moment. Be careful with network-attached storage that is always online and always visible to your computer, because ransomware can often reach that too. If you use such a device, look for a snapshot or versioning feature that keeps older copies the ransomware cannot easily overwrite, and keep an offline drive as well for the copies that matter most.

Finally, know the recovery rule in advance, so you can act calmly if it ever happens: do not pay the ransom if you can avoid it, because payment does not guarantee your files back and it funds more attacks. Instead, disconnect the infected device from the network, get help from a trusted technician or your school or workplace support team, wipe and rebuild the device if needed, and restore from your cleanest backup. That plan only works if a clean backup exists, which is why this section exists at all. Backups are your way out of a ransomware attack, but only if the ransomware could not reach them first. Disconnect, diversify, and protect the accounts, and your backups stay your escape route.

    return (not connected) and account_protected

print("Offline drive + protected account:", backup_is_safe(False, True))
print("Attached drive:", backup_is_safe(True, True))

Section 16: Encryption for Backups

Encryption scrambles your files so they can only be read by someone with the right key, usually your password. For backups, encryption matters because backups are copies of your private life sitting on drives and servers outside your direct control. A backup drive can be stolen from your bag. A cloud account can be breached. A drive you throw away or give to someone else can still hold readable files. If the backup is encrypted, the thief or finder gets scrambled data they cannot read, while you, with the key, can restore everything normally. Encryption does not make a backup safer from loss, but it makes it safer from other people, and that is a different and important kind of safety.

Many backup tools offer encryption as a checkbox, and you should switch it on for any backup that leaves your house, whether that is a drive you carry or a cloud account. The tool will ask you for a password or passphrase to protect the backup. Choose a strong one, different from your other passwords, and write it down somewhere safe that is not on the same drive, such as a password manager or a sealed note kept separately. This is the part people get wrong: an encrypted backup whose password you have forgotten is as useless as no backup at all, because nobody, not even the backup company, can unlock it for you. That is the honest trade-off of encryption. It protects you from everyone, including from a company trying to help you after you forget.

You do not need to understand the mathematics of encryption to use it well. You need to understand three practical rules. First, encrypt backups that leave your control, and consider encrypting all backups for consistency. Second, guard the key as carefully as the backup itself, because the key is the backup in a very real sense: without it, the files are noise. Third, test a restore from an encrypted backup, including typing in the key, before you rely on it, because a restore you have never practised with encryption is a restore you might fumble during a crisis. Practise once, calmly, and the real thing becomes routine.

There is one more consideration worth naming. If you back up someone else's files, or files belonging to your workplace or school, check whether they have rules about encryption and where backups may be stored. Some organisations require encryption, and some forbid storing work files in personal cloud accounts. Following those rules protects both you and the people whose data you hold. For your own personal files, the rule is simple: encrypt the copies that travel, keep the key safe, and test the restore. Encryption is not paranoia. It is the lock on the spare key's hiding place, and a spare key hidden in the open is not much of a spare.

Section 17: Labels, Dates and Finding Things Again

A backup is only useful if you can find what you need inside it when you are stressed and in a hurry. People lose backups not only to failed drives but to chaos: a drawer of unlabelled drives, a cloud account full of folders named "New folder", and backup files with names that mean nothing six months later. Organisation is not a luxury extra for tidy people. It is part of the backup itself, because a file you cannot find in time might as well not exist. The good news is that a simple, boring naming system works far better than a clever one, because a simple system is one you will actually keep using.

Start with dates. Put the date in the name of backup folders and milestone copies, in year-month-day order, such as 2026-10-30. That order sorts correctly on a computer, so your folders line up from oldest to newest without effort. Next, add a short plain description: what computer or phone the backup is from, and what is inside, such as "Laptop-photos-2026-10-30" or "Phone-full-2026-10-30". Avoid vague names like "Stuff", "Backup1", or "Final-final-really-final", which feel funny at the time and mean nothing later. If a backup spans multiple drives or files, number them clearly, such as "Part 1 of 3", so a missing part is obvious instead of silently absent.

Labels on physical drives matter just as much. A strip of tape and a pen, or a printed label, with the date, the device, and the contents, turns a drawer of mystery drives into a shelf you can use. Keep a matching note, on paper or in your phone, listing each drive, what it holds, and when it was last updated. That note is your map. If you ever need to restore, you will consult the map first instead of plugging in drives at random and hoping. Update the note each time you back up, which takes less than a minute and saves far more time later. Small habits like this are the difference between a backup plan and a pile of drives.

The same discipline applies inside a backup. Keep a similar folder structure in your backup as on your computer, so restoring is a matter of copying a folder back, not reconstructing your life from scattered pieces. Resist the urge to reorganise everything during a backup, because a backup is not the moment for a spring clean. Copy first, organise later, and keep the backup a faithful mirror of the original. When you can find a file in your backup as quickly as you can find it on your computer, your backup has passed an important test. Organisation is not about tidiness for its own sake. It is about making recovery fast when speed matters most.

Section 18: Backup Schedules That Fit Real Life

A backup schedule is a promise you make to your future self, and like any promise, it should be one you can actually keep. The perfect schedule that you abandon after two weeks protects nothing, while a modest schedule you follow for years protects everything that matters. The right schedule depends on how often your files change and how much loss you can tolerate. A writer who produces pages every day should back up daily, because losing a week's writing is painful. A person whose computer mostly holds old photos and rarely changes might back up monthly and lose almost nothing. The question is not what experts recommend in the abstract, but how much work you are willing to redo if the worst happens today.

A practical way to think about it is to ask: how much can I afford to lose? If the answer is one day's work, back up daily. If the answer is one week's work, back up weekly. If the answer is nothing, you need continuous backup, which copies files shortly after they change, and you should set that up if your tool offers it. Most people land on a mix: daily automatic backup of documents and active projects, weekly backup of everything else, and monthly copies to an offline drive. That mix is realistic because the daily part is automatic and invisible, the weekly part runs overnight, and the monthly part takes a few minutes of attention. Build the schedule around automation for the frequent parts and a calendar reminder for the parts that need you.

Be honest about your internet and your hardware too. A daily cloud backup of huge video files on a slow connection will never finish, and a schedule that never finishes is worse than a slower schedule that completes, because it gives false confidence. If your connection is slow, back up documents to the cloud daily and copy videos to a local drive weekly. If your computer is old and slow, schedule backups for the night, when you are not using it, so the backup does not fight you for the machine. A schedule that respects your real constraints is a schedule you will keep, and a schedule you keep is the only one that counts.

Finally, review your schedule twice a year. Lives change: a student graduates and starts work, a hobby becomes a business, a phone fills with a baby's photos. Each change alters what matters and how often it changes. A ten-minute review, asking what is new, what has grown, and whether the schedule still fits, keeps your backup plan matched to your life. Write the schedule down, put the monthly check in your calendar, and treat it like any other small appointment. Backups do not need heroics. They need a realistic rhythm, kept for years, and that rhythm is built one honest decision at a time.

Section 19: When a Drive Fails

Hard drives fail, and they usually fail without asking permission or giving much warning. Sometimes there are signs: strange clicking sounds, files that take a long time to open, error messages when saving, or a computer that suddenly runs very slowly. Sometimes there is no sign at all, and a drive that worked yesterday simply does not appear today. Both kinds of failure are normal, because drives are physical machines with moving parts or with memory cells that wear out over time. The important thing is not to panic, and not to make the failure worse by guessing. Your first job when a drive fails is to stop using it, because every further attempt to read or write can damage it more.

If the drive is your backup drive and your original files are safe on your computer, the failure is an inconvenience: buy a replacement drive, make a fresh backup, and carry on. If the drive is your main drive and your backup is good, the failure is also manageable: replace the drive, reinstall your system, restore your files, and you are back to work. The real crisis only comes when the failed drive holds the only copy of something important. That is why this chapter keeps repeating the same message about multiple copies. A failed drive is a normal event with a normal solution, but only if a second copy exists somewhere else.

When a drive fails and the files matter, resist the temptation to run random repair tools or open the drive yourself. Freezing a drive, tapping it, or opening its case are myths that usually destroy whatever chance of recovery remained. Professional data recovery services exist, and they work in clean rooms with specialised equipment, but they are expensive and they cannot guarantee success. They are worth considering for truly irreplaceable files, such as a business's only records or a family's only photos, and not worth it for files you can download or recreate. Before paying anyone, get a clear quote, check the company's reputation, and be honest with yourself about what the files are worth. Recovery is a last resort, not a backup plan.

The lesson to carry forward is simple and unglamorous: drives fail, backups are the answer, and recovery services are the expensive apology for not having one. After any drive failure, even a minor one, review your backup plan. Did you have a second copy? Was it current? Did the restore work smoothly? Fix whatever gap the failure revealed, and replace any drive that showed warning signs before it fails completely. A clicking drive is a drive telling you to back up today, not next week. Listen to it, and the next failure will be a story you tell calmly, not a crisis you relive.

Section 20: Recovering from Accidental Deletion

Accidental deletion is the most common recovery situation most people will ever face, and it is also the most forgiving, if you act quickly and calmly. The first place to look is the trash or recycle bin on your computer, or the trash folder in your cloud service. Deleted files usually sit there for a while before being permanently removed, and restoring from trash is as simple as selecting the file and choosing restore. Check there first, before trying anything cleverer, because the simplest solution is often the right one, and because every minute you spend on complicated tools is a minute in which the file's safety net might expire or its space might be overwritten by new files.

If the file is not in the trash, your backup is the next place to look. Open your backup, find the folder where the file lived, and look for the most recent version from before the deletion. If your backup keeps versions, you may find several copies from different dates, and you can choose the one you want. Restore it to a new location first, rather than overwriting anything, and open it to confirm it is the right file and complete. Only then put it back where it belongs. Restoring to a new location first is a small habit that prevents a second accident: if you restore the wrong version over the good one, you have created a new problem while solving the old one.

If the file is in neither trash nor backup, there are file recovery tools that can sometimes find deleted files still sitting on a drive, because deletion often only removes the directory entry, not the data itself, until new data overwrites it. This is why you should stop saving new files to that drive as soon as you realise something important is deleted. Recovery tools work best on your own computer, for your own files, and soon after deletion. They are not magic, they cannot recover files overwritten long ago, and you should only download such tools from reputable sources, because fake recovery tools are a common way malware spreads. If the file is truly irreplaceable and tools fail, a professional recovery service is the last option, with the cost warning from the previous section.

The deeper lesson is about prevention, not just recovery. Accidental deletion happens to everyone, so design your setup to forgive it: keep trash retention as long as your system allows, keep backups with version history, and make dated milestone copies of important work. After you recover a file, take five minutes to check why the safety nets worked or failed, and fix the gap. Did the trash empty too quickly? Did the backup skip that folder? A recovered file is a free lesson, and the cheapest time to learn it is right after the scare, while the memory is fresh and the motivation is real.

Section 21: Recovering After Ransomware

Recovering after ransomware is frightening, and the first thing to understand is that you should not face it alone or in a rush. Ransomware locks your files and demands payment, often with a countdown timer designed to make you panic. The timer is a pressure tactic, not a technical deadline you must obey. Your first steps are to disconnect the infected device from the internet and from any network, so the ransomware cannot spread to other devices or reach your cloud accounts, and to stop using the device for anything else. Do not pay immediately, do not delete the ransom note yet, because it may contain information a helper needs, and do not try to unlock files with random tools found online, because fake decryption tools are a common second attack.

Next, get help from someone trustworthy: your school or workplace IT team, a reputable computer repair shop, or a knowledgeable friend who understands security. Tell them honestly what happened, including what you clicked or downloaded, because that information helps them judge how far the infection spread and whether other devices are at risk. If the device belongs to your workplace or school, report it to them first, before doing anything else, because they may have their own recovery systems and because the infection may affect their network too. Trying to hide a workplace infection to avoid embarrassment can turn one infected computer into a whole office of locked files. Reporting quickly is responsible, not shameful, and IT teams deal with this regularly.

Once the infection is contained, the recovery path depends on your backups. If you have a clean backup on a disconnected drive or in a cloud service with version history, the usual route is to wipe the infected device completely, reinstall the operating system from trusted installation media, and restore your files from the backup, choosing versions from before the attack. Wiping sounds drastic, but it is the only way to be confident the ransomware is truly gone, because a half-cleaned system can hide a second infection. Restore carefully, checking that restored files open correctly, and change your important passwords from a clean device afterwards, because the attackers may have seen passwords stored on the infected machine.

If you have no clean backup, the situation is harder, and honesty matters more than hope. Some ransomware has free decryption tools published by security researchers for specific variants, and your helper can check reputable sources for your exact variant. For many modern variants, no free decryption exists, and the locked files cannot be recovered without the attacker's key. In that case, paying is still usually advised against, because payment does not guarantee decryption, it marks you as someone who pays, and it funds further attacks. The painful lesson is the one this chapter exists to teach: backups made before the attack, kept out of the attacker's reach, are the only reliable way out. Set them up now, while you are safe, so this section stays theory for you rather than a manual you read in tears.

Section 22: Recovering After Theft or Fire

Theft and fire are the disasters that prove why an off-site backup matters, because they destroy the computer and everything sitting next to it in one event. If your laptop is stolen from your home along with the backup drive in the same drawer, you have lost both copies at once, and no backup software can help you. But if your off-site copy is in the cloud or on a drive at a relative's house, the loss becomes manageable. The recovery steps are practical and, in the case of theft, include things beyond files, so this section walks through them in order, starting with safety and ending with getting your digital life back.

After a theft, report it to the police and to your insurer if you have one, and write down the device's serial number, make, and model if you know them, because those details help both reports. Then, from another device, sign in to your accounts and change your most important passwords, starting with your email, because email is often the key to resetting everything else. Use your device-tracking service to locate, lock, or remotely erase the stolen device if that service was switched on beforehand, but do not try to recover the device yourself or confront anyone. Remote erase is a trade-off: it protects your data but also removes the chance of tracking the device, so follow the guidance of your tracking service and the police rather than guessing.

After a fire or flood, the priority is safety first: do not enter a damaged building until it is declared safe, and do not plug in wet or heat-damaged electronics, because they can be dangerous and because powering them on can destroy data that a professional might otherwise have recovered. Once you are safe and have a replacement device, sign in to your cloud backup and start restoring, beginning with the files you need most urgently, such as identification documents, schoolwork, and work files. If your only backup was on a damaged drive, a professional recovery service may be able to help, but heat and water damage make recovery uncertain, which is again why distance between copies matters so much.

In both cases, the recovery is also a moment to rebuild better. Set up the new device with automatic backup from day one, before you have accumulated anything to lose. Keep the off-site copy current, keep a written record of your accounts and recovery options somewhere safe, and consider whether your most important documents, such as identity papers and certificates, should also exist as scanned copies in your encrypted backup. Theft and fire are rare for any one person, but they are common across a whole lifetime of device ownership. A plan made calmly in advance turns a devastating loss into a difficult week, and that difference is worth the small effort of keeping a copy somewhere else.

Section 23: A Simple Family Backup Plan

A family backup plan is a backup plan that works for people who are not interested in backup plans, which is most families. The goal is not to turn every family member into a security expert. It is to make sure that everyone's photos and important files survive the ordinary disasters of family life: a dropped phone, a spilled drink on a laptop, a child deleting a folder, a stolen bag on holiday. The plan should be simple enough to explain in five minutes, cheap enough to maintain, and automatic enough that it keeps working when nobody is thinking about it. If the plan needs weekly attention from a busy parent, it will fail, and a failed plan is worse than a modest plan that runs.

Start with the phones, because that is where most family photos live. Switch on cloud photo backup on every phone, and check that each phone has enough storage space in its account. For children or older relatives, help them set this up once, and write down which account each phone backs up to, because a backup to an account nobody can sign in to is not a backup. Next, set up one shared external drive for the household, and copy the family's photos and important documents to it monthly. Label the drive, keep it somewhere safe but not next to the main computer, and consider keeping a second copy at a relative's house for the photos that matter most. One drive, one monthly habit, and automatic phone backups cover most families well.

Teach the family two skills, and only two, to begin with: how to check that a backup worked, and how to restore a single deleted photo. Show them on their own phone, with their own photos, so the skill is real and not abstract. When a child deletes a photo by accident and a parent calmly restores it from the cloud in two minutes, the whole family learns that backups are useful, not scary. That small success is worth more than a lecture. Also agree on a simple rule for shared devices: nobody deletes files from a shared computer or drive without asking, and important files live in a folder that everyone knows is backed up, so nothing precious hides in a random download folder.

Finally, review the plan together once or twice a year, perhaps when the clocks change or at the start of a school term. Check that backups are still running, that accounts are not full, and that the external drive still works. Update the written note of accounts and drives as phones and computers change. A family plan is not a technical project, it is a household habit, like locking the door at night. Keep it simple, keep it automatic, keep the note up to date, and the family's memories will survive the accidents that family life guarantees.

Section 24: Backups for Small Businesses

A small business loses more than files when it loses data. It loses customer records, invoices, stock information, and the trust of people who expect it to be reliable. A shop that cannot tell a customer what they ordered, or a clinic that cannot find a patient's records, is in trouble even if the computers are eventually fixed. That is why business backups need a little more structure than personal backups, even for a one-person business. The principles are the same, copies in more than one place, tested restores, and protection from ransomware, but the stakes and the responsibilities are higher, because other people's data is involved as well as your own.

Start by listing what the business cannot operate without: customer and order records, financial records, stock or inventory data, and any work created for clients. Back those up daily and automatically, with an off-site copy, because a fire or burglary at the shop should not also destroy the business's records. Keep a written backup record too: what is backed up, where the copies live, how often they run, and who is responsible for checking them. If only one person knows how the backup works and that person is ill or on holiday when disaster strikes, the backup might as well not exist. Write it down, and make sure at least one other trusted person can follow the restore steps.

Business backups also carry legal and privacy responsibilities. Customer data, health information, and financial records may be covered by laws or contracts that require protection, encryption, and limits on where data may be stored. Storing customer records in a personal cloud account without encryption, or on an unlabelled drive that anyone can pick up, can be a breach of those responsibilities even if nothing bad ever happens. Use business-appropriate services, encrypt backups that leave your premises, limit who can access them, and keep backups only as long as you need them. If you are unsure what rules apply to your business, ask a local business adviser or a professional association, because guessing about other people's data is not good enough.

Finally, practise recovery as a business, not just as an individual. Once or twice a year, run a restore drill: pretend the main computer is gone, and restore the essential records to a spare machine or a clean folder, then check that the business could actually operate from them. Time the drill, note what went wrong, and fix it. A drill that reveals a gap is a success, because it found the gap on a calm day instead of during a real crisis. For a small business, a backup plan is not an IT expense. It is part of being a business people can trust, and trust, once lost to a data disaster, is far harder to restore than any file.

for item in business_essentials:
    print("Back up daily:", item)

Section 25: Checking Backup Health with Python

You can use a small Python program to check the health of your own backup, which is a good way to practise both programming and defensive habits at the same time. The idea is simple: write a program that looks at a folder of original files on your computer and a folder that holds their backup copies, and reports which files are missing from the backup, which are present, and how many of each. This is a read-only check, it changes nothing, and it runs only on your own files and folders. It does not scan anyone else's computer, it does not touch the network, and it does not collect passwords or secrets. It is a personal audit tool, and building it teaches you to verify rather than assume, which is one of the most valuable habits in security.

The program below uses only the Python standard library, which means it needs no extra installation. It lists the files in two folders, compares their names, and prints a short report. You can adapt it to your own folders by changing the two folder paths to places on your own machine. Run it after a backup and read the report carefully. If files are missing, find out why: were they in a subfolder the backup tool skipped, were they created after the backup ran, or did the backup fail partway through? Each missing file is a clue, not a reason to panic, and fixing the cause is better than copying the file by hand once and leaving the gap for next time.

A health check like this is most useful when run regularly. You could run it weekly, after your scheduled backup, and keep a short log of the results. Over time, the log shows patterns: a folder that is always missing, a backup that always runs late, a drive that is slowly filling up. Patterns like these are how small problems get caught before they become disasters. You can extend the program gradually, for example by also checking file sizes, which catches copies that are present but truncated, or by counting files in subfolders. Each extension keeps the same rule: read-only, your own files only, and a clear report you can act on.

Building your own check also teaches you something important about security tools in general: the best tool is one whose output you understand and act on. A commercial backup tool with a green tick you never question is less useful than a simple script whose report you read every week. As you work through later chapters, you will build more checks of this kind, and they will grow into a personal security audit toolkit. Start here, with a backup health check, because backups are the safety net under everything else, and a safety net you inspect is one you can trust.

import os

originals = set(os.listdir("my_documents"))
backups = set(os.listdir("my_backup"))
missing = sorted(originals - backups)
print("Original files:", len(originals))
print("Backed up files:", len(originals & backups))
print("Missing from backup:", missing)

Section 26: Copying Files Safely with Python

Copying files sounds trivial, but copying them safely, so you know the copy is complete and correct, is a skill worth learning. A drag-and-drop copy that is interrupted halfway can leave a file that looks finished but is missing its ending, and a copy made to a full drive can silently fail for the last few files. Python's standard library includes a module called shutil, short for shell utilities, which can copy files and folders, and it is a good tool for writing your own small backup script for your own files. Writing such a script teaches you exactly what a backup tool does underneath, and it gives you a backup method you fully understand, which is valuable even if you normally use a commercial tool.

The key idea is to copy, then verify. Copying alone is only half the job, and verification is the habit that separates a careful backup from a hopeful one. After copying, your script can compare the list of files in the original folder with the list in the backup folder, and report any differences. It can also compare file sizes, which catches copies that stopped early. In the next sections you will learn about hashes, which are an even stronger check, but size and presence checks already catch the most common failures. A script that copies and then reports is better than a script that copies and says nothing, because silence after a backup is not evidence of success.

Safety also means not overwriting things you did not mean to overwrite. A careless copy script that copies a folder over another folder can destroy the very backup it was meant to create. Good practice is to copy into a new folder named with today's date, so each backup run creates a separate snapshot instead of overwriting the last one. That way, a bad run cannot destroy a good backup from yesterday. Also, never write a script that deletes original files after copying them unless you have verified the copies thoroughly and you fully understand what you are doing. Moving files is not backing them up, as section one explained, and an automatic script that moves instead of copying can destroy your only copy with great efficiency.

Finally, keep your backup script simple and readable, and test it on test folders first, not on your real photos. Create two small folders with a few harmless text files, run your script, and check the results. When it works on test folders, point it at a copy of a real folder, not the original, and check again. Only when you trust it should you use it for real backups, and even then, keep your other backup methods running alongside it. A home-made script is a learning tool and a useful second method, not a reason to abandon your main backup. Build it carefully, verify its output, and let it deepen your understanding of what backup tools do for you every day.

import shutil
import os

os.makedirs("backup_test", exist_ok=True)
shutil.copy("my_note.txt", "backup_test/my_note.txt")
print("Copied:", os.path.exists("backup_test/my_note.txt"))

Section 27: Listing What Changed with Python

Knowing what changed since your last backup is useful for two reasons. First, it tells you whether your backup is actually keeping up: if you edited ten files today and your backup only copied two, something is wrong. Second, it helps you understand your own work: a list of recently changed files is often a good summary of what you have been doing, and it can help you find a file you edited but forgot to save in the right place. Python can list files and their modification times using only the standard library, and writing a small program to do this is a safe, read-only exercise on your own folders that teaches you how backup tools decide what to copy in an incremental backup.

The program below walks through a folder, reads each file's last modification time, and prints the files changed in the last day. The modification time is a piece of information your operating system keeps for every file, recording when it was last written. Backup tools use this information, among other things, to decide which files are new or changed and therefore need copying. Understanding this helps you understand why a file that you only opened and read, without changing, is not copied again: its modification time did not change, so the backup tool correctly leaves it alone. It also explains a subtle trap: if a file's modification time is changed without its contents changing, a backup tool may copy it unnecessarily, which wastes time but does no harm.

You can adapt this idea into a personal changed-files report that you run before a backup. If the report shows files you do not recognise, investigate before backing up, because an unexpected changed file can be a sign of a problem, such as a program writing where it should not, or another person using your computer. If the report shows nothing changed on a day you did a lot of work, check whether your work was saved somewhere your report does not look, such as inside an app's private folder. Either way, the report turns a vague feeling about your files into concrete information you can act on, which is the essence of defensive computing: look, check, and act on evidence rather than assumptions.

Keep the scope of this tool strictly to your own folders. Do not point it at system folders you do not understand, and do not use it to examine other people's files or shared computers without permission. The point of the exercise is to learn how your own backup works and to keep your own files safe. Run it on a test folder first, confirm the output matches what you expect, and then use it on your real documents folder. Small, honest tools like this, used regularly, build the habit of checking that will serve you throughout this book and throughout your computing life.

import os
import time

folder = "my_documents"
now = time.time()
for name in os.listdir(folder):
    path = os.path.join(folder, name)
    age_days = (now - os.path.getmtime(path)) / 86400
    if age_days < 1:
        print("Changed today:", name)

Section 28: Verifying Copies with Hashes

A hash is a short fingerprint computed from a file's contents. Change even one letter in a large document, and its hash changes completely. Copy a file perfectly, and the copy has exactly the same hash as the original. That property makes hashes the strongest simple way to verify a backup: compute the hash of the original and the hash of the copy, and if they match, the copy is identical, byte for byte. If they differ, something went wrong during copying, and you should copy again and check again. Hashes do not tell you what changed, only that something did, but for verification purposes, that is exactly what you need to know.

Python's standard library includes a module called hashlib, which can compute hashes for your own files. The program below reads a file in small pieces, so it works even for large files without loading the whole file into memory at once, and prints its hash. Run it on an original file and on its backup copy, and compare the two outputs. If you want to check many files, you can extend the program to walk through a folder, compute a hash for each file, and save the results to a list. Later, after a backup, compute the hashes again and compare the lists. Any file whose hash changed unexpectedly deserves investigation, because files do not change their own contents without a reason.

Hashes are also useful for detecting accidental corruption over time. Storage media can develop small errors, and a file that sat on a drive for years can slowly degrade. If you keep a list of hashes from when the files were fresh, you can re-check them yearly and discover corruption before it spreads to your backups. This is sometimes called a checksum audit, and it is a quiet, powerful habit for people who keep photos or archives for decades. It costs little, it runs in the background, and it gives you evidence about the health of your files that simply opening a few of them cannot provide.

One caution is important: a hash verifies that a copy matches an original, but it does not verify that the original was good in the first place. If you back up a corrupted file and hash both copies, the hashes will match perfectly, because both copies are equally corrupted. That is why verification should be combined with the other habits in this chapter: keep multiple generations, keep an off-site copy, and open important files from time to time to confirm they are actually readable. A hash is a strong tool, but it is one tool among several, and the combination of tools is what makes a backup trustworthy.

import hashlib

def file_hash(path):
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(path, "rb") as f:
        for chunk in iter(lambda: f.read(8192), b""):
            h.update(chunk)
    return h.hexdigest()

print(file_hash("my_note.txt"))

Section 29: Backing Up Settings and Lists

When people think about backups, they think about files they can see: documents, photos, and videos. But a computer also holds settings and lists that are invisible until they are gone: Wi-Fi passwords, browser bookmarks, app preferences, email account settings, and the custom setup of programs you use every day. Losing these does not destroy your creative work, but it can cost you days of tedious reconfiguration, and for some people it means losing access to services they cannot easily recover. Backing up settings is less dramatic than backing up photos, but it makes recovery after a lost device far smoother, because a restored computer that feels like your computer is much easier to get back to work on.

Many settings already back up automatically if you use an account with your device or browser. Signing in to a browser, for example, often syncs bookmarks, history, and saved passwords across devices, and signing in to a phone often restores Wi-Fi networks, app layouts, and accessibility settings. Check what your accounts actually sync, and switch on the options that matter to you, but remember the warning from earlier sections: sync is not backup. If you delete a bookmark on one device, sync may delete it everywhere. For settings that truly matter, such as a list of important bookmarks or a record of your accounts, keep a separate copy you control, such as an exported bookmarks file stored in your backed-up documents folder.

Some settings deserve a manual export from time to time. Most browsers can export bookmarks to a small file. Many apps can export their data or settings. Your password manager, if you use one, can usually export an encrypted backup, which you should store securely and never as a plain text file. Make a habit of exporting these things when you set up a new device or change an important setting, and store the exports with your other backups. Also keep a simple written list of your important accounts and services, without passwords, so that after a loss you know what needs to be signed in to again. That list alone can save hours of trying to remember which services you used.

Finally, test the restore of settings, just as you test the restore of files. When you get a new device, sign in and see what comes back automatically, and what you have to redo by hand. Note the gaps, and adjust your exports for next time. A backup plan that restores your files but leaves you spending a week reconfiguring everything is only half a plan. Settings and lists are the small details that make a computer yours, and including them in your backup turns a stressful rebuild into a short, familiar setup. They take minutes to export and save days of frustration later.

settings_to_export = ["browser bookmarks", "password manager (encrypted export)",
                      "email account list", "app preferences"]
for item in settings_to_export:
    print("Export and back up:", item)

Section 30: Email and Contacts Backups

Email and contacts are unusual backups, because most people do not store them as files on their own computer at all. They live in an online account, run by a company, and accessed from a phone or browser. That feels safe, because the company surely backs up its own servers, and in a sense it does. But the company's backup protects the company from its own disasters, not you from losing access to your account. If your account is locked, hacked, or closed, or if you accidentally delete years of messages, the company's servers will not give you your mail back just because you ask nicely. Your email backup is your responsibility, even when the mail itself lives in the cloud.

The most important email backup habit is also the simplest: keep a copy of the messages that matter outside the mailbox itself. For most people, that means saving important emails, such as receipts, contracts, acceptance letters, and legal correspondence, as files or PDFs in a backed-up folder, or forwarding them to a second account you control. You do not need to back up every newsletter and notification. You need the messages you would miss if the account disappeared tomorrow. Go through your mailbox once, identify the categories that matter, and set up a habit of saving those as they arrive, rather than trying to export ten years of mail in one painful weekend.

Contacts deserve similar treatment. Most phones sync contacts to an account, and that sync usually restores them to a new phone. But sync can also delete contacts if an account is merged badly or a phone is set up wrong, and a contact list that exists only as a sync is a single point of failure. Export your contacts to a file from time to time, most phones and email services offer an export option, and store that file with your other backups. An exported contacts file is small, it costs nothing to keep, and it turns a lost phone from a social disaster, in which you lose everyone's number, into a minor inconvenience.

Finally, protect the account itself as part of the backup plan. Use a strong, unique password for your email, switch on multi-factor authentication, and keep your recovery options, such as a recovery email address and phone number, up to date. Test the recovery process before you need it, by checking that the recovery options shown in your account settings are ones you can still reach. An email account with a forgotten password and an old recovery phone number is an account you may never get back, and with it goes every message and contact inside. Back up the important messages, export the contacts, and guard the account, and your email becomes a reliable part of your life rather than a fragile one.

Section 31: Browser Bookmarks and Passwords

Your browser holds a small map of your online life: bookmarks to sites you use, saved passwords for accounts, browsing history, and extensions that change how pages behave. Losing that map does not destroy your work, but it can feel surprisingly disorienting, like moving to a new town without an address book. You suddenly cannot remember the name of the site where you pay a bill, or the forum where you asked a question, or the password for an account you created years ago. Backing up browser data is therefore a small task with an outsized payoff, because it restores familiarity as well as function when you move to a new device.

Most browsers offer account sync, which copies bookmarks, passwords, and settings to the company's servers and restores them when you sign in on a new device. Switch this on if you trust the provider and use a strong account password with multi-factor authentication, because sync without account security is a risk, not a convenience. But do not rely on sync alone. Export your bookmarks to a file from time to time, most browsers offer this in their bookmarks manager, and store that file in your backed-up documents folder. An exported bookmarks file is tiny, readable, and independent of any company, which means it survives an account lock or a decision to switch browsers. For passwords, use a dedicated password manager rather than relying only on the browser, and keep an encrypted export of the password manager as described in section twenty-nine.

Be careful about what browser data you back up and where you store it. Saved passwords are sensitive, and an unencrypted export of them is a treasure chest for anyone who finds it. Never store a plain-text password export on an unencrypted drive, in a cloud folder shared with others, or as an email attachment. If you export passwords at all, encrypt the export, store it securely, and delete the plain-text file after encrypting it. The same caution applies to browsing history, which can reveal a great deal about you. You do not need to back up history at all, and for privacy reasons, you probably should not. Back up bookmarks and passwords, carefully, and leave history behind.

When you set up a new device, restoring browser data is a good test of your whole backup approach. Sign in, check that bookmarks and passwords return, and fill any gaps from your exported file. Note what did not come back, such as extensions or custom settings, and adjust your exports for next time. A browser that feels familiar on a new device makes the whole recovery feel manageable, which matters more than it sounds when you are already stressed by a lost or broken machine. Small, careful backups of browser data are a kindness to your future self, and they take only a few minutes to set up.

Section 32: Backups and Privacy

Backups protect your files from loss, but they also create new copies of your private life, and every new copy is a new place your private information could be exposed. This is a real trade-off, and ignoring it leads to careless backups: unencrypted drives left in cars, cloud accounts full of sensitive documents with weak passwords, and old backup drives given away without being wiped. A good backup plan protects you from loss and from exposure at the same time, and that means thinking about who could reach each copy, not only about whether the copy exists. Privacy is not the opposite of backup. It is part of doing backup properly.

The first privacy rule is to encrypt backups that contain sensitive information, especially backups that leave your home, as section sixteen explained. The second rule is to limit what you back up to sensitive places. Not every file needs to go to every backup destination. Your most private documents, such as medical records or financial statements, might belong on an encrypted external drive you keep at home, rather than in a cloud account you access from many devices. Your photos might belong in both places. Making these decisions deliberately, file by file or folder by folder, is better than backing up everything everywhere by default and hoping for the best. A backup plan should reflect what you actually want to protect and from whom.

The third rule concerns old backups. When you replace a drive, give one away, or throw one out, wipe it first, because deleting files does not truly erase them, as section twenty explained. Use your operating system's secure erase or drive-wiping tool, or if the drive is broken and cannot be wiped, destroy it physically before disposal. The same applies to old phones and computers you sell or give away: back them up, sign out of all accounts, and reset them to factory settings before they leave your hands. A backup drive you forgot in a drawer is a privacy risk only if someone finds it, but a drive you gave away with readable files on it is a privacy incident you created yourself.

Finally, review who has access to your backups. Shared cloud folders, family accounts, and workplace backup systems all involve other people, and that is fine, as long as it is deliberate. Check the sharing settings on your cloud backups occasionally, remove people who no longer need access, and use separate accounts for personal and shared backups where possible. Privacy in backups is not about secrecy for its own sake. It is about making sure that the copies you made to protect yourself do not become the way your private life leaks to strangers. Encrypt, choose destinations deliberately, wipe old drives, and check sharing, and your backups will protect your privacy as well as your files.

    print("-", rule)

Section 33: Old Backups and When to Retire Them

Backups do not last forever, and keeping every backup you have ever made is neither practical nor wise. Drives fill up, cloud storage costs money, and old backups accumulate like old newspapers, taking up space and making it harder to find the current copy. But deleting old backups carelessly is also risky, because an old backup may be the only copy of a file you deleted years ago and only now realise you need. Retiring backups is therefore a decision, not a reflex, and it deserves a simple policy you can follow without agonising over every file. The goal is to keep what is useful, in a form you can find, and let go of the rest with confidence.

A sensible retention policy keeps recent backups generously and older backups sparingly. For example, keep daily backups for the last month, weekly backups for the last year, and monthly or yearly snapshots beyond that. That pattern, sometimes called grandfather-father-son in professional circles, gives you fine detail for recent mistakes, which are the ones you most often need to undo, and a coarser record for older history, which you rarely need in detail. You do not need to implement this perfectly. The principle is enough: recent copies in detail, older copies as milestones, and a clear rule for when a copy can be deleted. Write your rule down, so you are not deciding from scratch each time you run out of space.

Before deleting any old backup, check two things. First, does it contain anything that exists nowhere else, such as photos from a particular year or a finished project you never copied elsewhere? If so, copy those unique files to your current archive before deleting the old backup. Second, is the backup still readable? An old drive that has sat in a drawer for five years may no longer work, in which case deleting it loses nothing, but you should discover that by testing it, not by assuming. If the drive still works and holds unique files, copy those files to a current drive first. Retiring a backup should never be the moment you discover it was your only copy of something precious.

Finally, retire the hardware as well as the data. Old drives, memory cards, and discs degrade, and a backup on a dying medium is a backup on borrowed time. When you replace a drive, copy its contents to the new one, verify the copy, wipe the old drive as section thirty-two explained, and then dispose of it. Keep a note of what you retired and when, so your backup map stays accurate. A tidy backup collection, with current copies you trust and old copies retired deliberately, is far more useful than a mountain of ageing drives nobody dares to touch. Retire with a policy, check for unique files first, and your backups will stay manageable for years.

retention = {"daily": "last month", "weekly": "last year", "monthly": "beyond a year"}
for level, period in retention.items():
    print(level, "backups kept for:", period)

Section 34: Two Copies in Two Places

This section returns to a simple idea because simple ideas are the ones people forget under pressure: a backup is not real until a second copy exists in a second place. Two copies on the same drive are not two copies in any meaningful sense, because one drive failure destroys both. Two copies on two drives in the same bag are barely better, because one stolen bag destroys both. Two copies in two places, such as your home and a cloud account, or your home and a relative's house, are genuinely independent, because the disasters that destroy one are unlikely to destroy the other at the same time. Distance is the protection, not duplication alone.

Many people believe they have two copies when they do not. A file on a laptop and a file in a synced folder on the same laptop is one copy with a mirror, not two copies, because deleting the original deletes the mirror. A file on a laptop and a file on an external drive that is always plugged in and always in the same room is closer to two copies, but still shares the risks of fire, flood, and theft. A file on a laptop and a file on a drive stored in another building, or in a reputable cloud account, is two genuinely separate copies. When you audit your own backups, ask not how many copies you have, but how many independent disasters it would take to destroy all of them. If the answer is one, you have one copy, however many files you can count.

Building two-place protection does not have to be expensive or complicated. For most people, the second place is a cloud account, and the first place is a local drive. For people with slow internet or strong privacy concerns, the second place can be a drive kept at a trusted relative's house and updated a few times a year. The key is that the second place is real, current, and reachable without the first place. A drive at your sister's house that has not been updated since 2022 is a historical artefact, not a second place. Update it when you visit, or swap drives with her so each of you holds the other's recent backup, which is a neat, free arrangement that protects you both.

Test the independence of your copies occasionally. Pretend your main computer is gone, and try to restore an important file using only the second place, without touching the first. If you can, your two-place system works. If you cannot, you have found a gap while there is still time to fix it. Two copies in two places is the heart of the 3-2-1 rule, stripped down to its simplest form, and it is the standard against which every backup plan should be measured. Count places, not files, and you will never be fooled by a mirror pretending to be a backup.

copies = [("laptop", "home"), ("cloud account", "elsewhere")]
places = {place for _, place in copies}
print("Independent places:", len(places))

Section 35: Travel Backups

Travelling creates a perfect storm for losing files: you take more photos than usual, you carry your devices through unfamiliar places, and you are far from your home backup drive and your usual routines. Phones get stolen on holiday, laptops get damaged in transit, and memory cards fill up at exactly the wrong moment. A travel backup plan is therefore worth making before you leave, not after you return with a story about lost photos. The plan does not need to be elaborate. It needs to handle the three travel risks: theft, damage, and running out of space, and it needs to work with whatever internet you actually have where you are going.

Before you leave, make a full backup at home, so that even if everything you take is lost, you lose only what you created during the trip, not your whole digital life. Switch on automatic cloud photo upload on your phone, and check that it works on Wi-Fi, because hotel or café Wi-Fi is often how travel photos get backed up. Carry a small spare memory card or a portable drive if you use a camera, and copy photos to it each evening, keeping it in a different bag from the camera. That evening copy is a simple habit that has saved countless holidays: if the camera is stolen the next day, yesterday's photos are already on the drive in your other bag. Keep the drive and the camera apart, always, because together they share the same fate.

During the trip, back up when you have good Wi-Fi, and do not wait until the end. Upload the day's photos each evening if you can, or at least every few days, so a loss costs you days, not weeks. Be careful with public Wi-Fi when backing up, and prefer networks you trust, or your own mobile data for sensitive uploads, because the Wi-Fi safety rules from chapter fifteen apply here too. Also be careful with storage: if your cloud account fills up mid-trip, uploads stop silently, so check your available space before you leave and free up room or arrange more space in advance. A full account on day three of a two-week trip is a preventable problem.

After you return, finish the job promptly. Copy everything from your travel devices to your home backup, verify the copies, and then clear space on your phone and camera for normal life. Do not leave the only copies of holiday photos on a phone for months, because the phone will eventually be lost, broken, or replaced, and the trip will become a memory without pictures. Travel backups are a small discipline: back up before, copy each evening, upload when you can, and consolidate when you return. Follow it, and your travels will be remembered in photos, not in regrets.

Section 36: Backing Up Before an Update

Updates to your operating system, your phone, or a major app are usually good for security, as chapter ten explained, but any major change to a device carries a small risk of going wrong. An update can fail halfway, a device can run out of power during installation, or a new version can have a bug that affects your files. These failures are uncommon, but when they happen, they happen to people who were doing the right thing by updating. A backup before a major update is therefore a simple insurance policy: it costs a few minutes, it protects everything, and it lets you update with confidence instead of anxiety. Make it a rule: no major update without a fresh backup first.

The backup before an update does not need to be elaborate. For a phone, check that its cloud backup completed recently, or trigger one manually and wait for it to finish. For a computer, run your normal backup and confirm it completed, or copy your most important folders to an external drive if you do not have an automatic system. The key word is fresh: a backup from three months ago will not save work you did last week, and an update that goes wrong today affects today's files, not last season's. After the backup finishes, verify a file or two, as you have practised throughout this chapter, and only then start the update. That order, backup, verify, update, is the whole technique, and it is worth making automatic in your mind.

Also prepare the practical details before updating. Plug a laptop or phone into power, so it cannot run out of battery mid-update. Connect to stable Wi-Fi if the update downloads over the internet. Note down anything unusual about your setup, such as custom settings or apps you depend on, so you can check them after the update. If the update offers a choice between a quick update and a clean install, understand the difference: a clean install erases everything and starts fresh, which is sometimes the right choice for a troubled device, but only if your backup is complete and tested. Never choose a clean install on the strength of an untested backup.

After the update, check your files and your most important apps before declaring success. Open a few documents, look at your photos, and confirm your settings survived. If something is missing, you have a fresh backup to restore from, and restoring one file is a small job. If everything is fine, you have lost nothing but a few minutes, and you have gained the security improvements of the update. Backing up before an update is one of those habits that feels unnecessary every time it works, and priceless the one time it does not. Make it a rule, follow it every time, and updates become routine instead of risky.

Section 37: Restoring to a New Computer

Getting a new computer is exciting, but moving your digital life to it can be stressful if you have never done it deliberately. The good news is that a good backup makes this a routine job, not a crisis. Start by setting up the new computer with the basics: your accounts, your Wi-Fi, and your backup tool, signed in with the same account your backups belong to. Then restore in order of importance, not in alphabetical order: documents and work files first, because you probably need them soonest, then photos, then settings and bookmarks, then apps and everything else. Restoring in priority order means that even if the process is interrupted, the most important things are already back.

Resist the urge to restore everything at once and then sort it out later, because a full restore of years of files can take hours, and a single error early on can confuse the whole process. Instead, restore folder by folder, checking each one as you go. Open a few files from each folder to confirm they are complete and readable, and compare the number of files restored with the number in the backup, if your tool shows that information. If a folder restores incompletely, you will catch it while you still remember what you were doing, rather than discovering it months later when you need a specific file. Patience during a restore is a form of care for your own files.

You should also use a new computer as a chance to tidy up, but carefully. Do not delete files from the backup during the restore, because you may delete something you later realise you needed. Restore everything important to the new computer, keep the old backup intact, and only delete things from the new computer after you have lived with it for a while and are sure you do not need them. Keep the old computer, if you still have it, until the new one is fully set up and tested, because the old machine is itself a backup during the transition. Only when the new computer is working, with files verified and settings restored, should you wipe and dispose of the old one, following the privacy rules from section thirty-two.

Finally, set up automatic backup on the new computer immediately, before you accumulate new files on it. A new computer without backup is a new risk, and the first week with a new machine is exactly when you are creating and downloading things you would hate to lose. Switch on the backup tool, run the first backup, verify it, and write down where it lives, updating your backup note. A move to a new computer, done this way, is not just a transfer. It is a fresh start for your backup habits, and a chance to make them better than they were before.

Section 38: Partial Restores: One File, Not Everything

Most restores are not dramatic full-system recoveries. They are small, everyday rescues: one deleted essay, one overwritten spreadsheet, one photo a child deleted by accident. Knowing how to restore a single file, quickly and safely, is therefore the most useful restore skill you can learn, and it is the one you will use most often. The technique is simple, but it has a discipline to it: find the right version, restore it to a safe place first, check it, and only then put it where it belongs. That discipline prevents the common mistake of restoring the wrong version over the only good copy you have left.

Start by finding where the file lives in your backup. Open your backup tool or your backup folder, navigate to the folder where the file used to be, and look for it. If your backup keeps versions, you may see several copies from different dates. Choose the version from just before the problem happened, not necessarily the latest version, because the latest version may be the broken one. If you are unsure which version is right, restore two candidates to a temporary folder and compare them, opening each one and checking its contents. Taking two minutes to compare is far better than restoring the wrong version and discovering the mistake after you have overwritten something.

Restoring to a temporary location first is the key safety step, and it deserves emphasis because it feels like extra work when you are in a hurry. When you restore directly over the original location, you risk overwriting a file that still has value, such as a partially good version or a file that another program is using. When you restore to a temporary folder, you can open the restored file, confirm it is correct and complete, and then move it into place deliberately, keeping the old file as a spare until you are sure. This habit has saved many people from turning a small loss into a larger one, and it costs only a few extra clicks.

Finally, after a successful partial restore, take a moment to understand why the file needed restoring. Was it deleted accidentally, overwritten by a bad edit, or lost in a reorganisation? Each cause suggests a different prevention step: longer trash retention, more frequent version snapshots, or a clearer folder structure. A partial restore is a small event, but it is also a free lesson about your own habits, and learning from it makes the next incident less likely. Practise partial restores on test files, so that when a real one is needed, your hands know the steps and your mind stays calm. Small rescues, done well, are the everyday proof that your backup plan works.

Section 39: Backup Mistakes Beginners Make

Beginners make the same backup mistakes again and again, and naming them in advance is the best way to avoid them. The first mistake is assuming a backup exists without checking. Someone sets up a tool, sees a friendly message, and never looks again, only to discover months later that the tool stopped working, ran out of space, or was backing up the wrong folder. The cure is the monthly check from section eight: look at the backup status, open the backup, and confirm your important files are there. A backup you have never inspected is a hope, not a plan, and hopes do not restore files.

The second mistake is keeping the backup in the same place as the original, such as a backup drive that lives permanently on top of the computer, or a backup folder on the same hard drive as the files it protects. A single drive failure, fire, or theft then destroys both copies at once. The cure is distance, as section thirty-four explained: at least one copy somewhere else, whether that is a cloud account or a drive in another building. The third mistake is the opposite: making backups so complicated that nobody maintains them. A plan with five drives, three services, and a spreadsheet nobody updates will collapse under its own weight. The cure is simplicity: one automatic system, one offline copy, and a short written note. A plan you actually follow beats a perfect plan you abandon.

The fourth mistake is forgetting the account. A cloud backup protected by a password nobody remembers, or a recovery email nobody can access, is a locked vault with the key thrown away. The cure is to keep account recovery details current, use a password manager, and test signing in from another device occasionally. The fifth mistake is backing up everything except the one thing that matters, because it lived in an unusual place, such as inside an app, on a desktop folder the tool skipped, or on a second drive nobody thought of. The cure is the priority list from section five, checked against what your tool actually covers. Walk through your list, folder by folder, and confirm each one is included.

Finally, the most human mistake is postponement: planning to back up tomorrow, next week, after exams, after the project is finished. Tomorrow is exactly when disasters do not wait for. The cure is to start today, with the smallest possible step: back up your most important folder to one place, right now, before you finish reading this chapter. A partial backup made today is worth more than a perfect backup planned for next month. Every experienced person who has lost files will tell you the same thing: they meant to back up soon, and soon never came. Do not join them. Start small, start today, and improve the plan as you go.

Section 40: Free Tools and Honest Limits

Free backup tools are genuinely useful, and there is no shame in using them, especially when you are a student or starting out. Most operating systems include a built-in backup tool at no extra cost, many cloud services offer a free tier with a few gigabytes of space, and several reputable programs offer free versions with basic features. For documents and a modest photo collection, these free options are often enough, and they are far better than no backup at all. The honest approach is not to dismiss free tools, but to understand their limits clearly, so you do not trust them further than they can actually go.

The most common limit is space. A free cloud tier of a few gigabytes fills quickly once photos and videos are involved, and when it fills, backups stop, sometimes silently. Check your usage regularly, and if you are near the limit, either free up space by removing things you do not need to back up, or accept that the free tier cannot hold everything and choose what matters most for it. Another limit is features: free versions sometimes lack version history, encryption, or the ability to back up certain folders, and those missing features matter when you need to recover an older version or protect sensitive files. Read what the free version actually offers, not what the paid version advertises, because the two are often very different.

A third limit is support and longevity. A free service run by a small company can close, change its terms, or be bought by another company, and your backup goes with it. That is why the 3-2-1 rule matters even more for free tools: never let a free service be your only copy of anything important. Keep a local drive copy alongside any free cloud backup, so that if the service disappears, you still have your files. Also be cautious about free tools from unknown sources, because fake backup and recovery tools are a known way malware spreads. Download backup software only from the official site of a reputable provider or from your operating system's own store, and be suspicious of any tool that asks for unnecessary permissions or tries to install extra software.

Used wisely, free tools are an excellent starting point, and many people never need more. The honest limits are space, features, support, and trust, and each one can be managed: watch your space, know your features, keep a local copy, and choose reputable providers. If your files grow beyond what free tools can hold, that is a good problem to have, because it means you have a lot worth protecting, and at that point a modest paid plan or a larger drive is money well spent. Start free, start now, and let your backup grow with your needs.

Section 41: Paying for Backup: What Matters

Paying for backup is worth it when the free options no longer fit your files, but paying does not automatically make a backup good. A paid plan with the wrong features, or one you never check, is no better than a free plan you ignore. When you consider paying, judge the service on a short list of things that actually matter for recovery, not on marketing claims about speed or unlimited everything. First, does it keep old versions, and for how long? Version history is what saves you from bad edits and ransomware, so a service that only keeps the latest copy is a weak backup, however much space it offers. Look for at least thirty days of version history, and longer if you can get it.

Second, does it encrypt your data, and can you control the key? Encryption protects your files if the service is breached or if a drive is stolen from its data centre. Some services encrypt with a key they hold, which protects against outsiders but not against the company itself or a government order. Others let you hold your own key, which is stronger for privacy but means the company cannot help you if you forget it. There is no universally right answer, but you should know which model your service uses and choose deliberately. Third, how easy is restore? A service that backs up beautifully but restores slowly, or only to the same device, or through a confusing process, will fail you at the worst moment. Test a restore during any free trial, before you commit to a year of payments.

Fourth, consider the company's reputation and longevity. A backup service is a long-term relationship: you are trusting it with your files for years. Choose a company with a track record, clear terms, and a way to get your data out if you leave. Avoid services that make export difficult, because a backup you cannot leave with is a form of lock-in, and lock-in is a risk in itself. Fifth, think about cost honestly. A monthly fee that you will actually keep paying is better than an expensive annual plan you cancel after three months. If money is tight, a one-time purchase of a larger external drive may serve you better than a subscription, because it has no ongoing cost and nobody can switch it off for non-payment.

Finally, remember that paying for one service does not replace the 3-2-1 rule. A paid cloud backup plus a local drive copy is a strong combination. A paid cloud backup alone is a single point of failure with a nicer logo. Pay for what matters, version history, encryption, easy restore, and a reputable provider, keep your local copy, and check both regularly. Money spent on backup is money spent on peace of mind, but only if the service earns that trust by actually restoring your files when you test it. Test before you trust, and trust only what you have tested.

Section 42: Backing Up Code and Projects

If you write code, build websites, design graphics, or work on any creative project, your project files deserve a backup approach that understands how projects actually work. A project is rarely a single file. It is a folder full of files that depend on each other, plus a history of changes, notes, and sometimes large assets like images or videos. Losing one file can break the whole project, and losing the history means losing the story of how the project grew and why decisions were made. Backing up a project therefore means backing up the whole folder, regularly, with its structure intact, so that a restore gives you a working project, not a pile of disconnected files.

Version control is the professional version of this idea, and it is worth learning even for small projects. A version control system, such as Git, tracks every change you make, lets you step back to earlier versions, and lets you push a copy to a remote service, which acts as an off-site backup as well as a collaboration tool. You do not need to master version control to benefit from it: even a basic habit of committing your work regularly and pushing it to a remote repository gives you version history and an off-site copy in one action. That said, version control is not a complete backup by itself, because it usually does not include large binary assets, ignored files, or the settings of your tools. Keep a regular folder backup alongside it, so nothing falls through the gaps.

For projects without version control, the dated snapshot habit from earlier sections works well. When you reach a milestone, copy the whole project folder to a backup location with the date in its name, such as myproject-2026-10-30. Keep several snapshots, not just the latest, because a bug introduced last week may only become visible today, and you may need to step back further than you expect. Also keep a short text file in each project, a readme, explaining what the project is, how to run it, and what tools it needs. A restored project without its readme can be a mystery, even to its own author six months later. The readme is part of the backup, because a project you cannot run is only half recovered.

Finally, back up the assets and the environment, not just the code. Images, fonts, audio files, and data files that your project depends on should be backed up with it, and if your project depends on specific tools or versions, note those in the readme. Test a restore by copying a project to a different folder or a different computer and trying to run it there. If it runs, your backup is complete. If it fails because a file is missing, you have found a gap while there is still time to fix it. Projects are the work you are proudest of, and they deserve a backup that restores them whole, working, and understandable.

Section 43: Game Saves and Creative Work

Game saves and creative work, such as music, video editing, artwork, and writing, share a painful property: they represent hours of personal effort that cannot be recreated by downloading something again. A lost game save can mean a hundred hours of play gone. A lost video project can mean weeks of editing gone, including raw footage that cannot be re-shot. A lost artwork file can mean a piece that existed only in that exact form, with layers and settings that a flattened export cannot reproduce. These files are often large, often stored in unusual places, and often overlooked by standard backup tools, which is why they deserve deliberate attention in your backup plan.

The first step is to find where these files actually live. Game saves often live in hidden folders, in a platform's cloud save system, or in a documents subfolder you would never think to check. Creative tools often store projects in their own folders, with linked media files scattered across your drive. Spend an hour once, for each game or tool you care about, finding where its data lives and confirming whether your backup covers that location. If a platform offers cloud saves, switch them on, but do not assume they cover everything: some games sync saves, others do not, and some only sync when you are online. A local copy of your saves folder, made regularly, is the safety net under the cloud feature.

The second step is to back up the working files, not just the finished exports. For a video project, that means the project file plus all the raw clips and audio it references, because a project file without its media is an empty shell. For artwork, it means the layered working file, not just the flat image you posted online, because the layers are what let you edit it later. These working files are large, so back them up to an external drive rather than relying only on a small cloud account, and keep dated snapshots of major milestones, so a corrupted project file does not destroy weeks of work. Test a restore by opening a backed-up project on another occasion and confirming it opens with its media intact.

Finally, accept that creative work grows, and plan storage for that growth. A video editor's drive fills faster than a writer's, and a gamer's save collection grows with every game. Buy storage with room to grow, archive finished projects to a separate drive rather than letting them clog your working drive, and keep the archive backed up too. Creative work is personal in a way that spreadsheets are not, and losing it hurts in a way that is hard to explain to someone who has not experienced it. Protect the working files, protect the raw media, and protect them in a place you can find again. Your future self, looking back at a finished game or a finished film, will be glad you did.

Section 44: Shared Computers and Backups

Shared computers, in families, schools, libraries, and workplaces, create a backup problem that personal computers do not: several people use one machine, each with their own files, and nobody feels fully responsible for backing any of them up. The result is often that nobody backs up anything, and when the shared computer fails or is reset, everyone loses their files at once and blames each other. A shared computer needs a backup plan that respects each person's privacy while protecting everyone's files, and that plan needs to be agreed in advance, not invented after a disaster. The key principle is simple: each person is responsible for their own files, but the machine's keeper is responsible for making backup easy.

The most practical arrangement is separate user accounts, one per person, each with its own folders and its own backup destination, such as a personal cloud account or a personal folder on an external drive. Separate accounts protect privacy, because one person cannot casually browse another's files, and they make backup simpler, because each person's files live in a known place. If separate accounts are not possible, agree on separate folders with clear names, and agree that nobody deletes or moves files from another person's folder. That agreement sounds obvious, but on shared machines it is the rule most often broken by accident, usually by someone tidying up with good intentions.

The keeper of the machine, whether that is a parent, a teacher, or an office manager, should also keep a whole-machine backup or system image from time to time, so that a failed drive or a corrupted system can be rebuilt without losing everyone's work. That image should be stored securely and access should be limited, because it contains everyone's files. Tell the users that such a backup exists and what it covers, so they understand both the protection and the privacy implications. Transparency builds trust, and trust is what makes a shared backup plan work. Nobody should discover after a disaster that their files were, or were not, included in a backup they knew nothing about.

Finally, teach each user the basic restore skill from section thirty-eight: how to recover a single deleted file from their own backup. On a shared machine, small accidents are frequent, and a user who can restore their own file without asking for help is a user who will not panic or hide a mistake. Agree on a simple rhythm: each user checks their backup monthly, the keeper checks the whole-machine backup monthly, and everyone knows where the backups live. Shared computers do not have to mean shared disasters. With separate spaces, clear agreements, and a keeper who makes backup easy, they can be safer than a personal computer that nobody ever backs up at all.

Section 45: Helping Someone Else Back Up

Section 46: A Yearly Backup Check-Up

A backup plan, like a car or a house, needs a regular check-up, because things change quietly over time. Drives age, accounts fill up, tools stop working after updates, and your files grow in ways you do not notice until a backup fails. A yearly check-up, done calmly on a day you choose, catches all of these slow changes before they become a crisis. Pick a date you will remember, such as the start of a new year, your birthday, or the anniversary of the day you first set up your backup, and put a recurring reminder in your calendar. A check-up you schedule is a check-up that happens. A check-up you intend to do someday is one that never does.

The check-up itself is a short list of tasks. First, check that each backup is still running: look at the last successful run date for every tool and account, and investigate anything that has not run recently. Second, check storage space on every drive and account, and free up space or expand capacity before it runs out. Third, test a restore from each backup destination, a single file from each, so you prove that every copy is still readable. Fourth, check your priority list from section five: has anything new become important since last year, such as a new project, a new child, or a new job, and is it included in your backups? Fifth, check your accounts: are the passwords current, is multi-factor authentication still on, and are the recovery options still ones you can reach? Each task takes minutes, and together they take less than an hour.

The check-up is also a good time to retire old backups, following section thirty-three, and to update your written backup note. Note any drives you replaced, any accounts you closed, and any new devices you added. If your life changed significantly, a new job, a move, a new family member, consider whether your backup plan still fits, and adjust it without guilt. A plan that fit a student may not fit a worker with client data, and a plan that fit one person may not fit a growing family. Changing the plan is not failure. It is maintenance, and maintenance is what keeps a plan alive for years instead of months.

Finally, use the check-up to celebrate what is working. If your backups ran all year without drama, that is a success, and noticing it reinforces the habit. Tell a family member or friend about your check-up, and encourage them to do their own, because backup habits spread best by example. A yearly check-up is a small ritual with a large payoff: it turns a backup plan from a one-time setup into a lasting part of your digital life, and it means that when the unexpected happens, you will face it with a plan you know is current, tested, and ready. Put the date in your calendar now, and keep it.

Section 47: When Backups Themselves Get Attacked

Backups are valuable, which means attackers value them too, and a backup system can itself become a target. An attacker who gains access to your computer may look for backup drives, backup software, and cloud accounts, and try to delete, encrypt, or corrupt what they find, precisely because a good backup would undo their attack. This is not a reason to despair about backups. It is a reason to design them with attack in mind, using the same principles you have learned throughout this chapter: distance, disconnection, and limited access. A backup that an attacker cannot reach is a backup that survives the attack, and designing for that is a practical skill, not a paranoid one.

The most important protection is one you already know: keep at least one backup disconnected. A drive in a drawer, unplugged, cannot be deleted by software running on your computer, however powerful the attacker's access. The second protection is to limit who and what can change your backups. Use separate credentials for your backup account, protected by multi-factor authentication, and never store those credentials in a plain file on the computer being backed up. If your backup software runs with its own account that has limited permissions, an attacker who compromises your everyday account may not be able to touch the backups. Ask your backup provider what access controls it offers, and use them, because default settings often allow more access than you need.

A third protection is versioning and immutability, two words that sound technical but describe simple ideas. Versioning, which you learned in section ten, keeps older copies even when newer ones are changed or deleted. Immutability means a backup copy that cannot be changed or deleted for a set period, even by someone with the password, which some business and advanced personal backup services offer. If your service offers either feature, switch it on for your most important backups. Even without immutability, keeping several generations of backups on different media means an attacker must destroy every generation to succeed, and each disconnected generation makes that harder. Attackers are often in a hurry, and a backup system with depth and distance is a hard target to fully destroy.

Finally, plan for the possibility that a backup is compromised, just as you plan for a drive failing. If you suspect an attack, do not restore from the most recent backup blindly, because it may itself be encrypted or corrupted. Restore from an older, disconnected copy that predates the attack, and check restored files carefully before trusting them. Change your passwords from a clean device, get trusted help, and report the incident if it involves work or other people's data. A backup system designed with attack in mind, disconnected copies, limited access, versioning, and a cautious restore, is not invulnerable, but it is resilient, and resilience is the realistic goal. Attackers attack backups because backups work. Make yours hard to reach, and they will keep working for you.

def backup_survives(disconnected, separate_credentials, versioning):
    return disconnected or (separate_credentials and versioning)

print("Offline drive survives:", backup_survives(True, False, False))
print("Online only, no protection:", backup_survives(False, False, False))

Section 48: Recovery Stories and Lessons

Stories teach backup lessons better than warnings do, because a warning is abstract and a story is something you can picture happening to you. Consider a student who wrote her final project on a laptop with no backup. The laptop was stolen from a café table during a five-minute bathroom break, and with it went three months of work, due in two weeks. She rewrote it in a panic, submitted a weaker version, and passed, but barely. Her lesson, which she now tells every new student she meets, is that a backup is not about the laptop, it is about the work, and work deserves a second copy the moment it matters to you. Her story is common, and its moral is simple: back up early, back up often, and never let a single device hold the only copy of something with a deadline.

Consider also a photographer who did everything almost right. He kept his photos on an external drive and backed them up to a second drive, but both drives lived in the same studio, and a fire destroyed the studio and both drives in one night. His cloud backup, which he had meant to set up, was still on his to-do list. He lost ten years of client work and personal photographs, and his business never fully recovered. His lesson is the one this chapter repeats most: distance matters. Two copies in one building are one disaster away from zero copies, and the off-site copy is the one people postpone and regret postponing. If his story makes you set up a cloud backup today, it has done its work, even though it is painful to read.

A happier story shows what good habits achieve. A small business owner was hit by ransomware that encrypted her office computer and its attached backup drive. She did not panic, because she had an older backup on a disconnected drive at home and a cloud backup with version history. Her IT helper wiped the computer, restored from the clean backup, and she was working again the next day, having lost only one day's entries, which she recreated from paper notes. She did not pay the ransom, she did not lose her business, and she now tells other business owners that the disconnected drive in her drawer was the best money she ever spent. Her story is the payoff of everything in this chapter, and it is an ordinary story, not a heroic one. Ordinary habits produced an ordinary recovery, which is exactly the point.

The lesson across all three stories is the same: backups are not about technology, they are about regret management. The people who recover are not smarter or luckier than the people who lose everything. They simply made a copy, in another place, before they needed it, and they tested that the copy worked. You can be the third story instead of the first two, and the decision takes less time than reading this section. Make the copy, put it somewhere else, test it once, and then get on with your life, knowing that whatever happens to your devices, your work and your memories have a second chance.

Section 49: Your Personal Backup Checklist

A checklist turns everything you have learned in this chapter into a short list you can actually follow, without rereading forty-eight sections every time you need to check your backups. The best checklist is one you write yourself, in your own words, for your own devices, because a generic list will always include things you do not need and miss things you do. But a starting template helps, and this section gives you one, which you can copy, adapt, and keep somewhere safe. Go through it once now, honestly, and note which items are already done and which are not. The gaps are your to-do list, and closing them one by one is the most useful homework this chapter can give you.

The checklist begins with the basics. Do you have a backup of your most important files, and can you name where it lives? Is there a second copy in a second place, such as a cloud account or a drive elsewhere? Does your backup run automatically, and when did it last complete successfully? Have you restored a file from it, proving it works? Is your phone backed up, including photos, and can you reach that backup from another device? Are your documents backed up daily or continuously, because they change often? Each yes is a strength. Each no is a specific task, and specific tasks are easier to complete than vague resolutions to be safer someday.

The checklist continues with the details people forget. Are your backups encrypted, at least the ones that leave your home? Do you know your backup account passwords, and are your recovery options current? Are your settings, bookmarks, and contacts backed up or exported? Have you backed up before your last major update, and will you before the next one? Is there a written note, somewhere safe, saying what is backed up, where, and how to restore it? Is someone else, a family member or a colleague, aware of the plan, in case you are unavailable when a restore is needed? These details are the difference between a backup that exists and a backup that works under stress, when you are tired, upset, or using an unfamiliar device.

Finally, the checklist ends with a date. When will you check all of this again? Write that date in your calendar now, and treat it as an appointment with your future self. A checklist without a review date is a snapshot, not a system, and snapshots go stale. Keep the checklist short enough to complete in an hour, keep it where you can find it, and update it when your devices or your life change. If you complete this checklist, honestly and fully, you will have a backup plan better than most people's, built from simple habits rather than expensive tools. That is a genuine achievement, and it is one you can build in a single weekend, starting today.

Section 50: Backups as a Habit, Not a Chore

If this chapter has done its job, backups should now feel less like a technical chore and more like a habit, something you do without thinking about it, the way you lock your door or charge your phone. Habits work because they do not depend on motivation. You do not lock your door because you feel inspired about security each evening. You lock it because it is what you do, and the same should be true of backups. Automatic tools do most of the work, a monthly glance confirms they are still running, and a yearly check-up keeps the plan matched to your life. That is the whole habit, and it is small enough to keep for decades, which is exactly how long you will need it, because your files will keep growing for as long as you keep creating them.

The habit is worth keeping because the alternative is not neutral. Without backups, every device you own is a countdown to a loss you cannot predict, and the loss, when it comes, will cost you time, money, stress, and sometimes memories that cannot be replaced at any price. With backups, the same event becomes an errand: buy a replacement, restore your files, carry on. That difference is not about technology or wealth. It is about a decision made once, followed by a system that runs itself. People who have lived through a data loss understand this viscerally, and people who have not yet often need to hear it plainly: the time to set up a backup is before you need one, because after you need one, it is too late to make it.

Building the habit is easier if you link it to something you already do. Check your backup status when you pay a monthly bill, or when the calendar turns to a new month. Back up your phone's photos when you return from a trip, before you unpack your bags. Copy your project folder when you finish a milestone, while the satisfaction is fresh. These small links, called habit anchors by people who study behaviour, turn an abstract intention into a concrete moment, and concrete moments are what actually happen. You do not need willpower. You need a trigger, a tiny action, and a tool that does the rest automatically. Design those triggers for yourself, and the habit will largely take care of itself.

As you move on to the next chapter, carry this habit with you. Malware, the subject of chapter twelve, can destroy files, lock them, or steal them, and a good backup is your safety net under all of those threats. But a backup is also more than a defence against malware. It is a statement about what you value: your work, your memories, your creative projects, and the people whose data you hold. Protecting those things, quietly and consistently, is one of the most responsible things a computer user can do. Start today, start small, keep it automatic, and let the habit protect you for years, without you ever having to think about it very much again.
