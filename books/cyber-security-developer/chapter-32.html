Chapter 32: Spotting Suspicious Activity in Plain Words

Section 1: What Suspicious Means

This section is about what suspicious means. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain what suspicious means to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

Suspicious activity is activity that does not fit the normal pattern for you, your device, or your account. It is not the same as proof of an attack. A forgotten password, a family member using your device, or a program updating itself can all look odd at first. The word suspicious is a prompt to check, not a verdict. A calm defender treats a suspicious sign as a question: is there a harmless explanation, and can I verify it? That question, asked early, stops small problems from growing.

The best way to spot trouble is to know your own normal. What time do you usually sign in? Which devices do you use? Which programs run when you start your computer? If you know those answers, a login at three in the morning from a device you have never used will stand out at once. If you do not know your normal, almost everything looks alarming, or worse, a real change blends in. Spend a little time learning your own patterns before you worry about advanced threats.

One strange sign on its own is often weak evidence. Two or three strange signs pointing the same way are much stronger. A single failed login might be a typo. Many failed logins, followed by a successful login at an unusual hour, followed by a settings change you did not make, is a pattern worth acting on. Defenders look for patterns because attackers, mistakes, and faults each leave different patterns. Write down each sign with its time, and the pattern becomes easier to see.

Put what suspicious means into practice on your own system this week, in a small and safe way. Start with one example you control, write down what you expected to see, and then compare what you actually saw. If the result surprises you, do not rush to a conclusion; check one more source, such as a second log, a settings page, or a trusted help page. A careful note, with the date and the result, turns a single exercise into a habit you can repeat. That habit is the real lesson of this section, more than any single fact, because defenders improve by repeating calm checks, not by memorising warnings. Note the time, the account, and the device before you change anything else. Check for a harmless explanation first, but do not dismiss a real pattern.

Section 2: Normal First, Suspicious Second

This section is about normal first, suspicious second. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain normal first, suspicious second to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

Accounts give you the clearest early warnings, because most services keep a record of recent activity. Check the recent sign-ins, the list of devices, and the list of active sessions. Look for places you have not been, devices you do not own, and times you were asleep. If you find one, do not panic and do not delete everything at once. Change the password from a device you trust, turn on multi-factor authentication if it is not already on, and sign out sessions you do not recognise.

Devices also warn you, but their warnings are quieter. A computer that suddenly slows down, a phone battery that drains fast, a program that starts on its own, or a setting that changes back after you fix it, can each have a harmless cause such as an update or a low battery. The defensive habit is the same: note when it started, note what changed just before it, and check one thing at a time. Reinstalling everything at the first slow day wastes time and can destroy the very clues you need.

Money signs deserve fast, calm action. An unexpected charge, a receipt for something you did not buy, or a payment method you did not add should be checked with the bank or provider through a channel you trust, not through a link in a suspicious message. Do not reply to the message that alarmed you. Contact the provider directly, freeze or lock the card if the provider offers that, and keep the receipt and the time. Financial providers deal with this often, and a clear, dated note helps them help you.

Messages from your own account are a special kind of warning, because they can harm other people too. If friends say they received odd messages from you, believe them and check quickly. Look at your sent folder, your recent activity, and your connected apps. Tell your contacts plainly that you did not send those messages, change your password, and review which apps can act on your behalf. Acting quickly protects your contacts, and honesty about what happened protects your reputation.

Fear makes people act badly during a scare. They click the first warning they see, download a supposed fix from an untrusted site, or pay a demand in a panic. A defender slows down on purpose. Disconnect from the network if you need to stop further harm, use a trusted device to change passwords, and ask a trusted person or the official support channel for help. The skill is not never being alarmed; it is checking each alarm against what you know, recording the outcome, and learning from it. Over time your checks get faster, and your judgement about which alarms matter gets better.

Fear makes people act badly during a scare. They click the first warning they see, download a supposed fix from an untrusted site, or pay a demand in a panic. A defender slows down on purpose. Disconnect from the network if you need to stop further harm, use a trusted device to change passwords, and ask a trusted person or the official support channel for help. You do not need to solve everything in the first five minutes. You need to stop the harm, preserve the evidence, and get help.

False alarms are normal, and they are not a failure. A new device warning after you sign in on a borrowed phone, a location warning while you travel, or a code message caused by an old app trying to refresh, can all look suspicious. The skill is not never being alarmed; it is checking each alarm against what you know, recording the outcome, and learning from it. Over time your checks get faster, and your judgement about which alarms matter gets better.

When you report suspicious activity, be specific and factual. Say what you saw, when you saw it, on which account or device, and what you have already done. Do not guess who did it, and do not exaggerate. A clear report helps support teams act quickly, and it helps you later if you need to show what happened. Keep copies of messages, receipts, and activity pages, but remove passwords and codes before you share anything with anyone.
