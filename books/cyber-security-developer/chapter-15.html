Chapter 15: Wi-Fi Safety at Home and in Public

Section 1: What Wi-Fi Actually Is

Wi-Fi is the invisible link between your devices and your network. Instead of a cable, your phone, laptop, tablet, or smart speaker talks to a router using radio waves, travelling through the air across your home, office, or café. The router then connects that traffic to the internet, usually through a cable or fibre line. When you open a website on your phone at home, your request travels by radio to the router, then by cable to the internet, and the answer comes back the same way. Wi-Fi is simply a wireless replacement for the last stretch of cable between you and the router. It is not the internet itself, and understanding that distinction helps you understand its risks.

Radio waves, unlike cables, do not stop at your walls. They travel through doors, windows, and thin walls, reaching neighbours, passers-by, and anyone sitting nearby in a café. That is convenient, because you can move around freely, but it also means your Wi-Fi signal is available to people you did not invite. Anyone within range can see that your network exists, and if it is unprotected, they can join it. Even if it is protected by a password, they can attempt to guess that password or exploit weaknesses in older security methods. Your network's boundary is not your front door. It is the edge of your radio signal, which is much further away.

Wi-Fi also carries your data through the air in a form that can be intercepted. If your Wi-Fi connection is encrypted, as modern networks are, the data is scrambled so that only your device and the router can read it. If it is not encrypted, as with many open public networks, anyone nearby with simple free tools can read unencrypted traffic passing through the air. That is why the security of your Wi-Fi connection matters as much as the security of the websites you visit. A secure website encrypts its own traffic, but an insecure Wi-Fi network still exposes other details, such as which sites you visit, and it can expose traffic from apps that do not encrypt properly.

The good news is that Wi-Fi safety is mostly a matter of a few clear habits, covered in this chapter. Use strong passwords and modern encryption at home. Be cautious on public networks, preferring encrypted websites and a VPN for sensitive tasks. Switch off automatic connection to unknown networks. Keep your devices updated. None of these habits requires technical skill, and together they cover almost every Wi-Fi risk an ordinary user will face. Wi-Fi feels mysterious because it is invisible, but its safety rules are as plain as locking a door: control who can enter, scramble what passes through, and be careful in places you do not control.

Section 2: How Wi-Fi Reaches Your Device

When you tap a Wi-Fi network name on your phone, a short conversation happens between your device and the router, in a fraction of a second, before any of your data flows. First, your device announces itself and asks to join. The router checks whether the network is open or password-protected, and if it is protected, your device offers the password you entered. If the password is correct, the two agree on encryption keys, secret codes that will scramble all further traffic between them, and only then does your device get an address on the network and access to the internet. This handshake, as it is called, is the foundation of Wi-Fi security, and its strength depends on the encryption method your network uses, covered in section six.

Once connected, your device and the router exchange data in small bursts of radio waves, many times per second. Your device does not hold the channel open continuously. It sends a burst, waits for acknowledgement, and sends the next, sharing the airwaves with every other device on the network. That is why Wi-Fi slows down when many devices are active at once, as chapter fourteen explained, and why distance and obstacles matter: walls, floors, metal objects, and even water, including the human body and fish tanks, weaken radio signals. A device far from the router, or behind several walls, gets a weaker, slower connection, and may drop off entirely.

Your device also constantly scans for networks, even when you are not actively looking. It sends out probes, asking whether any familiar network is nearby, and it listens for routers announcing themselves. This scanning is convenient, because it lets your phone reconnect automatically to your home network when you arrive, but it also leaks information: the names of networks you have joined before, which can reveal places you have been, such as hotels, offices, or cafés. Section twenty-five explains why automatic connection can be risky, and how to manage it.

Finally, your device chooses which network to join based on signal strength and your saved preferences, not on safety. It will happily join a network with a familiar name, even if it is a fake one set up by an attacker, as section eighteen explains in detail. That is why you should not let your device decide alone. Check the network name carefully before joining, especially in public places, and remove saved networks you no longer need, to reduce unwanted automatic connections. Understanding this short conversation, the handshake, the bursts, the scanning, and the choosing, turns Wi-Fi from magic into something you can reason about, and reasoning about it is the first step to using it safely, at home and in public alike.

Section 3: Wi-Fi Bands and Channels in Plain Words

Wi-Fi travels on radio bands, which are ranges of frequencies set aside for wireless communication, much like radio stations occupy different frequencies on a dial. Home Wi-Fi mainly uses two bands: 2.4 gigahertz and 5 gigahertz. The 2.4 gigahertz band travels further and passes through walls better, but it is crowded, shared with Bluetooth devices, microwaves, baby monitors, and your neighbours' networks, so it is often slower and more congested. The 5 gigahertz band is faster and less crowded, but it does not travel as far or pass through walls as well. Many modern routers offer both, sometimes under one network name, letting your device choose, and sometimes as separate networks you can pick between.

Within each band, Wi-Fi is divided into channels, narrower slices of frequency that networks use to avoid interfering with each other. Think of a band as a road and channels as lanes. If too many networks crowd into the same lane, they interfere, slowing each other down, much like traffic congestion. Most routers choose a channel automatically, scanning for the least crowded one, and for most homes that is fine. In flats and dense neighbourhoods, where dozens of networks overlap, manual channel choice can help, but it is rarely a security issue, only a speed one. Do not let anyone sell you channel optimisation as a security product. It is not.

A newer band, 6 gigahertz, is appearing on modern routers and devices, offering even faster speeds and less congestion, but with even shorter range. You do not need to chase the newest band for safety. All bands support the same modern encryption, and a 2.4 gigahertz network with strong encryption is far safer than a 6 gigahertz network with a weak password. When choosing between bands at home, pick based on your needs: 2.4 gigahertz for devices far from the router or behind walls, such as smart devices in the garden, and 5 gigahertz for devices nearby that need speed, such as a laptop streaming video in the same room as the router.

For safety, the band matters in one specific way: range. A 2.4 gigahertz signal travels further outside your home, reaching neighbours and the street, which means more people can see and attempt to join your network. That is not a reason to avoid it, but it is a reason to ensure your password is strong and your encryption is modern, as sections five and six explain. A signal that reaches further is a larger invitation, and a larger invitation needs a stronger lock. Bands and channels are mostly about speed and coverage. Security comes from passwords and encryption, whichever band you use, so choose your band for speed and coverage, and your password for safety.

Section 4: Your Home Wi-Fi Network Explained

Your home Wi-Fi network is the wireless part of your home network, covered in chapter fourteen, and it usually has one or two names, a password, and a router that manages it all. When you set up home internet, your provider usually supplies a router with a default network name and password printed on a sticker. Many people keep those defaults forever, which is understandable, because they work, but defaults are a weak starting point. The network name may identify your provider or router model, which helps attackers guess its weaknesses, and the default password, while often long and random on modern routers, is sometimes short, predictable, or shared across many units of the same model. Changing both, as sections nine and ten explain, is the first step to owning your network rather than renting its defaults.

Your home Wi-Fi network does more than connect your phone to the internet. It connects every wireless device in your home: laptops, tablets, televisions, speakers, cameras, thermostats, game consoles, and children's devices. All of them share the same wireless airspace and, unless you segment them as chapter fourteen advised, the same network. That means a compromised smart bulb could, in theory, reach your laptop, if your network is flat and your laptop's defences are weak. In practice, modern devices have their own firewalls and protections, but the principle stands: your home Wi-Fi network is a shared space, and its safety depends on every device on it, not just the router.

You control your home Wi-Fi network through your router's admin page, usually reached by typing an address into a browser, as chapter fourteen explained. From there you can change the network name and password, set up a guest network, see connected devices, update firmware, and adjust security settings. You do not need to visit this page often, but you should know how to reach it and have its admin password stored safely, because it is the control room for your home network. If you cannot sign in to your router, because you lost the admin password, you may need to reset it, which restores defaults and requires setting everything up again, another reason to store passwords properly.

Finally, remember that your home Wi-Fi network is yours to manage, but it affects others too. A weak home network can be used by a neighbour or a passer-by to download illegal content, send spam, or attack others, and that activity may be traced back to your internet account. A strong home network protects not only your data, but your reputation and your account. Setting it up well, once, and reviewing it occasionally, is one of the highest-value security habits in this whole book, because it protects every device in your home at once.

Section 5: Wi-Fi Passwords That Actually Protect You

A Wi-Fi password is the lock on your wireless network. If it is weak, anyone within range can guess or crack it and join your network, gaining access to your internet connection, your shared files, and your devices. If it is strong, that door stays shut, even against determined attempts. The difference between a weak and a strong Wi-Fi password is not subtle, and it is one of the few security choices entirely in your hands, costing nothing and taking only a few minutes to fix.

A weak Wi-Fi password is short, predictable, or personal. Examples include your address, your phone number, your pet's name, a simple sequence like 12345678, or the default password that came with your router if it is short or widely known. Attackers do not guess these by hand. They use automated tools that try millions of common passwords and patterns in minutes, and they use lists of leaked passwords from breaches, which include millions of real passwords people actually chose. If your Wi-Fi password appears on such a list, or follows an obvious pattern, it will be found quickly. Personal details are especially weak, because anyone who knows you, or can see your social media, can guess them.

A strong Wi-Fi password is long, random, and unique to your network. Aim for at least twenty characters, made of random words, numbers, and symbols, or a passphrase of four or five unrelated words with numbers mixed in, such as a phrase only a password generator would produce. It does not need to be memorable in the traditional sense, because you will type it rarely: once per device, and then your device remembers it. Store it in a password manager or a safe written note, as chapter eight advised, and share it with household members through safe means, not by shouting it across a room or posting it online anywhere. Uniqueness matters too: do not reuse your Wi-Fi password for email, banking, or any other account, because a leak of one would then open the others.

Changing your Wi-Fi password is also important when circumstances change. Change it when a housemate moves out, when you suspect an unknown device has joined, when you have shared it widely with visitors, or at least once every couple of years as routine hygiene. Changing it disconnects every device, so you will need to reconnect them all, which is inconvenient for an evening, but that inconvenience is the point: it evicts everyone, wanted and unwanted, and lets you invite back only the devices you recognise. A strong password, changed when needed, is the single most effective Wi-Fi protection you have, and it costs nothing but a few minutes of your time.

Section 6: Wi-Fi Encryption: WEP, WPA, WPA2, WPA3

Wi-Fi encryption is the scrambling that protects your wireless traffic from anyone listening nearby. Without it, your data travels through the air in plain form, readable by anyone in range with simple tools. With it, your data is scrambled using keys agreed during the handshake from section two, so only your device and the router can read it. The strength of that scrambling depends on which encryption standard your network uses, and the standards have improved dramatically over the years, while older ones have become dangerously weak. Knowing which standard your network uses is one of the most useful Wi-Fi checks you can make.

The oldest standard, WEP, dates from the late 1990s and is now completely broken. Its encryption can be cracked in minutes with freely available tools, and it should never be used. If your router still offers WEP, or if you see WEP mentioned in your Wi-Fi settings, treat that as an urgent warning: your network is effectively open, whatever password you set. Some very old devices only support WEP, such as ancient printers or early smart devices, and the safe answer is to retire those devices, not to downgrade your whole network to accommodate them. A single WEP device can weaken an entire network.

WPA was a temporary improvement, and WPA2, introduced in 2004, became the standard for nearly two decades. WPA2 with a strong password is still reasonably safe for home use, and it is what most home networks use today. Its main weakness is that its password handshake can be captured by an attacker nearby and then attacked offline, trying millions of password guesses without needing to stay in range. That is why a strong, long password matters so much on WPA2: a weak password can be cracked offline, while a strong one resists even powerful guessing. WPA2 also has a well-known flaw in its original key installation, patched years ago, which is another reason to keep router firmware updated.

WPA3, introduced in 2018, is the current standard and the one to choose for new equipment. It fixes WPA2's offline guessing weakness by using a stronger handshake that resists password guessing even when an attacker captures it, and it encrypts traffic individually for each device, even on open networks, in a mode called opportunistic encryption. It also makes it easier to add devices without typing long passwords, using QR codes or button presses. If your router and devices support WPA3, switch it on, ideally in WPA3-only mode, or in a mixed WPA2 and WPA3 mode if some older devices need WPA2. If your router does not support WPA3, that alone is not a reason to replace it, provided you use WPA2 with a strong password, but it is worth considering when you next upgrade.

To check your standard, look in your router's Wi-Fi security settings, or on your device's Wi-Fi details, where it usually says WPA2 or WPA3 next to your network name. If it says WEP, WPA, or nothing at all, meaning open, act promptly: change the setting to WPA2 or WPA3, set a strong password, and update your firmware. Encryption is invisible when it works, but its absence is an open door, and closing that door is the foundation of Wi-Fi safety.

Section 7: Why Open Wi-Fi Is Risky

An open Wi-Fi network is one with no password and no encryption, found in many cafés, airports, hotels, libraries, and public spaces. It is convenient: you join, you are online, no password to ask for, no setup to struggle with. That convenience is also its danger, because everything that makes it easy for you makes it easy for everyone else, including attackers sitting nearby with the same free connection and simple tools. Open Wi-Fi is not automatically a disaster, and you can use it safely with care, but you should understand exactly what is at risk before you join.

The first risk is eavesdropping. On an open network, your wireless traffic is not encrypted by the Wi-Fi itself, so anyone nearby can capture it. If you visit a website that uses HTTPS, shown by a padlock, that website's own encryption still protects the content of your visit, but an eavesdropper can still see which sites you visit and when. If you use an app or website that does not encrypt properly, the eavesdropper can read far more, including messages, photos, or login details. Many modern apps do encrypt, but not all, and you cannot easily tell which ones do not. On an open network, you should assume someone nearby could be listening, and behave accordingly.

The second risk is manipulation. On an open network, an attacker can do more than listen. They can intercept your connection and redirect it, sending you to a fake version of a website, injecting advertisements or malicious content into unencrypted pages, or capturing your session, the temporary token that keeps you signed into a website, and using it to impersonate you. These attacks, called man-in-the-middle attacks, are harder against HTTPS websites, but they still work against unencrypted traffic and against users who ignore browser warnings. A browser warning about a certificate or a connection that suddenly looks different is a signal to stop, not to click through.

The third risk is the network itself. An open network's name tells you nothing about who runs it. Anyone can set up a hotspot called Free Airport Wi-Fi or Café Guest and capture the traffic of everyone who joins, as section eighteen explains. Even a legitimate open network may be poorly maintained, with outdated equipment and no one watching for abuse. You have no way to verify who operates it, what they log, or whether they sell browsing data. That uncertainty is part of the risk, and it is why sensitive activities, banking, work systems, and account changes, should wait until you are on a trusted network, or be done through a VPN, as sections twenty-one to twenty-four explain.

None of this means you must never use open Wi-Fi. It means you should use it for low-risk activities, browsing news, checking maps, watching videos, and avoid sensitive ones, or protect them with a VPN. Open Wi-Fi is a public space, like a busy street: useful, lively, and not a place for private conversations. Treat it that way, and it serves you well without putting your data at risk.

Section 8: Checking Your Home Wi-Fi Security

Checking your home Wi-Fi security takes about ten minutes and tells you whether the habits in this chapter are actually in place. You do not need special tools or technical knowledge, only access to your router's admin page and your device's Wi-Fi settings, both covered in chapter fourteen. Do this check when you first set up a network, after any major change, and once a year as routine maintenance. A check that finds nothing wrong is reassuring. A check that finds a problem is valuable, because it finds it before an attacker does.

Start with the basics on your router's admin page. Confirm the admin password is not the default, the firmware is up to date, and the Wi-Fi security setting says WPA2 or WPA3, not WEP, WPA, or open, as section six explained. Confirm your Wi-Fi password is long, random, and unique, as section five advised, and that your network name does not identify you, your address, or your family. Check that a guest network is set up for visitors and smart devices, and that remote administration is off unless you deliberately need it. Each of these takes seconds to confirm, and together they form the core of home Wi-Fi safety.

Next, check from a device's point of view. On your phone or laptop, open the details for your home Wi-Fi network and confirm it shows WPA2 or WPA3 security, and that the network name matches the one you set, not a similarly named neighbour's network. Look at your saved networks list, covered in section twenty-six, and remove any you no longer use, especially old networks from previous homes, hotels, or workplaces, because your device may reconnect to them automatically. Check that your device's software is up to date, and that its firewall is on, because device defences matter alongside router settings.

Then, check the devices on your network. Open your router's connected-devices list, as chapter fourteen explained, and confirm you recognise every device. An unknown device may be a family member's new phone, but it may also be a neighbour who guessed a weak password, or a smart device you forgot. If it remains unknown after asking around, change your Wi-Fi password, which disconnects everything, and reconnect only recognised devices. Also check your smart devices, as section fifteen will discuss, confirming they are on the guest network where possible and still receiving updates.

Finally, write down what you checked and what you changed, and store that note safely. A written record turns a one-time check into a routine you can repeat, and it helps you notice drift, such as a setting that changed or a password that was shared. Home Wi-Fi security is not a mystery. It is a short list, checked occasionally, and this section is that list. Use it, and your home network will be safer than most, with very little effort.

Section 9: Changing Your Wi-Fi Password Safely

Changing your Wi-Fi password is one of the simplest security improvements you can make, and also one of the most disruptive if done carelessly, because it disconnects every device at once. Doing it safely means preparing first, changing the password in the right place, and reconnecting devices deliberately, rather than in a panic. This section walks through that process step by step, so you can do it confidently when you need to, whether because a housemate moved out, you suspect an unknown device, or it is simply time for routine hygiene.

Before you change anything, prepare. Choose a new password that is long, random, and unique, following section five: at least twenty characters, or a passphrase of unrelated words with numbers, generated by a password manager if possible. Write it down safely, in your password manager or a secure note, because you will need it for every device. Warn household members that the Wi-Fi will briefly disconnect, and pick a quiet time, not during an important video call or a child's online lesson. Gather the devices you will need to reconnect, and make sure you know your router's admin password, because you will need it to sign in. If you have forgotten the admin password, you may need to reset the router first, which restores all defaults, so check that before you start.

To change the password, sign in to your router's admin page, find the wireless or Wi-Fi settings section, and locate the password field, sometimes called the network key, passphrase, or pre-shared key. Replace the old password with the new one, save, and wait for the router to apply the change. Your devices will disconnect at this point, which is expected. Do not also change the network name at the same time unless you have a reason to, because changing both at once makes troubleshooting harder if something goes wrong. One change at a time, confirmed working, is the safe approach.

Now reconnect your devices, one by one, starting with your own phone or laptop. On each device, forget the old network if it tries the old password automatically, then join again with the new password, confirming it connects and reaches the internet before moving to the next device. Work through the household systematically: phones, laptops, tablets, televisions, speakers, and smart devices, checking each one. If a device refuses to connect, double-check the password, especially for symbols and capital letters, and check whether the device supports your encryption type, as section six explained. Very old devices that only support WEP should be retired, not accommodated by weakening your network.

Finally, update your records. Store the new password safely, update any note where the old password was written, and tell household members the new password through a safe channel, not a group chat or a note stuck to the router. If you shared the old password with visitors, tradespeople, or former housemates, changing it has now evicted them, which is the point. A password change done deliberately, with preparation and a systematic reconnect, turns a disruptive chore into a clean reset, leaving your network with only the devices you recognise and trust.

Section 10: Your Router's Wi-Fi Settings Explained

Your router's Wi-Fi settings page can look intimidating, full of abbreviations and options you never asked for, but only a handful of settings matter for safety, and understanding those few gives you control over your network. This section explains the important ones in plain words, so you can look at your router's page without fear, change what needs changing, and leave the rest alone. You do not need to understand every option. You need to understand the ones that protect you.

The first settings are the network name and password, called SSID and passphrase or network key on many routers. The SSID is the name your network broadcasts, visible to nearby devices. Choose one that does not identify you, your address, or your router model, as section four advised, because a name like 24 Smith Street or John's iPhone tells strangers more than they need to know. The passphrase is your Wi-Fi password, covered in section five. Change both from their defaults, and store them safely. These two settings are the front door of your network, and they deserve your attention.

The second setting is security mode, sometimes called authentication or encryption, where you choose WEP, WPA, WPA2, or WPA3. As section six explained, choose WPA3 if your router and devices support it, WPA2 if they do not, and never WEP or open, unless you have a deliberate, temporary reason and understand the risk. Some routers offer a mixed mode, WPA2 and WPA3 together, which is a good compromise for households with older devices. If you see an option for enterprise authentication, ignore it at home: it is for offices with a dedicated authentication server, and it will only confuse a home setup.

The third group of settings covers bands and guest networks. Band settings let you choose whether to broadcast on 2.4 gigahertz, 5 gigahertz, or both, as section three explained, and whether to use one combined network name or separate names per band. Either is fine, and combined is simpler. Guest network settings let you create a separate network for visitors and smart devices, with its own password and isolation from your main network, as chapter fourteen explained. Switch the guest network on and use it. It is one of the most valuable settings on the page, and it costs nothing.

Other settings you may see include WPS, a button-press method for adding devices without typing a password, channel selection, and transmit power. WPS is convenient but has known weaknesses, especially its PIN method, so switch it off if you can, and add devices by typing the password instead. Channel selection is usually best left on automatic. Transmit power controls how far your signal reaches: lowering it slightly can reduce how far outside your home your network is visible, but it can also weaken coverage inside, so adjust it only if you have a specific reason. The principle for all these settings is the same: understand the few that matter, change those deliberately, and leave the rest at their safe defaults.

Section 11: The Guest Wi-Fi Network, Revisited

Chapter fourteen introduced guest networks as a way to keep visitors off your main network, and this chapter returns to them because they are especially important for Wi-Fi safety. A guest network is a second Wi-Fi network broadcast by your router, with its own name and password, isolated from your main network. Devices on the guest network can reach the internet, but they cannot reach devices on your main network, such as your laptop, your shared folders, or your network drive. That isolation is the whole point, and it protects you in both directions: guests cannot accidentally or deliberately reach your private devices, and a compromised guest device cannot easily spread to your main network.

Setting up a guest network is usually simple. On your router's admin page, find the guest network or guest Wi-Fi section, switch it on, give it a clear name, such as Home Guest, and set a password, which can be simpler than your main Wi-Fi password, because guest access is temporary and isolated, though it should still not be trivially guessable. Some routers let you set time limits, bandwidth limits, or a schedule for the guest network, switching it off overnight, for example. Use those options if they suit you, but the essential step is simply switching the guest network on and using it consistently. A guest network you never use protects no one.

Use the guest network for more than human visitors. Smart home devices, as chapter fourteen explained, belong on the guest network too, because they are often less secure, less frequently updated, and more likely to be compromised than your main devices. Children's friends' phones, tradespeople's tablets, and any device you do not fully trust also belong there. The rule is simple: if a device does not need access to your private files and devices, it does not belong on your main network. That rule, applied consistently, turns your network from one flat space where everything can reach everything, into two spaces with a clear boundary between them.

Guest networks also simplify hospitality. Instead of sharing your main Wi-Fi password with every visitor, which you must then change when circumstances change, you can share the guest password freely, and change it whenever you like without disconnecting your own devices. If a visitor's device turns out to be infected, the damage is contained to the guest network, and you can switch the guest network off while you sort it out, without losing your own connection. Some routers even let you create a QR code for the guest network, so visitors can join by scanning it, without you dictating a password aloud.

The common mistake with guest networks is setting one up and then forgetting to use it, giving visitors the main password out of habit or convenience. Break that habit deliberately. Put the guest password where you can find it easily, tell household members to offer it to visitors, and make it the default answer to the question, what is your Wi-Fi password? A guest network is only a few minutes of setup, and it is one of the most effective Wi-Fi safety habits in this chapter, because it limits every unknown device to a safe, isolated space.

Section 12: Hidden Networks: Why Hiding Does Not Help

Many routers offer an option to hide your network name, so it does not appear in the list of nearby Wi-Fi networks on other people's devices. This sounds like good security: if attackers cannot see your network, they cannot attack it. In reality, hiding your network name provides almost no protection, and it can cause inconvenience and even new risks. Chapter fourteen mentioned this myth briefly, and this section explains it fully, because it is one of the most persistent misunderstandings in Wi-Fi safety.

The reason hiding does not work is that your network name is not actually secret when hidden. Your router stops announcing it in its regular broadcasts, but your devices still reveal it whenever they connect, because they must tell the router which network they want to join. Anyone nearby with simple, freely available tools can capture those connection messages and learn your network name in seconds. Hiding your network is like taking your house number off your door but still telling every visitor your address aloud: anyone paying attention learns it immediately. It deters only the most casual glance, not anyone actually trying to find your network.

Hiding can also cause practical problems. Devices that have previously joined a hidden network must actively probe for it, sending out messages asking, is this network nearby, which reveal the network name to anyone listening, even when you are far from home. That means a hidden network can actually leak your network name more widely than a visible one, as your phone probes for it in cafés, airports, and streets. Hidden networks can also be harder to connect to, especially for guests and new devices, leading to frustration and to workarounds, such as sharing passwords insecurely or switching security off, that create real risks.

There is one narrow situation where hiding might make sense: a network you want to keep obscure from casual neighbours, in a building where dozens of networks appear on every scan, and where you accept that it provides no real security. Even then, it should be an addition to strong security, never a replacement for it. Your real protection is a strong password and modern encryption, as sections five and six explained. Those protect you whether your network name is visible or not, because an attacker who knows your network name still cannot join without the password, and cannot read your encrypted traffic without the keys.

So, should you hide your network? For most homes, no. Leave it visible, choose a name that does not identify you, use a strong password and WPA2 or WPA3, and spend your security effort on the things that actually work. Hiding feels secretive and therefore safe, but in Wi-Fi, as in much of security, feeling safe and being safe are different things, and this chapter is about being safe.

Section 13: Wi-Fi Range: Signal Beyond Your Walls

Your Wi-Fi signal does not respect your property boundary. It travels through walls, windows, and floors, reaching neighbours, the street, and sometimes surprisingly far beyond, depending on your router, your home's construction, and the band you use, as section three explained. A 2.4 gigahertz signal can reach through several walls and across a garden, while a 5 gigahertz signal fades faster. That range is why you can use Wi-Fi in your garden or from a neighbouring room, and it is also why your network is visible and reachable by people you did not invite. Understanding your signal's reach helps you judge your exposure honestly.

You can see your own signal's reach indirectly. Walk around your home and outside it with your phone's Wi-Fi list open, noting where your network name appears, how strong its signal is, and where it finally disappears completely. You may be surprised to find it visible from the pavement, a neighbour's flat, or a nearby park. That visibility alone is not a problem, provided your password is strong and your encryption is modern, because visibility without access is harmless. But it is a reminder that your network's audience is larger than your household, and that a weak password would be tested by anyone in that audience who cared to try.

You can reduce your signal's reach, within limits, by placing your router centrally in your home, rather than against an outside wall or in a window, where half its signal spills outside. Avoid placing it in a window, which is one of the worst spots, because glass barely weakens radio waves. Some routers let you reduce transmit power, lowering how far the signal travels, but this can also weaken coverage in distant rooms, so adjust it carefully and test coverage afterwards. Physical barriers help too: a router in a central cupboard, surrounded by walls, reaches less far outside than one on a windowsill. These are modest adjustments, not guarantees, because radio waves will always escape to some degree.

Do not overstate the importance of range, however. Reducing your signal's reach is a useful extra, but it is not a substitute for passwords and encryption. A determined attacker with a good antenna can pick up Wi-Fi signals from much further away than your phone can, so no placement trick makes your network invisible. Conversely, a network with a strong password and WPA3 is safe even if its signal reaches the street, because the signal carries only encrypted data that an outsider cannot read or use without the password. Range management is good hygiene. Passwords and encryption are the lock. Do the lock first, and treat range as a helpful bonus, not as a replacement for real security.

Section 14: Neighbours and Your Wi-Fi

Living near other people means living near their Wi-Fi networks, and them near yours. In a flat, a terraced house, or a dense street, your phone may show dozens of nearby networks, and your neighbours can see yours. This is normal, and it is mostly harmless, provided everyone uses strong passwords and modern encryption. Problems arise only when networks are weak, passwords are shared carelessly, or neighbours, deliberately or accidentally, interfere with each other. This section covers how to be a good Wi-Fi neighbour, and how to handle problems when they arise.

The first issue is interference. Neighbouring networks on the same channel, as section three explained, can slow each other down, especially on the crowded 2.4 gigahertz band. Most routers handle this automatically by choosing a less crowded channel, but if your Wi-Fi is consistently slow and you live in a dense building, interference may be part of the cause. The fix is usually to let your router choose its channel automatically, or to use the 5 gigahertz band, which is less crowded, rather than to boost your signal power, which only increases interference for everyone. Being a good neighbour on Wi-Fi means not shouting louder than everyone else, because everyone shouting makes conversation harder for all.

The second issue is accidental or deliberate joining. If your network has a weak or default password, a neighbour, or their visitor, or their child's friend, may join it, intentionally or by mistake, especially if your network name is similar to theirs. Once on your network, they use your internet connection, slow it down, and may access shared files if your sharing settings are open. The defence is the same as ever: a strong, unique password, a guest network for visitors, and sharing switched off unless needed. If you discover a neighbour on your network, change your password, which disconnects them, and consider whether your network name or password was too guessable. There is usually no need for confrontation: a password change quietly solves the problem.

The third issue is trust and sharing. Neighbours sometimes ask to share Wi-Fi, to save money or during an outage, and sharing with a trusted neighbour is not inherently wrong, but it should be done deliberately, through a guest network with its own password, not by sharing your main password. Sharing your main password gives them access to your private devices and files, and makes you responsible for their activity on your internet account. If you do share, agree on limits, such as fair usage and no illegal downloads, and be prepared to change the guest password if the arrangement ends. Generosity is good. Uncontrolled sharing is not.

Finally, if a neighbour's network interferes with yours, or you suspect a neighbour is deliberately attacking your network, start with a calm conversation, if you feel safe doing so, or with a technical fix, such as changing your channel or password. Deliberate Wi-Fi attacks between neighbours are rare, and most problems are accidental. Keep your own network strong, be considerate about your signal and channel use, and treat neighbouring networks as a normal part of shared living, not a threat, provided your own defences are in place.

Section 15: Smart Devices on Wi-Fi

Smart devices, speakers, cameras, bulbs, thermostats, doorbells, televisions, and appliances, connect to your home almost entirely by Wi-Fi, and they bring specific Wi-Fi safety considerations that phones and laptops do not. They are often cheaper, less powerful, and less frequently updated than your main devices, and they may be made by companies that stop providing updates after a year or two. They also collect data about your home and habits, and they are always on, always connected, always listening or watching in the case of speakers and cameras. That combination, many devices, weak updates, and sensitive data, makes their Wi-Fi safety worth special attention.

The first rule, repeated from chapter fourteen, is to put smart devices on your guest network, not your main network. This isolates them from your computers and phones, so a compromised bulb or camera cannot easily reach your private files. It also simplifies management: when you change your main Wi-Fi password, your smart devices stay connected to the guest network, undisturbed. Some smart devices need to communicate with your phone on the same network to be set up or controlled, and guest isolation can interfere with that, so check your device's requirements during setup, and use your router's settings to allow specific exceptions if needed, rather than abandoning isolation entirely.

The second rule is to secure each device individually, not just its network. Change default passwords on cameras and doorbells, set strong passwords for their cloud accounts, and switch on multi-factor authentication where offered, as chapter fourteen explained. Review what each device records, where recordings are stored, and who can view them, and switch off features you do not use, such as voice purchasing without confirmation, or cameras recording continuously when motion-only would do. A smart device's own settings are part of its Wi-Fi safety, because a device with a weak cloud password is vulnerable even on a secure network.

The third rule is to manage smart devices over time. Review them periodically, removing ones you no longer use from your network and their cloud accounts, and updating or replacing ones whose makers have stopped providing updates. A device that no longer receives updates is a growing risk, because new vulnerabilities will not be fixed, and at some point the safest choice is to retire it, even if it still works. That is frustrating, especially for expensive devices, but a working device with unfixable vulnerabilities is a liability on your network, not an asset.

Finally, think about placement and privacy. A camera or speaker in a bedroom or bathroom collects far more sensitive data than one in a hallway or kitchen, and its Wi-Fi traffic, even encrypted, reveals patterns of activity. Place sensitive devices thoughtfully, cover cameras when not needed if they offer a shutter, and mute speakers when you want privacy. Smart devices can make a home more convenient, accessible, and enjoyable, and with guest network isolation, individual passwords, and periodic review, they can do so without turning your Wi-Fi network into a vulnerability.

Section 16: Public Wi-Fi: What It Is and Why It Exists

Public Wi-Fi is wireless internet offered to anyone in a place, such as a café, airport, hotel, library, park, or shopping centre, usually free or included with a purchase or stay. It exists because people expect to be connected wherever they go, and because businesses benefit when customers stay longer, work comfortably, or share their experience online. A café with free Wi-Fi attracts laptop workers. An airport with free Wi-Fi keeps travellers calm during delays. Hotel Wi-Fi is now a basic expectation, not a luxury. Public Wi-Fi is part of modern life, and learning to use it safely beats avoiding it.

Public Wi-Fi comes in several forms, and recognising them helps you judge safety. Some networks are open, with no password at all, as section seven explained. Some have a shared password, printed on a receipt, displayed on a wall, or given by staff, which feels more secure but often is not, because everyone in the place knows the same password, and a shared password means shared encryption keys, which an attacker nearby knows. Some use a captive portal, a login page that appears when you join, asking you to accept terms, enter a room number, or provide an email address, covered in section nineteen. And a few, usually in offices or universities, use individual logins, where each user has their own account, which is considerably safer, because your traffic is separated from other users'.

The key point is that public Wi-Fi is a network you do not control, run by people you do not know, shared with strangers you cannot vet. You cannot verify who operates it, whether its equipment is updated, what it logs, or whether anyone else on it is snooping. That does not make it unusable. It makes it a place for appropriate activities, as section twenty explains: browsing, maps, streaming, and messaging are fine, while banking, work systems, and account changes deserve extra protection, such as a VPN, or waiting for a trusted network. The same judgement you apply to a public place, enjoy it, but do not leave valuables unattended, applies to public Wi-Fi.

Public Wi-Fi also varies enormously in quality and honesty. A well-run airport network, maintained by professionals, is very different from a small café's ageing router with a default password, or a hotspot set up by a stranger. You cannot tell the difference from the network name alone, which is why the habits in this chapter, checking names carefully, preferring HTTPS, using a VPN for sensitive tasks, and switching off automatic connection, matter more than trying to judge each network individually. Treat every public network as untrusted until you have reason to trust it, and you will rarely go wrong.

Finally, remember that public Wi-Fi is not your only option when you are out. Your phone's mobile data connection is generally safer than public Wi-Fi, because it is encrypted between your phone and the provider, and it is not shared with strangers nearby. If you need to do something sensitive while out, and you have mobile data available, use it instead of public Wi-Fi, or use your phone as a personal hotspot, as sections twenty-nine and thirty explain. Public Wi-Fi is convenient, and convenience has its place. For sensitive tasks, a connection you control is worth the extra cost or effort.

Section 17: The Risks of Public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi risks fall into three broad groups: snooping, manipulation, and impersonation, and understanding each helps you choose the right protection. Snooping means someone nearby captures your wireless traffic and reads what they can. Manipulation means someone alters your traffic, redirecting you or injecting content. Impersonation means someone pretends to be a network, a website, or even you, to capture your data. These risks overlap, and they are all more likely on public networks than at home, because public networks are shared, often unencrypted, and rarely monitored.

Snooping was introduced in section seven, but it deserves detail. On an open network, an attacker with a laptop and free software can capture traffic from everyone nearby, looking for unencrypted data: messages, photos, login details from apps that do not encrypt, and metadata, such as which websites everyone visits. Even on networks with HTTPS, metadata leaks: the attacker can see which sites you visit, when, and for how long, which can reveal a surprising amount about you. A VPN, covered in section twenty-two, protects against snooping by encrypting all your traffic, not just one website, so it is the strongest single defence for public Wi-Fi use.

Manipulation is less common but more damaging. An attacker on the same network can intercept your connection to a website and redirect it to a fake version, especially if the website does not use HTTPS, or if you ignore a browser warning. They can inject advertisements, malicious downloads, or fake login pages into unencrypted web pages, a technique that once plagued hotel and café networks. They can also poison the network's address system, so that typing a familiar website name takes you to their server instead. Modern browsers and HTTPS make these attacks much harder than they were a decade ago, but they still work against users who click through warnings, use outdated browsers, or visit unencrypted sites.

Impersonation takes two forms. The first is a fake hotspot, covered in section eighteen, where an attacker sets up a network with a plausible name and captures everyone who joins. The second is session hijacking, where an attacker captures the temporary token that keeps you signed into a website and uses it to impersonate you on that site, without needing your password. HTTPS and modern session protections have reduced this risk greatly, but it has not vanished, especially on older websites and apps. Signing out of sensitive accounts when you finish, rather than staying signed in indefinitely, reduces the window for hijacking.

The honest overall picture is reassuring and cautionary at once. Public Wi-Fi is much safer than it was ten years ago, thanks to HTTPS becoming standard, browsers warning loudly about insecure connections, and phones and laptops improving their defences. But it is still a shared, untrusted network, and the risks above are real, if less common than headlines suggest. The protections in this chapter, HTTPS, VPN for sensitive tasks, careful network choice, and switching off automatic connection, reduce those risks to a level where ordinary public Wi-Fi use, for ordinary activities, is reasonable. Fear is not the right response. Informed caution is.

Section 18: Spotting a Fake Wi-Fi Hotspot

A fake Wi-Fi hotspot, sometimes called an evil twin, is a network set up by an attacker to impersonate a legitimate one, capturing the traffic of everyone who joins. It might be called Free Airport Wi-Fi in an airport, Café Guest in a café, or Hotel Wi-Fi in a hotel lobby, mimicking the real network's name closely enough that travellers join without thinking. Once connected, victims send their traffic through the attacker's device, which can capture unencrypted data, redirect them to fake login pages, and steal credentials. Fake hotspots are one of the most effective public Wi-Fi attacks, because they exploit trust and haste rather than technical flaws.

Spotting a fake hotspot starts with checking the name carefully. In a café, airport, or hotel, ask staff for the exact network name, and compare it character by character with what your device shows. Attackers often use names that are close but not exact, such as Cafe Guest instead of Café Guest, or Free_Airport_WiFi instead of Airport Free Wi-Fi, relying on you glancing quickly. If you see two similar networks, one of which you have never seen before, or one that appears suddenly with a very strong signal, be suspicious. A hotspot with an unusually strong signal, stronger than the venue's own network, may be an attacker's device placed close to you, deliberately outshining the real network.

Other warning signs appear after you join. A legitimate network usually connects you to the internet quickly, or shows a captive portal, covered in section nineteen, that matches the venue's branding. A fake network may show a login page asking for unusual details, such as your email password, your credit card number for free Wi-Fi, or your social media login, which no legitimate free network should ever ask for. It may redirect you to strange websites, show repeated certificate warnings, or fail to load familiar sites properly. Any of these is a reason to disconnect immediately, forget the network, and ask staff whether the network is genuine.

Your device's behaviour can also warn you. If your phone automatically joins a network you expected, but the connection behaves differently, slower, with different login pages, or with warnings you have not seen before, do not ignore that difference. Disconnect, switch Wi-Fi off, and use mobile data instead until you can confirm the network with staff. Automatic connection, covered in section twenty-five, makes fake hotspots more dangerous, because your device may join one without you even looking. Switching off automatic joining for public networks, and removing saved networks you no longer need, reduces that risk greatly.

Finally, if you suspect you joined a fake hotspot, act promptly, as section forty-five explains in detail: disconnect, forget the network, change passwords for any accounts you used while connected, from a trusted network, and watch those accounts for unfamiliar activity. Reporting the fake network to the venue's staff helps protect the next person, who might be less alert than you. Fake hotspots succeed because people are busy, tired, and trusting. Slowing down for ten seconds to verify a network name is a small habit that defeats most of them.

Section 19: Captive Portals: Hotel and Café Login Pages

A captive portal is the login or agreement page that appears when you join many public Wi-Fi networks, before you get full internet access. You have seen them in hotels, airports, cafés, and shopping centres: a page asking you to accept terms and conditions, enter a room number, a code from a receipt, or an email address, sometimes with an advertisement or a promotion. The portal's job is to control access, making sure only guests or customers use the network, and to present terms you agree to by continuing. Understanding captive portals helps you use them safely, because they involve a moment of trust that attackers can exploit.

A legitimate captive portal appears automatically when you first join a network and try to browse. It usually matches the venue's branding, uses plain language, and asks for only what it needs: acceptance of terms, a room number, or a voucher code. It should never ask for sensitive credentials, such as your email password, your bank login, or your social media password. If a portal asks for those, disconnect immediately: it is almost certainly fake, or at least deeply untrustworthy. A portal that asks for your email address for marketing is common, though you may prefer to use a secondary email address rather than your main one, to reduce spam and tracking.

Captive portals have a technical quirk worth knowing: to show you the portal page, the network must intercept your first web request and redirect it to the portal, which means your browser may show a warning about an insecure connection or a certificate mismatch. That warning is often normal for captive portals, but it is also exactly the warning an attacker would trigger with a fake portal. The safe way to handle it is to visit a plain, unencrypted website address, such as a well-known example site, to trigger the portal deliberately, rather than trying to reach a sensitive site first, and to complete the portal process before doing anything sensitive. Once the portal accepts you, close that browser tab and use your usual sites, which should then load with proper HTTPS.

After passing a captive portal, remember that you are still on a public network, with all the risks from section seventeen. The portal does not make the network private or encrypted. It only gates access. Your traffic is still shared with strangers, still potentially visible, and still deserving of the protections in this chapter: HTTPS, a VPN for sensitive tasks, and caution about what you do. Do not let the feeling of having logged in fool you into thinking you are now on a private network. You have passed a gate, not entered a locked room.

Finally, be careful what you share through portals. Read the terms briefly, looking for anything about data collection, tracking, or sharing, and be sceptical of portals that demand excessive personal details for a short Wi-Fi session. If a café's portal wants your full name, phone number, date of birth, and social media login for thirty minutes of internet, that is disproportionate, and you should consider using mobile data instead. A captive portal is a transaction: a little information for internet access. Make sure the exchange is fair, and never trade a password for Wi-Fi.

Section 20: What to Do and Not Do on Public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi is safe enough for many activities and risky for a few, and knowing the difference is the core skill of this chapter. This section gives you a practical list, so you do not have to reason from first principles every time you sit down in a café or an airport. The rule of thumb is simple: if an activity would embarrass, harm, or cost you if someone nearby saw or altered it, protect it with a VPN or postpone it until you are on a trusted network. Everything else is generally fine.

Safe activities on public Wi-Fi include browsing news, checking maps and transport times, streaming music and video, reading articles, and messaging friends through apps that encrypt messages end to end. These activities may reveal metadata, which sites you visit, but their content is protected by HTTPS or app encryption, and the harm from someone seeing that metadata is usually low. Even these activities benefit from basic care: keep your device updated, use HTTPS sites, and ignore browser warnings only if you understand them, which usually means never ignoring them. Safe does not mean careless. It means the risk is low enough to accept with normal precautions.

Activities to avoid, or to protect with a VPN, include online banking, shopping with a credit card, accessing work systems, changing passwords, and signing into important accounts for the first time on a new device. These activities involve credentials, money, or sensitive data, where interception or manipulation could cause real harm. If you must do them on public Wi-Fi, use a VPN, covered in section twenty-two, which encrypts all your traffic, and prefer your bank's official app, which usually has stronger protections than a browser, over a mobile website. Even with a VPN, prefer mobile data for the most sensitive tasks if it is available, because a connection you control is safer than one you share.

Also avoid activities that expose you in other ways. Do not download software, updates, or files from unfamiliar sources on public Wi-Fi, because a manipulated network could replace them with malicious versions, as section seventeen explained. Do not leave your device unattended and connected, do not let strangers use your device to check something quickly, and do not join a network and then walk away while your device syncs email, photos, and cloud files in the background, because that background traffic may include sensitive data. Turn off automatic syncing for sensitive apps while on public networks, if your device allows it, or disconnect when you are not actively using the connection.

Finally, develop a leaving habit. When you finish using public Wi-Fi, disconnect, and if you do not expect to return to that network, forget it, as section twenty-six explains, so your device does not reconnect automatically next time. Sign out of sensitive accounts you used, rather than staying signed in. These small leaving habits close the door behind you, reducing the chance that a later automatic connection exposes you without your knowledge. Public Wi-Fi use, done thoughtfully, is a normal part of modern life. This list is not a ban. It is a way to enjoy public Wi-Fi's convenience while keeping its risks in proportion.

Section 21: HTTPS on Public Wi-Fi

HTTPS is the padlock in your browser's address bar, and it is your most important protection on public Wi-Fi, because it encrypts the traffic between you and a website even when the Wi-Fi itself does not. HTTPS stands for Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure, and it adds a layer of encryption, called TLS, to ordinary web browsing. When you visit an HTTPS site, your browser and the website agree on encryption keys, verify the website's identity through a certificate, and then scramble everything you send and receive: the pages you view, the forms you fill in, and the passwords you enter. On an open network, an eavesdropper can see that you visited the site, but not what you did there.

Checking for HTTPS is simple. Look at your browser's address bar: it should show a padlock icon and an address starting with https, not http. Most browsers now warn loudly, or block you entirely, if you try to visit a site without HTTPS, especially if it asks for a password. Do not ignore those warnings. A warning that a site's certificate is invalid, expired, or mismatched may mean the site is misconfigured, but on a public network it may also mean someone is intercepting your connection, as section seventeen explained. If you see such a warning on public Wi-Fi, stop, disconnect if you are unsure, and try again on a trusted network. A warning is information, not an obstacle to click past.

HTTPS protects websites, but it does not protect everything. Many apps use HTTPS internally, which is good, but some older or poorly made apps do not encrypt all their traffic, and you cannot easily tell from outside. Email apps, messaging apps, and cloud sync tools may send some data unencrypted, especially on older versions. Keeping your apps updated helps, because updates often fix encryption gaps. A VPN, covered in the next section, fills the remaining gap by encrypting all traffic from your device, not just web browsing, which is why it is recommended alongside HTTPS for public Wi-Fi, rather than instead of it.

There is also a limit to what HTTPS hides. As sections seven and seventeen explained, an eavesdropper on an open network can still see which websites you visit, because the destination address is visible even when the content is encrypted, and they can see the timing and size of your traffic, which can reveal patterns. That metadata is usually low-risk for ordinary browsing, but it is a reason to use a VPN if you are concerned about privacy, not just security. HTTPS protects what you say. A VPN also hides, to a large extent, who you are saying it to, from local eavesdroppers.

Finally, make HTTPS your default everywhere, not only on public Wi-Fi. Most browsers now offer a setting to prefer or require HTTPS, sometimes called HTTPS-Only Mode, which automatically upgrades unencrypted connections where possible and warns you when it cannot. Switch that on. It costs nothing, breaks very few legitimate sites, and ensures that even if you forget to check the padlock, your browser checks for you. On public Wi-Fi, HTTPS is your seatbelt: simple, automatic, and effective, provided you actually wear it and heed its warnings.

Section 22: VPN on Public Wi-Fi

A VPN, introduced in chapter fourteen, deserves a closer look here, because public Wi-Fi is where it is most useful. A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a server run by your employer or a VPN provider, and sends all your internet traffic through that tunnel. On a public network, that means other devices nearby, and the network operator itself, see only encrypted traffic between you and the VPN server, not the websites you visit, the apps you use, or the data you send. For sensitive activities on public Wi-Fi, a VPN is the strongest single protection available to an ordinary user.

Using a VPN on public Wi-Fi is straightforward. You install an app from your employer or a reputable provider, sign in, and switch it on before joining a public network, or immediately after joining, before doing anything sensitive. Most VPN apps show a clear connected indicator, often a key icon in your phone's status bar or a green light in the app. Check that indicator before banking, working, or signing into important accounts. If the VPN disconnects unexpectedly, many apps offer a kill switch, which blocks all internet traffic until the VPN reconnects, preventing your data from leaking onto the public network unprotected. Switch the kill switch on if your app offers it, because a VPN that silently disconnects leaves you exposed without warning.

Choosing a VPN provider deserves care, as chapter fourteen warned. A VPN shifts your trust from the public network operator to the VPN provider, because the provider can now see your traffic instead. Choose a provider with a clear no-logs policy, independent security audits, transparent ownership, and a good reputation, not merely the cheapest or most advertised option. Free VPN services deserve particular scepticism: running VPN servers costs money, and a free service must pay for them somehow, often through advertising, data collection, or selling browsing information, which defeats the purpose. If your employer provides a VPN, use it for work, and consider whether you also want a personal one for private use.

A VPN also has limits you should understand honestly. It does not protect you from phishing websites, malicious downloads, or scams, because those dangers live at the destination, beyond the tunnel, as chapter fourteen explained. It does not make you anonymous, because websites can still identify you through accounts, cookies, and device fingerprints. It can slow your connection, because your traffic travels further, through the VPN server, and it may cause some websites or streaming services to block you or show different content, because you appear to be in the server's location. These are acceptable trade-offs for sensitive tasks, but they are reasons not to leave a VPN on permanently without thinking.

The practical rule is this: on public Wi-Fi, switch your VPN on before sensitive activities, and keep it on while you do them. For casual browsing, HTTPS alone is usually enough, and a VPN is a welcome bonus. For banking, work, shopping, or account changes, a VPN is strongly recommended, and mobile data is an even safer alternative if available. A VPN is not magic, but on public Wi-Fi, it turns a shared, untrusted network into a private tunnel, and that transformation is exactly what sensitive data needs.

Section 23: Banking and Shopping on Public Wi-Fi

Banking and shopping on public Wi-Fi deserve special caution, because they involve money, account credentials, and personal details, where a mistake can cost you directly. The safest advice is simple: avoid banking and shopping on public Wi-Fi if you can, and use mobile data or wait until you are on a trusted network instead. Life is not always that tidy, however, and sometimes you need to check a balance or buy a ticket while out. This section explains how to do those tasks as safely as possible when you must.

If you must bank on public Wi-Fi, use your bank's official app, not a browser, wherever possible. Bank apps are built with strong encryption, certificate checking, and fraud detection, and they are harder to spoof than websites, because an attacker cannot easily create a fake version of your bank's app on your phone. Make sure the app is genuine, downloaded from your official app store, and up to date, and never bank through a link in a message or email, which may lead to a fake site, as chapter nine on phishing explained. Sign in with your usual credentials and multi-factor authentication, check that the app shows no warnings, and sign out when you finish, rather than leaving your session open.

For shopping, the same principles apply. Use official apps or well-known websites with HTTPS, shown by the padlock, and check the website address carefully before entering payment details, because fake shopping sites are common, and a public network makes redirection attacks easier, as section seventeen explained. Prefer payment methods with buyer protection, such as a credit card or a reputable payment service, over direct bank transfers or debit cards, because they offer better fraud recovery if something goes wrong. Avoid saving your card details on a shopping site while on public Wi-Fi, and avoid shopping on sites you have never used before while on an untrusted network, because you have no baseline for what their genuine site looks like.

A VPN, covered in section twenty-two, is strongly recommended for both banking and shopping on public Wi-Fi. It encrypts all your traffic, protecting you from local snooping and manipulation, and it is a reasonable extra layer even when using official apps. If you do not have a VPN, and you cannot use mobile data, consider whether the task can wait. A train ticket can often be bought later. A bank transfer rarely needs to happen from a café. Delay is free, and it is the safest option when the network is untrusted and the task is sensitive.

After banking or shopping on public Wi-Fi, check your accounts soon afterwards, from a trusted network, for unfamiliar transactions, and report anything suspicious to your bank immediately. Banks have fraud teams and time limits for reporting, and prompt reporting improves your chances of recovery. Also, if you entered credentials on a network you later suspect was fake, change those passwords promptly, from a trusted network, as section forty-five explains. Banking and shopping while out are sometimes necessary, and with official apps, HTTPS, a VPN, and prompt checking, they can be done with reasonable safety. But whenever you have a choice, a trusted network or mobile data is the better choice for money.

Section 24: Work on Public Wi-Fi

Working on public Wi-Fi is increasingly common, from cafés, airports, hotels, and co-working spaces, and it carries extra responsibilities, because work data belongs not only to you but to your employer, your clients, and your colleagues. A breach of work data on a public network can affect your organisation's customers, reputation, and legal obligations, not just your own accounts. That is why work on public Wi-Fi deserves stricter habits than personal browsing, and why your employer's policies, covered in chapter fourteen, should be your first guide, not an afterthought.

The first rule is to use your employer's VPN for all work traffic, without exception, when working outside a trusted office network. Your employer's VPN, managed by their IT team, encrypts your connection to work systems and lets you access internal tools securely, as chapter fourteen explained. Do not work on internal systems, shared drives, or sensitive documents on public Wi-Fi without the VPN, even for a quick check, because a quick check can expose as much as a long session. If the VPN will not connect, stop work that needs internal access and contact IT, rather than working around the VPN, because workarounds bypass the protections your employer put in place for good reason.

The second rule is to separate work and personal activity, even on the same device. Use work accounts, work apps, and work cloud storage for work, and personal accounts for personal matters, and avoid mixing them, because mixing makes work data harder to protect and to remove if a device is lost or compromised. Be careful with what is visible on your screen in a public place: a colleague's salary, a client's details, or an unreleased product plan on a laptop screen in a café is visible to anyone nearby, a risk called shoulder surfing, which no VPN can prevent. Use a privacy screen filter if you often work in public, sit with your back to a wall where possible, and lock your screen whenever you step away, even for a minute.

The third rule is to be careful with files and devices. Do not download sensitive work files to a personal device over public Wi-Fi unless your employer explicitly allows it, and do not transfer work files through personal email, messaging apps, or USB drives, because those routes bypass work security controls and may breach policy. Keep your work device updated through your employer's update system, and report any security incident, such as a suspicious email, a strange network behaviour, or a lost device, to IT promptly, as chapter fourteen advised. Prompt reporting is not an admission of failure. It is how incidents are contained before they spread.

Finally, respect your employer's rules about which networks are approved. Some employers prohibit public Wi-Fi for certain systems entirely, requiring mobile data or a personal hotspot instead, and that prohibition should be followed, even if public Wi-Fi seems convenient. If you are unsure whether a task is allowed on public Wi-Fi, ask IT. Asking takes a minute. A breach takes months to resolve. Working in public can be productive and enjoyable, and with a VPN, separation, screen awareness, and prompt reporting, it can also be safe, for you and for everyone whose data you handle.

Section 25: Automatic Wi-Fi Connection: A Hidden Risk

Automatic Wi-Fi connection is one of your device's most convenient features, and one of its least understood risks. When you join a Wi-Fi network and tick remember this network, or when your device does so by default, it saves that network's name and password, and thereafter it reconnects automatically whenever it sees a network with the same name. That is wonderful at home, where you want instant connection on arrival, but it becomes risky in public, because your device will join any network with a familiar name, without asking you, and without checking whether it is genuine.

The danger is the fake hotspot from section eighteen, combined with automatic connection. An attacker who knows, or guesses, the name of a popular network, such as a hotel chain, an airport, or a coffee shop, can set up a fake network with that name, and your device, seeing a familiar name, will join it automatically, before you even look at your phone. You may then send traffic, sync email, and receive notifications through the attacker's device, without ever choosing to join. This attack works because your device trusts names, not identities: it cannot tell a genuine Hotel Wi-Fi from a fake one with the same name, unless the network uses individual logins or certificates that verify its identity, which most public networks do not.

Your device also leaks information through automatic connection, even when it does not join anything. As section two explained, it constantly probes for saved networks, sending out their names to see if any are nearby. Anyone listening can collect those names, learning where you have been: your home, your office, hotels you stayed in, cafés you frequent. That information can be used for targeted attacks, such as setting up a fake version of your hotel's network when you return, or for simple tracking. Modern phones have reduced this leakage by randomising their hardware addresses and limiting probes, but they have not eliminated it, especially for networks you joined long ago and forgot about.

Managing automatic connection is straightforward. On your phone and laptop, review your saved networks, covered in section twenty-six, and remove ones you no longer need, especially public networks from trips, hotels, and cafés. For networks you keep, check whether your device offers a setting to ask before joining, or to disable automatic joining, and use it for public networks, while keeping automatic joining for your home network, where it is safe and convenient. Some devices also offer a setting to use a random hardware address for each network, which reduces tracking: switch it on where available.

The principle is simple: automatic connection should be a privilege for networks you trust, not a default for every network you ever joined. Your home network has earned that trust. A café network from a holiday three years ago has not. Reviewing saved networks occasionally, and limiting automatic joining to trusted ones, closes a quiet, persistent risk that most users never notice, until an attacker exploits it.

Section 26: Forgetting Networks You No Longer Use

Your device remembers every Wi-Fi network you join, unless you tell it to forget, and over years that list grows long: homes you no longer live in, hotels from holidays, offices from old jobs, cafés from trips, airports from layovers. Each saved network is a small risk, as section twenty-five explained, because your device may reconnect to it automatically, or probe for it, revealing where you have been. Forgetting networks you no longer use is a simple, periodic cleanup that reduces that risk, and it takes only a few minutes.

To forget a network, open your device's Wi-Fi settings, find the list of saved or known networks, and remove the ones you do not recognise or no longer need. On most phones, you tap a network name and choose Forget. On most laptops, you find saved networks in the Wi-Fi or network settings and remove them there. Do not worry about forgetting a network you might need again: forgetting only removes the saved name and password from your device, it does not ban the network, and you can rejoin it later by entering the password again. Forgetting is reversible. The risk of keeping a network is not.

Which networks should you forget? Start with public networks from trips and holidays, hotels, airports, conference venues, and cafés in places you do not regularly visit, because those are the networks most likely to be impersonated, and the least likely to be needed again. Next, forget networks from previous homes, previous jobs, and previous schools, because those networks may have changed passwords, changed owners, or become untrusted since you left, and your device probing for them leaks information about your past. Keep your current home network, your current work network if you trust it, and any network you use at least monthly, because those earn their place on your list.

Make forgetting part of a routine. After a holiday or a work trip, spend two minutes forgetting the networks you joined while away, before you forget you joined them. Once a year, review the full saved list, as section forty-eight's family plan will suggest, and remove anything unfamiliar. If you find a network name you do not recognise at all, remove it without hesitation: you cannot lose anything by forgetting a network you do not know, and you may close a risk you did not know you had.

Forgetting networks also helps in a subtle way: it reduces clutter and confusion. A long saved list makes it harder to spot a suspicious network, harder to manage your connections, and more likely that your device joins the wrong network in a place with many similar names. A short, deliberate list, containing only networks you trust and use, is easier to understand, easier to manage, and safer. Your device's memory should reflect your current life, not every place you have ever been. Forgetting is not losing. It is tidying, and tidying is a security habit.

Section 27: Wi-Fi on Your Phone: Settings That Protect You

Your phone is the device you connect to Wi-Fi most often, in the most places, and with the least thought, which makes its Wi-Fi settings especially important. Most phones ship with sensible defaults, but a few settings deserve your attention, because changing them once protects you every time you go out. This section covers the phone settings that matter most, in plain words, without assuming any particular brand, because the settings exist, under slightly different names, on almost all modern phones.

The first setting is Wi-Fi itself: switch it off when you do not need it. A phone with Wi-Fi on constantly scans for networks, probes for saved ones, and may join familiar names automatically, as sections two and twenty-five explained. Switching Wi-Fi off when you are out and not using it, or when you are using mobile data, stops that scanning and joining entirely. It also saves battery. Make it a habit: Wi-Fi on at home and in places where you deliberately use it, Wi-Fi off when travelling between places or when you have no need for it. This single habit removes a whole category of risk.

The second setting is automatic joining. On most phones, you can choose, per network, whether to join automatically, and you can switch off a general setting that joins open networks automatically. Switch automatic joining off for public networks, and keep it on only for your home network and other trusted networks, as section twenty-five advised. Also check whether your phone offers a setting to use a random hardware address, sometimes called private Wi-Fi address, for each network. Switch it on, because it prevents networks and nearby observers from tracking your phone by its permanent hardware address across different places.

The third setting is sharing. Many phones can share their internet connection as a hotspot, covered in sections twenty-nine and thirty, and can also share files, photos, and contacts with nearby devices over Wi-Fi. Those sharing features are convenient, but they should be off unless you are actively using them, because an enabled sharing feature is an open door to nearby devices. Check your phone's sharing, hotspot, and nearby-device settings occasionally, and switch off anything you do not recognise or no longer use. A phone that shares nothing by default is a phone that leaks nothing by accident.

Finally, keep your phone's software updated, and use its built-in security features: a lock screen with a strong passcode or biometric, encryption switched on, which most modern phones do by default, and automatic updates for apps. Wi-Fi safety is not only about network settings. A phone with an unlocked screen, outdated software, and sharing left on is vulnerable even on a safe network, because its own defences are down. Check these settings once, after reading this section, and then occasionally, and your phone, the device you carry everywhere, will be a much harder target, wherever you connect it.

Section 28: Wi-Fi Calling and Messaging Safety

Wi-Fi calling lets your phone make ordinary voice calls over a Wi-Fi network instead of the mobile phone network, which is useful when mobile signal is weak indoors, in basements, or abroad. Messaging apps, similarly, send your messages over Wi-Fi when available, rather than as traditional text messages. Both are convenient, and both raise a natural question: is my call or message safe on Wi-Fi, especially public Wi-Fi? The answer is reassuring for modern phones and apps, with a few cautions worth knowing.

Wi-Fi calling, provided by your mobile network operator, encrypts your call between your phone and your operator, using the same security as an ordinary mobile call, and it travels over the Wi-Fi network as encrypted data. An eavesdropper on a public network can see that you are making a Wi-Fi call, and possibly which operator you use, but cannot listen to the call itself, because the voice data is encrypted. That protection depends on your phone and operator supporting Wi-Fi calling properly, which most modern ones do, and on your phone's software being up to date. Wi-Fi calling is not a reason to avoid public Wi-Fi, and it is generally as safe as an ordinary mobile call.

Messaging safety depends on the app. Messages sent through apps with end-to-end encryption, where only you and the recipient can read them, are safe on any network, including public Wi-Fi, because the content is encrypted on your device before it ever reaches the network. Messages sent as traditional SMS text messages, or through apps without end-to-end encryption, are less protected, and may be visible to network operators or, on insecure networks, to eavesdroppers. If you are unsure whether an app encrypts end to end, check its settings or its official description, and prefer encrypted apps for sensitive conversations, especially on public networks.

There are still cautions. Wi-Fi calling and messaging rely on your phone's connection to the network, and a fake hotspot, covered in section eighteen, could interfere with that connection, blocking calls or redirecting you, even if it cannot read encrypted content. A public network that blocks Wi-Fi calling ports, which some restrictive networks do, may simply prevent calls from working, which is inconvenient but not dangerous. And metadata, who you called, when, and for how long, may still be visible to the network operator, even when content is encrypted, which is a privacy consideration, not a security emergency, for most people.

The practical advice is simple. Use Wi-Fi calling and encrypted messaging freely, even on public Wi-Fi, because their encryption protects content. Prefer encrypted messaging apps for sensitive conversations, keep your phone updated, and if a call or message fails repeatedly on a public network, switch to mobile data or move to a trusted network, rather than troubleshooting on an untrusted one. Wi-Fi calling and messaging are designed for exactly the situation you are in: a weak mobile signal with Wi-Fi available. Used with updated software and encrypted apps, they are safe, convenient tools, not risks to avoid.

Section 29: Sharing Your Phone as a Hotspot

Your phone can act as a Wi-Fi hotspot, sharing its mobile data connection with other devices, such as your laptop or tablet, by broadcasting its own Wi-Fi network. This is called tethering or a personal hotspot, and it is extremely useful when you need internet on a laptop but only have mobile data, or when public Wi-Fi is untrusted and you would rather use a connection you control, as section sixteen suggested. A personal hotspot is generally safer than public Wi-Fi, because you control it, but it still needs to be set up safely, or it becomes a small public network of its own, with the same risks.

Setting up a hotspot safely starts with its password. When you switch on your phone's hotspot, it creates a Wi-Fi network with a name, usually based on your phone's name, and a password, which your phone may generate or let you set. Change that password to a strong, unique one, following section five, and do not use your main Wi-Fi password, your phone's unlock code, or a simple word. A hotspot with a weak or default password can be joined by anyone nearby, who then uses your mobile data, potentially running up costs, and gains access to a network with your laptop on it. A strong hotspot password is the lock on your personal network, just as at home.

Also consider your hotspot's name. By default, it may reveal your phone's name, which may include your full name, such as John's iPhone, broadcasting your identity to everyone nearby. Change your hotspot name, and ideally your phone's device name, to something neutral that does not identify you, as section four advised for home networks. This is a small privacy step, but it costs nothing, and it prevents strangers nearby from learning your name from a Wi-Fi scan.

While your hotspot is on, remember that it is a network, with the same sharing considerations as any other. Your laptop, connected to your hotspot, may have file sharing or network discovery enabled, which could expose files to anyone else who joins the hotspot, if your password were ever compromised. Keep sharing off unless needed, as chapter fourteen advised, and switch the hotspot off when you are not using it, rather than leaving it broadcasting all day. A hotspot left on unnecessarily drains your battery, uses data if devices connect in the background, and extends the window for someone to attempt to join it.

Finally, watch your data usage. A hotspot shares your mobile data allowance, and a laptop can consume data far faster than a phone, through updates, cloud syncing, and video streaming, potentially exhausting your allowance or incurring charges. Check your data usage while hotspotting, pause automatic updates and cloud syncing on the connected device if you are on a limited plan, and switch the hotspot off when you finish. A personal hotspot, set up with a strong password, a neutral name, and switched off when not needed, is one of the safest ways to get a laptop online while out, and a good alternative to untrusted public Wi-Fi.

Section 30: Sharing a Hotspot Safely with Others

Sometimes you need to share your phone's hotspot with someone else: a colleague who needs to send an email, a family member whose data ran out, or a friend in an airport with no Wi-Fi. Sharing is generous, and it is also a security decision, because you are inviting another device onto a network you control, with your laptop or tablet possibly on it, and your mobile data paying for their activity. Sharing safely means setting boundaries, so generosity does not become exposure.

The first boundary is the password. Share your hotspot password directly with the person, verbally or through a secure message, not by shouting it across a room or posting it where others can see it. Use a temporary password for sharing, if your phone allows you to change it easily, rather than your regular hotspot password, so you can change it afterwards without disrupting your own devices. After the person finishes, change the hotspot password, or switch the hotspot off entirely, so their device does not reconnect automatically next time you switch it on. A shared password that lives forever is a standing invitation, not a one-time favour.

The second boundary is time and scope. Switch the hotspot on only for as long as needed, and switch it off when the person finishes, rather than leaving it on for the rest of the day. Tell the person what the hotspot is for, a quick email or a map check, not hours of video streaming, because their usage consumes your data allowance and may incur charges. If your phone offers a setting to limit connected devices, or to set a data limit for the hotspot, use it. Clear expectations prevent awkwardness later, when a generous share becomes an unexpectedly large phone bill.

The third boundary is your own devices. If your laptop is also connected to your hotspot, make sure its file sharing and network discovery are off, as chapter fourteen advised, so the other person's device cannot browse your files. If you are sharing with someone you do not know well, consider disconnecting your own laptop from the hotspot while they use it, using your phone alone, to remove any risk entirely. This may sound cautious, but it costs little, and it separates your private devices from a device you do not control, which is the same principle as a guest network at home, applied to your phone.

Finally, be careful in reverse: when someone shares their hotspot with you, you are joining their network, and the same public Wi-Fi cautions apply, in miniature. Their hotspot is safer than a public network, because you know and trust the person, but it is still a shared network, so avoid sensitive activities unless you must do them, use HTTPS and a VPN for sensitive tasks, and disconnect and forget the network when you finish, as section twenty-six advised. Sharing hotspots, done with temporary passwords, time limits, and device separation, is a kind, practical habit. Done carelessly, it is an open door. Choose the careful version.

Check: Why should you change your hotspot password after sharing it with someone, rather than leaving it unchanged?

Section 31: Wi-Fi in Hotels

Hotel Wi-Fi is one of the most common public networks travellers use, and it has its own quirks, because hotels balance guest convenience, old buildings, and large numbers of simultaneous users. Most hotels offer Wi-Fi included in your stay or for a fee, usually through a captive portal, covered in section nineteen, asking for your room number, surname, or a voucher code. That portal gates access, but it does not make the network private, as section nineteen warned. Hotel Wi-Fi should be treated as public Wi-Fi throughout, with the protections in this chapter, even though it feels more private because it requires a room number.

The first hotel-specific risk is the shared password or room-number login. Many hotels use one shared password for all guests, or a login based only on room number and surname, both of which are easy for another guest, or someone in the lobby, to guess or overhear. That means your fellow guests are effectively on the same network with the same access as you, and one compromised guest device could affect others. Do not assume hotel Wi-Fi is separated per guest unless the hotel explicitly says it uses individual logins or per-device isolation. Assume it is shared, and behave accordingly: HTTPS, VPN for sensitive tasks, and sharing off.

The second risk is the hotel's own equipment and practices. Hotel networks are often installed years ago, maintained by outside contractors, and rarely updated, leaving old routers, weak encryption, or captive portals with security flaws. Some hotels also monitor or log guest traffic, for legal or business reasons, and some inject advertisements or tracking into unencrypted web pages. You cannot verify any of this from your room, which is why a VPN, covered in section twenty-two, is especially valuable in hotels: it encrypts your traffic before it reaches the hotel's equipment, protecting you from both other guests and the hotel's own systems.

Practical hotel habits help too. Ask reception for the exact network name, to avoid fake hotspots in the lobby, as section eighteen warned, and be wary of networks with similar names. Prefer a wired connection in your room, if the hotel offers an ethernet port, because a cable is not shared through the air and is harder for other guests to intercept, as chapter fourteen explained. If you must do sensitive work, consider using your phone's hotspot instead, as section twenty-nine explained, especially for banking or work systems. And when you check out, forget the hotel's network, as section twenty-six advised, so your device does not reconnect to a fake version of it on a future trip.

Finally, be careful with hotel services that use Wi-Fi in other ways. Smart televisions in hotel rooms may ask you to sign into your streaming accounts, which you should avoid, or sign out of carefully if you must, because the next guest could access your account. Hotel business centres, with shared computers, are a separate risk, covered in chapter sixteen, and should not be used for sensitive accounts at all. Hotel Wi-Fi, used with a VPN, HTTPS, careful network choice, and a forget-on-checkout habit, is perfectly usable for ordinary travel needs. It simply is not, and should never be treated as, a private network.

Check: Why should hotel Wi-Fi be treated as public Wi-Fi, even though it requires a room number to join?

Section 32: Wi-Fi in Airports and Stations

Airports and train stations are among the busiest public Wi-Fi environments, with thousands of travellers, many networks, and a high proportion of tired, hurried people, which makes them attractive to attackers and a good test of your Wi-Fi habits. Most large airports offer free Wi-Fi, often for a limited time or with a captive portal, and many stations offer similar services. The networks are run by professional providers, better maintained than a small café's router, but they are still public, shared, and untrusted, and the number of people nearby increases the chance that someone is snooping or running a fake hotspot.

The first airport-specific risk is the fake hotspot, covered in section eighteen, amplified by scale. In a busy terminal, you may see a dozen networks with similar names: Airport Free Wi-Fi, Airport WiFi, Free Airport, Airport Guest, and variations with different spellings or capitalisation. Only one, or a few, are genuine. Attackers exploit this confusion by creating a network with a plausible name and a strong signal near seating areas, capturing travellers who join quickly without checking. The defence is to ask staff or check official signs for the exact network name, and to be suspicious of any network with an unusually strong signal or asking for unusual details, such as a credit card number for free Wi-Fi, which no genuine airport network should require.

The second risk is time pressure. Travellers often join the first network they see, accept terms without reading, and immediately check email, banking, or work, because a flight is boarding or a train is due. That haste is exactly what attackers rely on. Build a small airport routine: join only the verified network, wait for the captive portal, complete it, then switch on your VPN before anything sensitive, as section twenty-two advised. If you cannot verify the network or your VPN will not connect, use mobile data for sensitive tasks, and the airport network only for maps, boarding passes, and entertainment. A few minutes of care is worth more than a rushed connection.

The third consideration is your device's behaviour in a busy place. With Wi-Fi on, your phone will scan, probe for saved networks, and possibly join familiar names automatically, as sections two and twenty-five explained, and in an airport it may encounter dozens of networks, including fake versions of networks you joined on previous trips. Switching Wi-Fi off while moving through the terminal, and switching it on only when you sit down, reduces that exposure. Forgetting old airport networks after your trip, as section twenty-six advised, prevents your device from rejoining fake versions of them on your next journey.

Finally, remember physical safety alongside network safety. Airports and stations are busy, distracting places where devices are stolen, screens are overlooked, and chargers are shared. Do not use public USB charging ports without caution, because some can be manipulated to access your device, a risk called juice jacking, covered in chapter sixteen: prefer your own charger and a wall socket. Keep your device in sight, lock its screen when not using it, and be aware of who can see your screen when entering passwords. Airport Wi-Fi, used with verification, a VPN, and deliberate connection habits, is a useful travel tool. Used hastily, it is one of the easiest places to be caught.

Check: Why are airports and stations especially attractive places for fake Wi-Fi hotspots?

Section 33: Wi-Fi in Cafés and Restaurants

Cafés and restaurants are where most people first encounter public Wi-Fi, and they are also where the most relaxed, and therefore riskiest, Wi-Fi habits form. You sit down, order a coffee, join the Wi-Fi without thinking, and work, chat, or browse for an hour, surrounded by strangers who share the same network. Café Wi-Fi is usually run by the café owner or a small provider, on a consumer router, with a shared password displayed on a wall or a receipt, and rarely monitored. That informality is part of its charm, and part of its risk.

The shared password is the first issue, as section sixteen explained. Everyone in the café knows the same password, which means everyone shares the same encryption keys, if encryption is enabled at all, and an attacker sitting two tables away has the same network access as you. That does not mean they can read your HTTPS traffic, which remains encrypted, but it does mean they can see metadata, attempt to manipulate unencrypted traffic, and probe your device for open sharing features. Keep sharing off, use HTTPS, and use a VPN for sensitive tasks, as sections twenty to twenty-two advised, even in a friendly neighbourhood café where everyone seems trustworthy. Trust is not a security control.

The second issue is the casualness of café use. People linger, leave devices unattended to order or visit the bathroom, and let friends or strangers use their devices briefly, all of which create opportunities for theft, snooping, or unauthorised access. Lock your screen whenever you leave your table, even for a minute, and do not leave a laptop open and connected while you are away. Be aware of shoulder surfing: in a small café, the person behind you can easily read your screen, including passwords, messages, and work documents, so sit with your back to a wall where possible, use a privacy screen filter if you often work in cafés, and avoid entering sensitive credentials where others can see.

The third issue is the network's own honesty. A café's Wi-Fi password, displayed publicly, may be photographed and shared online, allowing people outside the café to join from the street, and the café's router may be old, unpatched, or still using default admin credentials, making it vulnerable to takeover. You cannot fix the café's router, but you can protect yourself: verify the network name with staff, be suspicious of similar names, as section eighteen warned, and treat the network as untrusted regardless of how cosy the café feels. A pleasant atmosphere is not a security guarantee.

Café Wi-Fi, used thoughtfully, is a wonderful resource: a place to work, study, and connect, with coffee. The habits that make it safe are the same as elsewhere in this chapter, but they matter more in cafés precisely because the setting encourages relaxation. Verify the network, use HTTPS, switch on a VPN for sensitive tasks, lock your screen, and forget the network when you leave if you do not expect to return regularly. Enjoy the coffee. Keep your data as carefully as you keep your wallet on the table.

Check: Why does a café's shared Wi-Fi password not protect you from other customers on the same network?

Section 34: Wi-Fi at School and University

Schools and universities run some of the largest Wi-Fi networks most people will ever use, covering campuses, dormitories, libraries, and classrooms, with thousands of students, staff, and visitors connecting daily. These networks are usually professionally managed, with better equipment and monitoring than a café's router, but they are still shared networks, and they come with their own rules, risks, and responsibilities. Understanding how school and university Wi-Fi differs from home and café Wi-Fi helps students use it safely and within the rules.

The main difference is individual accounts. Most universities, and many schools, give each student their own Wi-Fi login, rather than a single shared password, which separates users' traffic and allows the institution to manage access, apply filtering, and investigate abuse. This is considerably safer than a shared password, because other students cannot easily snoop on your traffic or impersonate you, and it also means your activity can be linked to your account. School and university networks are usually monitored, within policy and law, for security and for compliance with acceptable-use rules, and students should assume their network activity is logged, as chapter fourteen explained for workplaces. Use school networks for school work, keep personal activity on personal networks where possible, and do not attempt to bypass filtering or monitoring, which usually breaches policy and may have disciplinary consequences.

The second difference is scale and diversity. A campus network hosts a huge variety of devices, including personal laptops, phones, game consoles, smart devices, and research equipment, some of which may be outdated, infected, or misconfigured. That diversity increases the chance that a compromised device is nearby on the same network, which is why individual logins, device firewalls, and updated software matter even more on campus than at home. Keep your device's firewall on, sharing off unless needed, and software updated, and be careful about connecting to peer-to-peer shares or open folders offered by other students, which may contain malware or copyrighted material you should not access.

Dormitories deserve special mention, because they blur the line between home and school. Your dorm room may feel private, but its network is shared with hundreds of other students, and your roommates, visitors, and neighbours may have physical access to your devices. Use your individual login, not a shared one, keep your devices locked, and do not share your Wi-Fi credentials with friends, even trusted ones, because shared credentials destroy accountability and may breach your institution's rules. If your dorm offers a wired connection as well as Wi-Fi, prefer the cable for your main computer, as chapter fourteen advised, because it is less exposed than wireless.

Finally, use campus Wi-Fi as a learning opportunity. Universities often offer security advice, free antivirus, VPN services for remote access, and IT help desks, all of which are worth using. If you notice a suspicious network on campus, a fake hotspot mimicking the university's name, or a security problem, report it to IT, as section forty-six advises, because reporting protects the whole campus. School and university Wi-Fi, used within its rules, with individual logins, updated devices, and sensible caution, is a safe and powerful resource for learning. Treat it as a shared, managed space, not as your private home network, and it will serve you well.

Check: Why is an individual Wi-Fi login at a university safer than a single shared password for all students?

Section 35: Wi-Fi at Work: Guest vs Staff Networks

Most workplaces offer at least two Wi-Fi networks: a staff network for employees, and a guest network for visitors, clients, and contractors, and understanding the difference between them is essential for work Wi-Fi safety. The staff network gives access to internal systems, shared drives, printers, and work tools, and it is protected by individual logins, strong encryption, and IT management. The guest network gives internet access only, isolated from internal systems, and it is intended for people who do not work there. Using the wrong network, or blurring the boundary between them, creates risks for both you and your employer.

As an employee, you should use the staff network for work, with your own login, never sharing your credentials with colleagues, visitors, or family, as chapter fourteen explained. Your staff login links your activity to you, which protects you if something goes wrong, because it proves who did what, and it allows IT to give you access to the systems your job needs, and no more, following least privilege. If you work from a personal device under a bring-your-own-device policy, follow your employer's setup instructions carefully, including any VPN, security software, or device management they require, because a personal device on a staff network without those controls is a risk to the whole office.

As a visitor, use the guest network, and do not ask employees for the staff password, even if you are a trusted client or contractor. The guest network exists precisely so visitors can work online without accessing internal systems, and asking for staff access puts the employee in an awkward position and may breach policy. If you genuinely need access to an internal system as a contractor, request it formally through IT, who can grant limited, monitored access, rather than borrowing someone's credentials. Borrowed credentials are a policy breach for both parties, and they destroy accountability if anything goes wrong.

Employers also have responsibilities for work Wi-Fi. Staff networks should use modern encryption, WPA3 or WPA2 with strong, individual credentials, not a single shared password that never changes, because a shared staff password, known to every current and former employee, is barely better than no password at all. Guest networks should be isolated from staff networks, with their own passwords changed regularly, and with basic protections, such as blocking access to internal systems and logging guest activity within policy. IT teams should also monitor both networks for rogue hotspots, fake networks set up nearby to capture staff credentials, and for devices that should not be there.

Finally, when working away from the office, remember that work Wi-Fi rules travel with you. A staff laptop on a café network should use the employer's VPN, as section twenty-four explained, and should not be used to share its connection with strangers. A guest network at a client's office should be treated as public Wi-Fi, with the same cautions as any other untrusted network. Work Wi-Fi safety is about boundaries: staff and guest, internal and internet, trusted and untrusted. Respect those boundaries, use the network intended for you, and keep your credentials to yourself, and work Wi-Fi will be both convenient and safe.

Section 36: Checking a Public Network Before You Join

Before you join any public Wi-Fi network, a ten-second check can prevent most of the risks in this chapter. It is tempting to tap the first network name you see and get online, especially when you are busy, but that haste is what fake hotspots and misconfigured networks rely on. A short, deliberate check, done every time, becomes a habit quickly, and it costs far less time than dealing with a compromised account afterwards. This section gives you that check, step by step.

First, verify the network name. Ask staff, or check an official sign, for the exact name, and compare it character by character with what your device shows, as section eighteen advised. Be suspicious of duplicates, near-miss spellings, and networks with unusually strong signals. If the venue's genuine network requires a password or a captive portal, and the network you are about to join is open with no portal, that difference is a warning: it may be a fake set up to capture users who do not check. When in doubt, ask. Staff are used to the question, and a genuine venue will happily confirm its network name.

Second, check your device's warnings before you proceed. If your device warns that a network is unsecured, insecure, or uses weak security, believe it. That warning means the network has no encryption or outdated encryption, and your traffic will be more exposed, as section seven explained. You can still use such a network for low-risk activities, with HTTPS and a VPN, but you should not use it for sensitive tasks without a VPN, and you should prefer mobile data if a VPN is unavailable. If your browser shows certificate warnings after joining, stop and disconnect, rather than clicking through, because those warnings may indicate interception.

Third, check what the network asks for. A legitimate captive portal, covered in section nineteen, asks for acceptance of terms, a room number, or a voucher code. It should never ask for your email password, bank login, or social media credentials. If it does, disconnect immediately and report it to staff. Also be wary of portals that demand excessive personal details, such as your full address and phone number for a short café session, and consider using mobile data instead if the exchange feels disproportionate. Your personal details are not the price of Wi-Fi.

Finally, after joining, switch on your VPN before sensitive activities, confirm it shows connected, and keep sharing off, as sections twenty-two and twenty-seven advised. Then, when you finish, disconnect and forget the network if you do not expect to return, as section twenty-six explained. This four-part check, verify the name, heed warnings, question what it asks for, and protect yourself with a VPN, takes seconds once it is habit, and it turns public Wi-Fi from a gamble into a deliberate choice. The best network check is the one you actually do, every time, without exception.

Section 37: When Public Wi-Fi Asks for Personal Details

Public Wi-Fi networks often ask for personal details before granting access, through a captive portal, a sign-up form, or a social media login, and deciding what to share deserves thought, because those details have value beyond the Wi-Fi session. A café that asks for your email address gains a marketing contact. A hotel that asks for your room number verifies you are a guest. An airport that asks for your phone number may send you a code, or may add you to a marketing list. Not all requests are unreasonable, but none should be accepted blindly, and some should be refused outright.

The first principle is proportionality. Ask yourself whether the details requested match the service offered. An email address for a loyalty scheme or a receipt is proportionate. A room number for hotel Wi-Fi is proportionate. A full name, date of birth, home address, and phone number for thirty minutes of café Wi-Fi is not, and you should consider using mobile data instead, or choosing a different venue. A network that demands a social media login, giving it access to your profile, friends, or posts, in exchange for Wi-Fi, is asking for far more than internet access is worth, and you should refuse it. Your social media account is not a Wi-Fi voucher.

The second principle is never share passwords. No legitimate public Wi-Fi network should ever ask for your email password, your bank password, or any other account password, as section nineteen warned. If a portal asks for a password, it is either fake or dangerously incompetent, and you should disconnect immediately, forget the network, and report it to staff. Similarly, be wary of portals that ask you to install an app, a profile, or a certificate to use the Wi-Fi, because those can give the network operator deep access to your device, including the ability to intercept encrypted traffic. A genuine public network should work with your device's normal Wi-Fi, without extra software.

The third principle is to minimise what you share, even when a request seems reasonable. Use a secondary email address for Wi-Fi sign-ups, rather than your main one, to reduce spam and to limit the damage if that address is leaked or sold. Avoid using your real phone number if a code can be sent another way, or if the network offers a guest option without a number. Read the terms briefly, looking for mentions of data sharing, marketing, or tracking, and if the terms say your data will be shared with third parties for marketing, weigh that against the value of the Wi-Fi. Often, the answer is that mobile data, or waiting, is better.

Finally, remember that details you share through a captive portal may be stored, shared, or breached long after your Wi-Fi session ends. An email address given to a café in 2024 may still be receiving marketing in 2027, and a phone number given to an airport network may be sold to data brokers. That persistence is the real cost of casual sharing, and it is why minimising matters even for small details. Public Wi-Fi is a transaction, and like any transaction, you should know what you are paying, and decide whether it is worth it, before you agree.

Section 38: Free Wi-Fi Scams and Tricks

Free Wi-Fi attracts scams, because it attracts people who are busy, trusting, and eager to get online, and scammers have developed several tricks that go beyond fake hotspots. Knowing these tricks helps you recognise them quickly, because they share a pattern: urgency, a plausible setting, and a request for something valuable, such as a password, a payment, or an app installation, in exchange for something free. Free Wi-Fi should never cost you credentials, money beyond a stated fee, or control of your device.

The first trick is the fake hotspot, covered in section eighteen, but with a payment twist: a network that claims to be free, then shows a portal demanding a credit card number for verification, a small fee, or a premium upgrade, before granting access. No genuine free network should require a credit card for verification. If you encounter one, disconnect immediately, do not enter card details, and check your accounts if you already did, reporting any charge to your bank. A similar trick uses a fake portal that mimics a well-known brand, such as a coffee chain or an airline, asking you to sign in with your account for that brand, capturing your credentials for reuse elsewhere.

The second trick is the malicious download or update. On a manipulated network, an attacker may intercept your browsing and show a convincing pop-up claiming your device needs an urgent update, a security scan, or a Wi-Fi helper app to stay connected. Clicking it downloads malware, or installs a profile that lets the attacker intercept your encrypted traffic. Never install software, updates, or profiles prompted by a public Wi-Fi network. Updates should come only from your device's official update system or app store, on a trusted network, as chapter twelve advised. A Wi-Fi network that insists you install something to use it is not a network you should use.

The third trick is social engineering in person. In a busy café or airport, someone may offer to help you connect, share their hotspot, or fix your connection, while actually guiding you to a fake network, shoulder surfing your password, or installing something on your device. Be polite but cautious with strangers offering unsolicited tech help in public places, and do not hand your unlocked device to anyone you do not know and trust. Genuine staff wear identification and work at a desk or counter. A stranger who approaches you in seating areas offering Wi-Fi help deserves scepticism, not gratitude.

The defence against all these tricks is the same set of habits from this chapter: verify network names with staff, never enter passwords or card details into a Wi-Fi portal, never install software prompted by a network, use HTTPS and a VPN, and trust your instincts when something feels off. If a free Wi-Fi offer feels too pushy, too detailed, or too good, it probably is, and mobile data is a safe alternative. Free Wi-Fi is a convenience, not a necessity, and no convenience is worth your credentials.

Section 39: Wi-Fi and Children

Children use Wi-Fi at home, at school, and in public places, often on devices they understand better than the adults around them, but with less awareness of the risks. Wi-Fi safety for children builds on chapter fourteen's family network advice and section thirty of that chapter on parental controls, but it adds Wi-Fi-specific habits: managing automatic connection, choosing safe networks, and understanding that public Wi-Fi is not private. Teaching these habits early, calmly, and by example, gives children skills they will use for life.

At home, children's devices should use the main or family network, not the guest network, so parents can manage them, and the router admin password should stay private, because a child who finds it can undo controls, as chapter fourteen warned. At school, children should use their individual logins, never share them with friends, and understand that school networks are monitored, as section thirty-four explained. The conversation matters as much as the controls: explain, in plain words from this chapter, that Wi-Fi is radio waves that travel beyond walls, that strangers nearby can see unprotected traffic, and that passwords should never be shared, even with friends. A child who understands why will follow the rule when no adult is watching.

In public places, children need specific guidance. Teach them never to join a Wi-Fi network without asking a trusted adult first, never to enter passwords, personal details, or payment information on public Wi-Fi, and never to accept help with connecting from a stranger. Show them how to check a network name with staff, how to recognise a padlock in a browser, and how to switch Wi-Fi off when not using it, as section twenty-seven advised. These are simple, concrete skills, and practising them together, on a family outing, turns abstract warnings into muscle memory.

Also teach children about the social side of public Wi-Fi. A child on a café network may be approached online by strangers, through games, chats, or social apps, and public Wi-Fi does not make those approaches safer. The rules from chapter ten on staying safe online apply with extra force in public: do not share personal details, photos, or location with strangers, do not meet online contacts without a trusted adult knowing, and report anything worrying, such as a stranger asking for photos or personal information, to a trusted adult immediately. Reporting should be praised, never punished, or children will stop reporting.

Finally, model the behaviour you want. If you join any network without checking, enter passwords on public Wi-Fi, or leave your device unlocked in a café, children will copy that, regardless of what you tell them. If you verify networks, use a VPN for sensitive tasks, lock your screen, and talk about why, they will copy that too. Wi-Fi safety for children is not a separate subject. It is the same habits from this chapter, taught patiently, practised together, and modelled consistently, until they become the child's own habits.

Section 40: Helping Older Relatives with Wi-Fi Safety

Older relatives often need help with Wi-Fi, and helping them safely is a kindness that also protects them, because older adults are frequent targets for scams, including Wi-Fi-related ones, and they may be less familiar with the risks in this chapter. Helping well means being patient, setting things up securely for them, and teaching a few simple habits, rather than overwhelming them with technical detail or taking over their devices entirely.

Start by setting up their home Wi-Fi securely, following sections four to ten: a strong, unique password, WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, a guest network for visitors, and a network name that does not identify them. Write the Wi-Fi password down safely, in a place they can find it, such as a notebook kept securely at home, or store it in a password manager if they use one, and show them where it is, so they do not need to call you every time a visitor asks. If their router is old and only supports WEP or weak security, help them replace it or ask their provider for a modern one, explaining that an old router is like an old lock: it may look fine, but it no longer keeps anyone out.

Next, teach a few simple, memorable habits, not a long list. Three habits cover most Wi-Fi risk for an older adult: only join Wi-Fi networks you know and trust, at home or at places you visit regularly; never enter passwords, bank details, or personal information on a public Wi-Fi network, and call a trusted family member if a website or message asks you to; and never install an app or update prompted by a Wi-Fi network or a pop-up, but wait and ask for help first. These three rules are easy to remember, easy to repeat, and they block the most common Wi-Fi scams, including fake hotspots, phishing portals, and malicious downloads, covered in sections eighteen and thirty-eight.

Also help them with their phone settings, as section twenty-seven advised: switch off automatic joining for public networks, remove saved networks from past trips, and switch Wi-Fi off when not using it, if they are comfortable with that. Show them, slowly and hands-on, how to forget a network, how to check for a padlock in a browser, and how to spot a suspicious message, and practise together, rather than simply doing it for them. Patience matters more than speed: an older relative who feels rushed or talked down to will stop asking for help, and will then face the next scam alone.

Finally, make yourself approachable for future questions. Tell them explicitly that it is always okay to call you before clicking, joining, or paying, and that you will never be annoyed by a false alarm. Scammers rely on urgency and embarrassment: a victim who feels too embarrassed to ask for help is a victim who stays silent. An older relative who knows they can call you, without judgement, at any time, is far harder to scam, on Wi-Fi or anywhere else. Helping older relatives with Wi-Fi safety is not a one-time setup. It is an ongoing relationship, built on patience, respect, and a phone call away.

Section 41: Wi-Fi Extenders and Mesh Systems Safely

Large homes, thick walls, and gardens can leave Wi-Fi dead zones, rooms where the signal is too weak to use, and two common solutions are Wi-Fi extenders and mesh systems. An extender is a small device that picks up your router's signal and rebroadcasts it, extending coverage into a distant room. A mesh system is a set of units that work together as one network, with your devices moving between units automatically as you walk around. Both can improve coverage greatly, and both need to be set up safely, or they become new weak points on your network.

An extender's main safety consideration is that it creates a new point of entry to your network, often with its own admin page, password, and firmware, separate from your router's. Many people set up an extender once, with a weak or default admin password, and never update it, leaving a device that, if compromised, gives an attacker access to your network from a distant room or even outside. When you set up an extender, change its admin password from the default, update its firmware, and use the same strong Wi-Fi password and encryption, WPA2 or WPA3, as your main network, so it does not become a back door. Check for extender firmware updates occasionally, alongside your router updates, as chapter fourteen advised.

A mesh system is usually safer and simpler than an extender, because its units are managed together through one app, with automatic updates and consistent security settings across the whole system. That convenience has a trade-off: mesh systems often rely on a cloud account and a phone app, which means your network's management depends on that account's security. Use a strong, unique password for the mesh app account, switch on multi-factor authentication if offered, and be aware of what data the mesh provider collects, such as device names, usage patterns, and network performance, which is usually described in its privacy policy. A mesh system managed through a weak cloud password is only as safe as that password.

Placement matters for both extenders and mesh units, for safety as well as coverage. Place units inside your home, not in windows or outside walls where their signals spill far beyond your property, as section thirteen advised for routers. An extender in a garden shed or a window, broadcasting your network to the street, extends your exposure as well as your coverage. Place units centrally in the rooms they serve, test coverage after setup, and remove or relocate units that are not needed. More units are not always better: each additional unit is another device to secure, another signal to manage, and another potential entry point.

Finally, when you replace or discard an extender or mesh unit, reset it to factory settings first, so your Wi-Fi passwords, network names, and account details do not remain on a device that goes to a new owner, a charity shop, or recycling. The same applies to your old router, as chapter fourteen noted. Extenders and mesh systems, set up with strong passwords, updated firmware, sensible placement, and proper disposal, turn dead zones into safe, usable spaces. Set up carelessly, they turn a coverage problem into a security problem, which is a worse trade.

Section 42: Generating a Strong Wi-Fi Password with Python

A strong Wi-Fi password, as section five explained, should be long, random, and unique, and the easiest way to create one is to let your computer generate it for you, rather than inventing it yourself. Human-invented passwords follow patterns, favourite words, dates, and sequences, which attackers can guess. Computer-generated passwords, made from a secure random source, have no patterns to guess. This section shows you a short Python program that generates a strong Wi-Fi password on your own computer, using only Python's standard library, with no extra software to install and no data sent anywhere.

Python's secrets module is designed for generating passwords and tokens, because it uses a random source suitable for security, unlike the older random module, which is designed for games and simulations and should never be used for passwords. The program below asks for a length, defaulting to twenty-four characters, and builds a password from uppercase letters, lowercase letters, digits, and symbols, choosing each character securely at random. You can run it on your own computer, copy the result into your router's Wi-Fi password field, following section nine, and store it in your password manager. The program never sends the password anywhere, and it never saves it to a file, unless you deliberately choose to.

import secrets
import string

def make_wifi_password(length=24):
    alphabet = string.ascii_letters + string.digits + "!@#$%^&*"
    return "".join(secrets.choice(alphabet) for _ in range(length))

password = make_wifi_password()
print("Your new Wi-Fi password is:")
print(password)
print("Store it safely and do not share it publicly.")

A generated password like this looks intimidating, a jumble of characters with no meaning, but you will rarely need to type it. You will enter it once per device, and then your device remembers it, as section five explained. If you prefer a password that is easier to type, for example for a guest network shared verbally, you can generate a passphrase instead: several random words joined together, which is also strong if the words are chosen randomly and the phrase is long enough. The program below generates a passphrase from a small word list, purely as an example: in real use, you should use a larger word list, or your password manager's passphrase generator, because a short list makes the passphrase easier to guess.

import secrets

words = ["river", "candle", "meadow", "harbour", "velvet",
         "orchard", "marble", "compass", "willow", "copper",
         "falcon", "meadow", "harbour", "lantern", "prairie"]
phrase = "-".join(secrets.choice(words) for _ in range(5))
print("Your passphrase is:")
print(phrase)

Whichever style you choose, generate it, do not invent it, store it safely, and use a different password for each network: your main network, your guest network, and your hotspot should all differ, so a leak of one does not open the others. A password generator is a small tool with a large payoff, turning the hardest part of Wi-Fi safety, choosing a password no one can guess, into a few seconds of work on your own computer.

A final tip: test your generated password on one device first, confirming it connects, before rolling it out to the rest of your household, so a copying error does not lock everyone out at once. If a device cannot accept symbols, reduce the symbol set rather than shortening the password, because length matters more than symbol variety for resisting guessing attacks against modern Wi-Fi encryption.

Section 43: Reviewing Your Saved Wi-Fi Networks with Python

Section twenty-six advised you to forget Wi-Fi networks you no longer use, and this section shows you a simple way to review that list systematically, using a short Python program on your own computer. The program does not read your device's saved networks directly, because different operating systems store them in different places, and reading them requires system-specific tools. Instead, it works from a list you type in yourself, your own saved networks, copied from your phone or laptop's Wi-Fi settings, and it flags the ones most worth reviewing. That keeps the program simple, portable, and entirely under your control, with no access to anyone else's data.

The idea is straightforward. You list the network names you have saved, one per line, and the program checks each name against warning signs: names containing words like Free, Public, Guest, or Airport, which often indicate public networks; names you have marked as old, such as a previous home or job; and duplicates, where similar names may indicate a genuine network and a fake copy. It then prints a short report, grouping networks into keep, for your current home and trusted networks, review, for public or old networks, and remove, for duplicates or networks you do not recognise. You make the final decision for each network, on your device, following section twenty-six. The program advises. You decide.

saved_networks = [
    "Home Wi-Fi",
    "Free Airport Wi-Fi",
    "Old Flat Wi-Fi",
    "Cafe Guest",
    "University Wi-Fi",
    "Free Airport Wi-Fi",
]

public_words = ["free", "public", "guest", "airport", "hotel", "cafe"]
seen = set()
for name in saved_networks:
    lower = name.lower()
    if name in seen:
        print(name, "-> REMOVE: duplicate entry")
    elif any(word in lower for word in public_words):
        print(name, "-> REVIEW: public network, forget if not needed")
    elif "old" in lower:
        print(name, "-> REVIEW: old network, forget if moved on")
    else:
        print(name, "-> KEEP: trusted network")
    seen.add(name)

Replace the example list with your own network names, copied from your device, and run the program on your own computer. It will not change anything on your device. It only prints advice, which you then act on by forgetting networks in your Wi-Fi settings. That separation, advice from the program, action by you, is deliberate: a program that automatically deleted saved networks could remove one you still need, and a program that only advises cannot harm you. It also means you stay in control of your own data, reviewing names you typed yourself, not granting a program access to your device's stored passwords or system files.

Reviewing saved networks this way, once or twice a year, or after a holiday, turns a vague intention, I should clean up my Wi-Fi list, into a concrete, checkable task. Share the habit with family members too, because their devices carry the same saved networks, and a household that reviews together stays safer than one person tidying alone. Pair it with the habit from section twenty-six: after a trip, forget the networks you joined while away, before you forget you joined them. Your saved network list should reflect your current life, not every place you have ever been, and a short program on your own computer can help you keep it that way, without any risk to anyone else's data.

Section 44: Wi-Fi Safety Myths and Misunderstandings

Wi-Fi attracts myths, because it is invisible, technical, and full of jargon, and myths lead to false confidence or unnecessary fear. Chapter fourteen covered network myths generally, and this section tackles the Wi-Fi-specific ones you are most likely to hear from friends, advertisements, and well-meaning advice online. Clearing them up helps you spend your effort on the habits that actually protect you, and ignore the ones that only feel protective.

The first myth is that a strong Wi-Fi signal means a safe network. Signal strength tells you how close you are to the router and how clear the radio path is. It tells you nothing about encryption, passwords, or who else is on the network. A fake hotspot placed close to you, as section eighteen warned, will show a very strong signal, precisely because it is close, while a genuine network in the next room may show a weaker one. Judge networks by their name, verified with staff, their security type, and what they ask for, not by how many bars your phone shows. Bars measure radio, not trust.

The second myth is that changing your Wi-Fi password frequently makes you safer. Changing a password when circumstances change, a housemate leaves, a device is lost, or a password was shared widely, is good hygiene, as section nine explained. Changing it on a fixed schedule, every week or month, for no reason, adds inconvenience without adding safety, because a strong, unshared password does not weaken with age. Worse, frequent forced changes encourage people to choose weaker, predictable passwords, or to write them on notes stuck to the router, which creates new risks. Change your Wi-Fi password when there is a reason, not on a timer.

The third myth is that paid Wi-Fi is safer than free Wi-Fi. Paying for Wi-Fi does not make its encryption stronger, its operator more trustworthy, or its fellow users less risky. A paid hotel network with a shared password and outdated equipment is no safer than a free café network, and a free network run by a reputable provider with individual logins may be safer than both. Price is a business model, not a security feature. Judge a network by its security, verified name, encryption, and what it asks for, as section thirty-six advised, not by whether you paid for it.

The fourth myth is that turning Wi-Fi off completely is the only safe option. Turning Wi-Fi off when not using it, as section twenty-seven advised, is a good habit, but avoiding Wi-Fi entirely is neither necessary nor practical for most people, and it pushes you towards other risks, such as using mobile data in areas with poor signal, or joining any network in desperation when you need connectivity urgently. Wi-Fi, used with the habits in this chapter, strong home passwords, modern encryption, careful public network choice, HTTPS, and a VPN for sensitive tasks, is safe enough for ordinary life. Myths make Wi-Fi seem mysterious and frightening. Reality is more reassuring: a few clear habits, applied consistently, cover almost every risk you will actually face.

Section 45: What to Do If You Used an Unsafe Network

Sometimes you realise, too late, that you used an unsafe network: you joined a hotspot that turned out to be fake, entered credentials on a suspicious portal, or did banking on an open network without a VPN, before you understood the risks in this chapter. That realisation is unpleasant, but it is not a disaster, and acting promptly, calmly, and in the right order, limits the damage greatly. This section gives you that order, so you do not have to improvise while worried.

First, disconnect and contain. Switch Wi-Fi off on your device, or disconnect from the suspicious network, and forget it, as section twenty-six explained, so your device does not reconnect automatically. If you were in the middle of a sensitive activity, stop it, and do not continue it on the same network. If you suspect your device itself may have been compromised, for example by installing something prompted by the network, as section thirty-eight warned, disconnect it from all networks and seek professional help or follow chapter twelve's malware guidance, rather than continuing to use it normally. Containment first, investigation second, is the rule from chapter fourteen, and it applies here too.

Second, change passwords, from a trusted network, in order of importance. Start with your email account, because email is the key to resetting most other accounts, then banking and financial accounts, then work accounts, then social media and shopping accounts. Use a trusted network for this, your home Wi-Fi or mobile data, not the suspicious network, and use a different device if you suspect your original device is compromised. Switch on multi-factor authentication for every account that offers it, as chapter eight explained, because even if an attacker captured a password, multi-factor authentication blocks them from using it alone. If you entered card details on a suspicious portal, contact your bank promptly, report the incident, and watch for unfamiliar transactions.

Third, check for damage. Review your email for unfamiliar messages, password reset requests, or sent messages you did not write. Review your bank and card statements for unfamiliar transactions. Review your social media and cloud accounts for unfamiliar posts, shares, or access from unknown locations, which many services show in their security settings. If you find unfamiliar activity, report it to the relevant service, change that account's password again, and keep records, screenshots, dates, and times, because they help fraud teams and, if a crime occurred, the police. Most incidents, caught promptly, cause no lasting damage, because the attacker captured only a password that you have now changed, or traffic that was encrypted by HTTPS.

Finally, learn and report. Note what went wrong: a network name you did not verify, a portal that asked for too much, a VPN you forgot to switch on, or a warning you clicked through. Fix that habit, and share the lesson with family or colleagues, without embarrassment, because everyone makes these mistakes, and sharing prevents others from repeating them. Report fake hotspots to the venue, as section forty-six explains, and report crimes to the police or your country's cybercrime service if money or identity data was involved. Using an unsafe network is a mistake, not a catastrophe, provided you act promptly, and the habits in this chapter make the next network you join a safer one.

Section 46: Reporting a Suspicious Hotspot

If you encounter a suspicious Wi-Fi hotspot, a fake network, a portal asking for passwords, or a network that behaves strangely, reporting it helps protect the next person, who might be less alert than you, and it helps the venue or provider fix the problem. Many people notice something odd, disconnect, and move on without telling anyone, which leaves the trap in place for the next traveller. Reporting takes a few minutes, and it is one of the most useful things you can do with a Wi-Fi experience, because it turns a personal near-miss into a shared defence.

Start with the venue, if the network is tied to a place. Tell a member of staff, at the counter or reception, the exact network name you saw, what it asked for, and when you saw it, and ask them to confirm their genuine network name, as section thirty-six advised. Staff may not know about the fake network, because it is often set up by someone outside, or in a seating area, without the venue's knowledge, and your report may be the first they hear of it. Be specific and calm: a network name, a time, and a description of what happened are far more useful than a vague warning that the Wi-Fi seems dodgy. If the venue has an IT contact or a manager, ask for the report to be passed to them.

If the suspicious network is not tied to a venue, or the venue cannot help, report it to the network provider, if you can identify one, or to your country's cybercrime reporting service, especially if you entered credentials or payment details, as section forty-five explained. Include the network name, location, date and time, what it asked for, and any screenshots you took, without including your own passwords in the report. Cybercrime reports help authorities track patterns, warn others, and investigate organised scams, even if your individual case cannot be fully resolved. A report that seems small to you may be the missing piece in a larger investigation.

Also warn people around you, proportionately and without causing panic. If you are in a café or airport and you spot a likely fake network, a quiet word to nearby travellers, or to staff who can warn others, may prevent someone else from joining it. Do not attempt to investigate the fake network yourself, by connecting to it deliberately, probing it, or trying to identify its operator, because that puts your own device at risk and may break the law. Your job is to notice, disconnect, and report, not to hunt the attacker. Leave investigation to professionals with the tools and authority to do it safely.

Finally, report within your own circles too. Tell family, friends, or colleagues about the experience, in plain words, without embarrassment, because shared stories are how most people learn about scams. A colleague who hears about a fake airport hotspot from you is far less likely to join one on their next trip. Reporting is not complaining. It is contributing to a shared understanding of what Wi-Fi risks actually look like, in real places, to real people, and that understanding is what makes everyone safer over time.

Section 47: Wi-Fi Safety for Small Businesses

Small businesses that offer Wi-Fi to customers, such as cafés, salons, guesthouses, and shops, have a responsibility that goes beyond their own network safety, because their customers' data passes through their network. A café's Wi-Fi is a public network, with all the risks in this chapter, but the café owner controls it, and customers trust it, often without checking. Setting up customer Wi-Fi safely protects customers, protects the business's reputation, and reduces legal risk if something goes wrong. It need not be expensive or complicated, but it does need to be deliberate.

The first step is separation, as chapter fourteen explained for business networks generally. Customer Wi-Fi should be on a separate guest network, isolated from the business network that holds payment systems, staff computers, and business files. Never give customers the password to the business network, and never run customer Wi-Fi on the same network as a payment terminal or a staff computer. Most modern routers support a guest network with isolation built in, and switching it on, with its own password, is the single most important step. If your router does not support guest isolation, consider upgrading it, because the cost of a modern router is small compared with the cost of a breach that reaches business systems through customer Wi-Fi.

The second step is sensible access controls. Use a password for customer Wi-Fi, even if you display it prominently, because an open network, as section seven explained, exposes customer traffic more than a password-protected one, even with a shared password. Change that password regularly, for example monthly, or whenever staff change, so former employees and long-past visitors cannot continue using it. Consider a captive portal, covered in section nineteen, that asks customers to accept terms, which sets expectations and provides a record of acceptance, but keep the details requested minimal, as section thirty-seven advised, and never ask customers for passwords or excessive personal information. Your customers' trust is worth more than their marketing data.

The third step is maintenance and honesty. Keep the router's firmware updated, use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, and check the network occasionally for unknown devices or suspicious activity, as section eight advised for home networks. Tell customers, through a small sign or a note on the portal, that the Wi-Fi is a public network and that they should avoid sensitive activities or use a VPN, as section twenty advised. That honesty does not drive customers away. It builds trust, because customers appreciate a business that cares about their safety, and it reduces the chance that a customer blames you if they ignore the warning and something goes wrong.

Finally, understand your legal and practical limits. You are not responsible for everything a customer does on your Wi-Fi, but you may be responsible for keeping business systems separate, for not logging excessive customer data, and for responding promptly if you learn of abuse, such as illegal downloads or a fake hotspot mimicking your network. If you discover abuse, disconnect the device if you can identify it, change the guest password, and seek advice from your internet provider or a professional. A small business that offers Wi-Fi safely, separated, password-protected, maintained, and honestly described, gives its customers a genuine benefit without putting them, or itself, at unnecessary risk.

Section 48: A Family Wi-Fi Safety Plan

A family Wi-Fi safety plan turns the habits in this chapter from one person's knowledge into a household's shared practice, so everyone, including children, older relatives, and visitors, knows what to do. A plan need not be long or formal. A single page, discussed together, stuck on a fridge or saved in a shared note, is enough, provided it covers the essentials and is reviewed occasionally. This section gives you a plan you can adapt to your own household, in plain words that every family member can understand.

Start with the home network. The plan should state the Wi-Fi password's safe storage place, so family members can find it without asking the one person who set it up, and the rule that visitors and smart devices use the guest network, not the main network, as sections eleven and fifteen explained. It should state who the router admin is, usually one adult, and that no one else should change router settings without asking them, to prevent well-meaning changes that weaken security. It should also state a simple review routine: twice a year, the admin checks connected devices, updates firmware, and reviews saved networks, as chapter fourteen's audit advised, and the family helps by reporting unfamiliar devices or problems promptly.

Next, cover public Wi-Fi. The plan should state three simple rules for everyone, including children and older relatives: only join networks you know and trust, or that a trusted adult has verified; never enter passwords, bank details, or personal information on public Wi-Fi; and never install anything prompted by a network, but ask for help first. These three rules, adapted from sections thirty-nine and forty, cover most public Wi-Fi risk, and they are easy for every family member to remember, regardless of age or technical confidence. Add a fourth rule for older children and adults: use a VPN for sensitive tasks on public Wi-Fi, or use mobile data instead, as section twenty-two advised.

Then, cover what to do when something goes wrong. The plan should name a trusted adult or family member to call, without embarrassment, if anyone suspects a fake network, enters details on a suspicious portal, or notices unfamiliar activity on an account, following sections forty-five and forty-six. It should state that reporting promptly is always praised, never punished, because a child or older relative who fears blame will stay silent, and silence lets damage grow. It should also state where important passwords are stored, and how to change the Wi-Fi password if needed, following section nine, so a crisis does not become a panic about lost credentials.

Finally, review the plan together, at least once a year, or when the household changes, a child gets their first phone, an older relative moves in, or a housemate leaves. Update the Wi-Fi password when circumstances change, remove saved networks that are no longer needed, and celebrate the plan's successes, such as a family member spotting a fake network or reporting a suspicious message, because recognition reinforces habits. A family Wi-Fi safety plan is not a set of restrictions. It is a shared agreement that everyone in the household deserves a safe network, and that everyone has a part in keeping it safe.

Section 49: Wi-Fi Safety Checklist: Home

This section distils everything in this chapter about home Wi-Fi into a single checklist you can work through in about fifteen minutes, and repeat once or twice a year. A checklist is useful because home Wi-Fi safety involves several small settings, spread across your router, your devices, and your habits, and it is easy to forget one. Working through a list, ticking each item, turns vague intentions into confirmed actions, and it gives you a record to compare next time. Keep this checklist somewhere safe, and use it after any major change, such as a new router, a move, or a new housemate.

Router checks first. Admin password changed from the default, and stored safely. Firmware up to date. Wi-Fi security set to WPA3, or WPA2 if WPA3 is unavailable, never WEP or open. Wi-Fi password long, random, and unique, following section five. Network name does not identify you, your address, or your family. Guest network switched on, with its own password, for visitors and smart devices. Remote administration off, unless deliberately needed. WPS switched off, if your router allows it. Each of these is covered in sections four to eleven, and together they form the core of home Wi-Fi safety. If any item fails, fix it before moving on, because router settings protect every device at once.

Device checks next. Every main device updated, with its firewall on and protection active. File and printer sharing off, unless deliberately needed. Saved networks reviewed, with old, public, and unfamiliar networks forgotten, following section twenty-six. Smart devices on the guest network, with their own strong passwords and multi-factor authentication where offered, following section fifteen. Children's devices on the family network, with parental controls agreed and explained, following section thirty-nine. Each device you check is a device you can trust a little more, and the check itself often reveals surprises, such as a forgotten smart device or an old saved network you no longer need.

Habit checks last. Wi-Fi password changed when circumstances changed, such as a housemate leaving. Connected-devices list reviewed, with unknown devices investigated. Backups running and tested, as chapter eleven advised, because Wi-Fi safety and backup safety reinforce each other. Family Wi-Fi safety plan, from section forty-eight, reviewed and understood by everyone. If you can tick most of these items, your home Wi-Fi is safer than most, and the items you cannot tick become your next actions, not reasons for guilt. A checklist is a tool for improvement, not a test to pass perfectly on the first try.

Keep the completed checklist, with the date, so next year's check can compare. Note what you changed, what you still need to do, and any questions for further learning, such as a router feature you did not understand. Home Wi-Fi safety is not a one-time achievement. It is a routine, and this checklist is that routine, written down, ready to repeat. Use it, and your home network will stay safe with far less effort than fixing a breach afterwards.

Section 50: Wi-Fi Safety Checklist: Public and Next Steps

Public Wi-Fi safety also deserves a checklist, shorter and more portable than the home one, because you need it in the moment, in a café, airport, or hotel, not at a desk with time to think. This final section gives you that checklist, small enough to remember, and points you towards the next steps in your learning, because Wi-Fi safety is one part of a larger security journey that this book continues. Memorise the public checklist, keep the home checklist from section forty-nine for routine reviews, and you will have Wi-Fi safety covered, at home and away.

Before joining a public network, verify the network name with staff or an official sign, comparing it character by character, as sections eighteen and thirty-six advised. Check your device's warnings, and do not ignore them. Question what the network asks for: never enter passwords, bank logins, or card details into a portal, and refuse excessive personal details, as sections nineteen and thirty-seven warned. Switch on your VPN before sensitive activities, and confirm it shows connected, as section twenty-two explained. These four steps, verify, heed, question, and protect, take seconds once they are habit, and they prevent most public Wi-Fi harm.

While using a public network, prefer HTTPS sites, shown by the padlock, and official apps for banking and shopping, as sections twenty-one and twenty-three advised. Avoid sensitive activities without a VPN, and prefer mobile data for the most sensitive tasks if available. Keep sharing off, lock your screen when you step away, and be aware of who can see your screen. Do not install anything prompted by the network, do not download software from unfamiliar sources, and do not leave your device unattended and connected. These while-using habits are the everyday practice of the principles in this chapter, applied in real places, with real distractions, and they matter more than any single setting, because they govern what you actually do.

When you finish, disconnect, forget the network if you do not expect to return, and sign out of sensitive accounts, as sections twenty and twenty-six advised. If you suspect you used an unsafe network, follow section forty-five: disconnect, change passwords from a trusted network, starting with email, check for unfamiliar activity, and report fake networks to staff or authorities, as section forty-six explained. Leaving habits close the door behind you, so a network you used once does not keep exposing you afterwards through automatic reconnection.

Where next? Later chapters in this book cover passwords, accounts, phishing, and incident response in greater depth, building on the Wi-Fi habits you have learned here, and the capstone chapters will help you build a defensive audit toolkit for your own computer, including the Wi-Fi checks from sections forty-two and forty-three. Wi-Fi safety, at home and in public, is not about fear or technical mastery. It is about a few clear habits: strong passwords and modern encryption at home, careful network choice and a VPN in public, and leaving habits that close the door behind you. Those habits, practised consistently, keep you connected and safe, wherever you go.
