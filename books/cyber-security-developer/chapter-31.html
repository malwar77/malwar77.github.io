Chapter 31: Logs and How Defenders Read Them

Section 1: What Is a Log

This section is about what is a log. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain what is a log to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

A log is a record that a computer writes about something it did, something it tried to do, or something that happened to it. Think of it like a diary that is written automatically, without anyone having to remember to write it down. When a program starts, when a person signs in, when a file is opened, or when an error happens, a short entry can be added to a log. Each entry usually has a time, a source, and a short message. Defenders read these records because they show the order of events, and order matters when you are trying to understand what really happened.

Logs are useful because human memory is unreliable and computers do many things too quickly to watch by eye. A log gives you a second chance to look back after something has gone wrong, or after something has gone right and you want to know why. It can show whether a problem happened once or many times, whether it affected one account or many accounts, and whether it started before or after a change you made. Without logs, you are left guessing. With logs, you can check your guess against evidence, and that is the heart of defensive work.

Every log entry answers a few simple questions, even when the wording looks technical. When did this happen, which program or service wrote it, and what happened in plain words? Sometimes it also says who was involved, which device was used, and whether the action succeeded or failed. Learning to find those answers is more important than memorising any one format. Formats change between systems, but the questions stay the same. If you can answer when, where, what, and whether it worked, you can read almost any log.

Put what is a log into practice on your own system this week, in a small and safe way. Start with one example you control, write down what you expected to see, and then compare what you actually saw. If the result surprises you, do not rush to a conclusion; check one more source, such as a second log, a settings page, or a trusted help page. A careful note, with the date and the result, turns a single exercise into a habit you can repeat.

Section 2: Why Computers Keep Records

This section is about why computers keep records. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain why computers keep records to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

Time is the thread that ties logs together. One event on its own may mean little, but the same event repeated every minute, or a login followed seconds later by a strange change, tells a story. That is why timestamps matter so much, and why you should check whether a system clock is correct. A clock that is hours out can make a harmless event look suspicious, or hide the real order of events. Before you draw a conclusion from times, confirm the time zone and confirm the clock is roughly right.

Good log reading starts with the ordinary. Before you hunt for attacks, learn what your own system looks like on a quiet day. Which messages appear when you start the computer, sign in, open your browser, and shut down? Which warnings appear regularly and mean nothing serious? Once you know the normal pattern, a new message stands out naturally. Beginners often do the opposite: they search for scary words and worry about every warning. A calm defender first learns the baseline, then notices what has changed.

Noise is a real problem in logs. Many entries are routine, and many warnings are not emergencies. Filtering does not mean ignoring everything; it means choosing what to look at first. You might start with failures, then with security messages, then with events around a time you care about. You can count how often a message appears, which is often more useful than reading every copy of it. If the same routine message appears a thousand times, reading one copy carefully is usually enough.

Section 3: Events, Times and Sources

This section is about events, times and sources. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain events, times and sources to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

Logs must be treated with care, because they can contain private information. A log might include user names, file paths, or parts of a request that should not be shared widely. When you share a log to ask for help, remove passwords, tokens, and personal details first, and share only the part that is relevant. Keep your own logs in a safe place, just as you would keep a diary. A log that anyone can edit is not reliable evidence, so protect the original file and work from a copy when you can.

A defender reads logs in a careful order: first, decide what question you are answering; second, find the right log for that question; third, look at the time range that matters; fourth, filter and count; and fifth, write down what you found in plain words. Skipping the first step leads to endless scrolling. Skipping the last step means you will forget what you learned. A short written summary, with times and counts, is far more useful than a feeling that something looked odd.

When a log entry is hard to understand, break it into parts instead of reading it as one long string. Find the time first, then the source, then the level such as information, warning, or error, then the message itself. Look up only the words you need, and ignore codes that do not change your decision. Over time you will recognise common patterns without looking them up. The goal is not to understand every single entry perfectly; it is to understand enough to decide what to do next.

Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain logs and honest reporting to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

Good log reading starts with the ordinary. Before you hunt for attacks, learn what your own system looks like on a quiet day. Which messages appear when you start the computer, sign in, open your browser, and shut down? Which warnings appear regularly and mean nothing serious? Once you know the normal pattern, a new message stands out naturally. Beginners often do the opposite: they search for scary words and worry about every warning. A calm defender first learns the baseline, then notices what has changed.

Noise is a real problem in logs. Many entries are routine, and many warnings are not emergencies. Filtering does not mean ignoring everything; it means choosing what to look at first. You might start with failures, then with security messages, then with events around a time you care about. You can count how often a message appears, which is often more useful than reading every copy of it. If the same routine message appears a thousand times, reading one copy carefully is usually enough.

Logs must be treated with care, because they can contain private information. A log might include user names, file paths, or parts of a request that should not be shared widely. When you share a log to ask for help, remove passwords, tokens, and personal details first, and share only the part that is relevant. Keep your own logs in a safe place, just as you would keep a diary. A log that anyone can edit is not reliable evidence, so protect the original file and work from a copy when you can.

If the result surprises you, do not rush to a conclusion; check one more source, such as a second log, a settings page, or a trusted help page. A careful note, with the date and the result, turns a single exercise into a habit you can repeat. That habit is the real lesson of this section, more than any single fact, because defenders improve by repeating calm checks, not by memorising warnings. Write your finding in one plain sentence before you move on to the next entry.

Section 50: Review: Reading Logs Like a Defender

This section is about review: reading logs like a defender. It matters because it is one of the everyday habits that separate a careful defender from someone who only reacts after trouble arrives. Take it slowly, in plain words, and connect it to systems and files you own and control. The examples in this section use only your own computer, your own accounts, and your own notes. By the end, you should be able to explain review: reading logs like a defender to a friend without jargon, and to say what you would check first, what you would do next, and what you would write down.

A defender reads logs in a careful order: first, decide what question you are answering; second, find the right log for that question; third, look at the time range that matters; fourth, filter and count; and fifth, write down what you found in plain words. Skipping the first step leads to endless scrolling. Skipping the last step means you will forget what you learned. A short written summary, with times and counts, is far more useful than a feeling that something looked odd.

When a log entry is hard to understand, break it into parts instead of reading it as one long string. Find the time first, then the source, then the level such as information, warning, or error, then the message itself. Look up only the words you need, and ignore codes that do not change your decision. Over time you will recognise common patterns without looking them up. The goal is not to understand every single entry perfectly; it is to understand enough to decide what to do next.

Logs are evidence, not verdicts. A failed login does not prove an attack, and a successful login does not prove everything is safe. One entry can be a mistake, a forgotten password, or a program retrying on its own. A pattern across many entries is stronger evidence than a single entry. A good defender therefore says what the log shows, what it does not show, and what else would need to be checked. That honest wording protects you from false alarms and from missing a real problem.
