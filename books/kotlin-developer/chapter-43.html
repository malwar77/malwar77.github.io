The idea behind Android basics matters more than any single tool name, because tools change while the underlying idea stays useful. Think of keeping a design consistent as one small, practical skill you can practise on its own before you combine it with other skills. When you understand what it is for, when it helps, and when it does not help, you can make calm choices instead of copying a setup you do not understand. That calm understanding is the real goal here, and every example in this section is chosen to support it.

In everyday terms, keeping a design consistent answers a simple question: how do we handle this part of the work in a way that is clear, safe, and easy to check? Start by naming the inputs, the expected output, and the person who will use the result. For Android basics, that habit stops small misunderstandings from growing into large problems later. Imagine a tiny example first, with only a few items and no special cases, and walk through it slowly in your head. Then add one complication at a time, such as an empty input, a missing value, or a mistake in the data. Each complication teaches you something about the edges of the idea, and edges are where real programs usually fail.

A useful way to practise keeping a design consistent is to build the smallest version that still works, then test it with examples you can check by hand. Write down what you expect before you run anything, so a surprising result becomes information instead of confusion. If the result matches your expectation, you have evidence, not just hope, that your understanding is correct. If it does not match, read the difference carefully: it often points to one assumption you made without noticing it. Fix that assumption, not just the output, and test again with a second example. Repeating that loop a few times builds confidence that is based on evidence, and it keeps your learning honest and practical.

There are common mistakes with keeping a design consistent, and most of them come from rushing or from hiding complexity instead of managing it. One mistake is to assume the happy path is the only path, so empty data or an error surprises you later. Another is to copy a pattern without checking whether it fits your situation, your data, or your users. A third is to leave no trace of what happened, so when something goes wrong you cannot tell where it went wrong. The antidote to all three is the same: keep the design simple, handle the common failure cases openly, and leave a clear record you can read later. Simplicity here does not mean doing less care; it means doing the important care in a way others can see.

fun example() {
    val isVisible = true
    if (isVisible) println("View is visible")
}
