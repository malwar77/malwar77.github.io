Chapter 22: Navigation Bars and Menus

Section 1: Why Navigation Deserves Its Own Design

Think of why navigation deserves its own design as a promise to the reader: this part of the page will behave in a predictable way, and it will look like it belongs with everything around it. That promise is kept through consistency. When similar elements share spacing, colour and type, readers learn the pattern once and then relax, because they can predict what will happen next. When every element invents its own rules, readers must relearn the page on every screen, and that extra effort is felt as friction even if nobody can name it.

The core idea behind why navigation deserves its own design is simple, even when the code looks detailed. Decide what the element must communicate, choose the smallest set of properties that communicates it, and then test the result against real content. Real content is messy: headings run long, images arrive in odd sizes, and users zoom text or navigate without a mouse. A style that only survives perfect demo content is not finished. A style that survives messy content, and still looks intentional, is doing its job well.

When you build an example of why navigation deserves its own design, work in stages and check each stage before moving on. First get the structure and the normal appearance right. Then add interaction states, one at a time, and watch how the element changes. Then check narrow and wide screens, light and dark backgrounds, and the page with styles partly loaded. Each check is quick, and together they prevent the embarrassing situation where a page looks fine on your screen and broken on everyone else's screen.

Accessibility is not an extra layer to add at the end; it is part of why navigation deserves its own design from the start. Text must stay readable against its background, focus must stay visible for keyboard users, and motion must stay gentle for people who prefer reduced motion. Colour alone should never carry a meaning that text or shape does not also carry. Those choices help everyone, including readers on bright screens, older devices and slow connections, not only readers who use assistive technology.

A useful habit is to compare your version of why navigation deserves its own design with two or three examples you admire, and to ask what each one does differently. Notice the spacing first, because spacing often explains why one design feels calm and another feels crowded. Then notice colour and type, and finally notice the small details. Borrowing a principle is fine; copying a whole design without understanding it is not. Understanding lets you adapt the principle to your own content and audience.

To finish this section well, summarise why navigation deserves its own design for yourself in two or three sentences, then try to build it without looking back at the notes. If you get stuck, that stuck point tells you exactly what to review. Return to the notes, fix the gap, and try again. That loop of trying, checking and fixing is how CSS skill grows, and it is far more reliable than reading without building. Keep your experiments, because they become your personal reference library.

Section 2: The Anatomy of a Navigation Bar

Readers judge the anatomy of a navigation bar in seconds, often without realising they are judging it at all. They notice whether text is easy to scan, whether buttons look pressable, and whether the page guides their eye to the important part first. Those judgements are not mysterious; they follow from clear structure, generous but consistent spacing, and a restrained set of colours and sizes. This section teaches you to make those choices deliberately, so the result feels designed rather than accidental.

Behind the anatomy of a navigation bar there is usually one decision that matters more than the rest. Find that decision first. It might be how much space the element needs, which state must stand out, or how the element should behave when the screen shrinks. Once that decision is clear, the other properties fall into place around it. If you start with decoration instead, you will spend a long time adjusting details that never fix the underlying problem, and the page will still feel slightly wrong.

A practical way to learn the anatomy of a navigation bar is to start from a plain, unstyled element and add one rule at a time, refreshing the browser after each change. Watch what each rule actually does, not what you expected it to do. That slow, visible process teaches the relationship between properties far better than pasting a large block of code and hoping it works. It also makes debugging natural, because you already know which rule introduced a problem when one appears.

Consider the whole page, not just the single element, when you work on the anatomy of a navigation bar. An element that looks beautiful alone can still clash with its neighbours if its spacing, colour or type scale does not match the system around it. Step back, look at the page as a reader would, and check the rhythm from top to bottom. Consistency across the page is what makes a design feel professional, even when individual elements are simple and restrained.

Beginners often struggle with the anatomy of a navigation bar because they try to solve every variation at once: every screen size, every state and every edge case in a single rule. It is better to solve the common case cleanly, then add careful variations for the cases that truly need them. Each variation should have a reason you can state plainly. If you cannot state the reason, the variation is probably unnecessary complexity, and removing it will make the stylesheet easier to maintain.

When you can teach the anatomy of a navigation bar to someone else, you truly know it. Explain the purpose first, show the simplest working example, then show one variation and one mistake to avoid. That teaching structure mirrors how this section is organised, and it works because it moves from understanding to practice to judgement. Keep that structure in mind as you work through the practical task, and notice how much easier the task feels when the purpose is clear before the code begins.

.nav a {
  background: #ffffff;
  border-radius: 0.75rem;
  padding: 1.25rem;
}

To put horizontal bars with flexbox into practice, begin with a small example on a simple page, not with the whole site at once.
