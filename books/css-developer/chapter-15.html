Section 1: What Fixed Positioning Means

This section is about what fixed positioning means, and it belongs in this chapter because it is one part of how fixed and sticky elements are laid out on a page. The heart of this chapter is positioning against the viewport and against scrolling, because fixed elements stay put while the page scrolls, and sticky elements switch between flowing and staying put. As you read, keep the example in mind as a picture of what fixed positioning means, and ask yourself what would change on screen if you removed or altered the key rule. In plain terms, this idea is about how fixed and sticky elements take up room on a page and how the browser decides where the edges of that room should fall.

The browser does not guess at layout, because every rule you write is a clear instruction, and the result on screen follows from those instructions in a predictable order. When you understand this idea, you can look at a layout and explain why each piece sits where it does, instead of changing values at random until something looks right. A useful habit is to name the pieces before you style them, so you know which element is the container, which elements are the children, and what space is left over after the fixed parts are placed. The order of those steps matters, because a rule that sets a starting size is applied before a rule that grows or shrinks that size, and the final size is the result of both.

Content size also plays a part, since a long word, a wide image, or a button label cannot always be squeezed below a certain width without overflowing or breaking badly. That is why two elements with the same rules can end up at different sizes, because their content is different, and the browser respects a minimum size that keeps the content readable. When you set a size in your stylesheet, you are usually setting a preference or a starting point, not an absolute command, because other rules and content limits can still adjust the result.
