Chapter 23: Bias, Fairness and Responsible AI

Section 1: What Bias Means in AI

Bias, in AI, means a systematic difference in how a tool treats different people, topics, or kinds of input, in a way that is unfair or inaccurate. It is not the same as a single random mistake. A random mistake happens once, unpredictably, and affects anyone equally. Bias happens repeatedly, in a pattern, so that one group, style, or topic is consistently served worse than another. That pattern is what makes bias harmful: it is not bad luck for one user, but a built-in disadvantage for many users who share something in common, such as language, writing style, topic, or background, and that disadvantage repeats every time they use the tool.

It helps to distinguish bias from preference. A tool that prefers short summaries to long ones is not biased in the fairness sense, unless that preference systematically harms a group, for example by cutting out information that matters more to one group than another. A tool that sorts messages by urgency is not biased merely because it treats urgent and routine messages differently; that difference is its job. Bias appears when a difference in treatment is not justified by the task, or when it reflects an unfair pattern in the data or design, rather than a real difference in what users need. Judging bias therefore requires asking not just "is there a difference?" but "is this difference fair, justified, and accurate for this task?"

Bias can also be invisible in an overall score, as Chapter 21 warned. A tool that scores eighty-five percent overall might score ninety-five percent for one group and sixty percent for another, and the average hides that gap. That is why fairness evaluation, like other honest evaluation, must break scores down by group, by input type, and by topic, wherever that breakdown is possible and appropriate. A builder who only reports an average has not shown their tool is fair; they have shown only that they have not looked for unfairness, which is a very different claim, and one that users should treat with caution in practice.

Finally, bias is not always intentional, and it is rarely a matter of a builder deliberately favouring one group. Most bias in AI comes from ordinary, well-meaning decisions: choosing convenient data, copying existing labels, optimising for an average score, or testing on examples that resemble the builder's own experience. Those decisions, repeated across a project, can produce a tool that is systematically unfair, even though nobody intended it. That is actually good news, because it means bias can often be reduced by changing those ordinary decisions: broadening data, clarifying labels, testing diversely, and reporting breakdowns, all habits this chapter teaches in practical, local terms. A small habit that helps is naming the group or input type that might be served worse before you trust any average score.

Section 2: Where Bias Comes From: Data

Most AI bias starts in data, because tools learn patterns from the examples they are given, and those examples reflect the world as it was recorded, not the world as it should be. If a dataset contains many examples of one group and few of another, the tool will learn the majority group's patterns well and the minority group's patterns poorly, simply because it has seen them less often. That is representation bias: the data does not represent all users equally, so the tool does not serve all users equally. It is one of the most common and most fixable sources of bias, provided builders notice it and act on it.

Data can also reflect historical unfairness. If past decisions were themselves unfair, for example if certain messages were labelled urgent less often for one group, a tool trained on that data will learn and repeat those patterns, even if the builder never intended it. The tool does not know the history; it only knows the pattern, and it will reproduce the pattern as if it were a neutral fact about the task. That is why builders must examine their data critically, asking not just "is this data large?" but "whose experiences does it record, whose does it miss, and what past patterns might it be repeating?"

Data gaps are another source. A dataset might contain no examples of a dialect, style, topic, or format, because those examples were harder to collect, or collectors did not think of them. A tool tested only on the collected data will appear to work well, while failing completely on the missing kinds of input, which real users will bring. Gaps are often discovered only when a user from the missing group tries the tool and finds it useless, which is the most expensive way to learn about a gap. Proactive gap-hunting, by listing who and what your data covers and who and what it does not, catches gaps earlier, while they are still cheap to fix.

Measurement bias is subtler: it happens when the measured thing does not match what the tool should predict. For example, if a dataset records how often messages were replied to, and a tool uses that as a measure of importance, it may learn that messages from groups who receive slower replies are less important, when in fact they were simply deprioritised by past practice. The measurement, reply speed, is a flawed proxy for the real concept, importance, and the flaw systematically disadvantages one group. Checking whether your data's measurements actually match your task's concepts, for all groups, is therefore a key fairness step, not just a data-quality step.

Finally, data bias can enter through labels, which are covered in Section 5, and through the way data is cleaned. Cleaning that removes "messy" examples, such as short messages, messages with typos, or messages in non-standard styles, may disproportionately remove one group's examples, making the cleaned dataset less representative than the original. Builders should track what cleaning removes, and ask whether a rule improving tidiness also harms fairness. Data work that is careful about representation, history, gaps, measurement, and cleaning is the foundation of fair AI, and it deserves as much attention as model design.

Section 3: Where Bias Comes From: Design Choices

Bias does not only come from data; it also comes from design choices builders make, often without realising their fairness impact. One such choice is the task definition itself: what the tool is asked to do, and what counts as success. If a summariser is defined as "keep the most important facts," but importance is judged only by frequency or by expert opinion, it may systematically drop facts that matter to minority users, even though those facts are important to them. Task definitions that seem neutral can therefore encode a particular viewpoint, and builders should ask, when defining a task, "important to whom, according to whom, and for what purpose?" That question, asked early, prevents many fairness problems later.

Another choice is features: what information the tool may use. A classifier using writing style might associate a dialect with a negative label, not because the dialect causes it, but because training data correlated them historically. A search tool that ranks by popularity might systematically bury newer or minority topics, not because they are less relevant, but because they have had less time to become popular. Feature and ranking choices that seem technical are therefore also fairness choices, and they should be examined for their impact on different groups, not just for their impact on average accuracy.

Thresholds and defaults are also design choices with fairness effects. A spam filter's cutoff, as in Chapter 21, determines how many innocent messages are blocked versus how much spam slips through. If that cutoff is chosen to maximise overall accuracy, it may block innocent messages from one group more often, because that group's writing style resembles spam in the training data, or because their messages are rarer and therefore less well modelled. A default that seems neutral, such as "block if score is above 0.5," can therefore have unequal effects, and builders should test thresholds by group, and consider whether a different threshold, or a human review step for borderline cases, would reduce unfair harm without losing much overall performance.

Optimisation choices matter too. Optimising for an average score rewards improvements for the majority, because they move the average most, and gives little reward for improving a small group, even if its performance is terrible. A builder who only optimises the average will therefore naturally neglect minority groups, not out of malice, but because the metric tells them to. Fairness-aware optimisation, which also tracks the worst-performing group, or which requires a minimum score for every group, changes those incentives, and it is a design choice builders can make deliberately, by adding group breakdowns to their evaluation and to their improvement goals.

Finally, interface design can create bias, even when the tool itself is fair. A tool requiring a particular format or skill will exclude users who lack it, however fair its predictions are. A tool whose errors are hard for some users to spot or correct, because its explanations assume background knowledge they do not have, will harm those users more, even if its error rate is equal. Fair design therefore includes accessibility, clarity, and correctability for diverse users, not just fairness in predictions. A tool is not fair if some users cannot use it, understand it, or fix its mistakes, even when its scores look equal.

Section 4: Representation: Who Is Missing

Representation asks a simple question: who and what is present in your data, your test set, and your design process, and who and what is missing? Missing representation is one of the most reliable predictors of unfair performance, because tools cannot learn what they have never seen, and builders cannot fix problems they have never considered. A tool built and tested only by people like its builder, on data like the builder's own writing, will serve people like the builder well, and others poorly, not because it was designed to exclude, but because exclusion was never noticed as a design flaw.

Start representation checking with your data. List the kinds of inputs your tool will receive in real use: different topics, lengths, styles, dialects, formats, and levels of formality, and check how many examples of each kind your data contains. If a kind is missing or rare, note it as a gap, and either collect more examples, with appropriate permission, or restrict the tool's claimed scope to the kinds it actually covers, with that limit stated honestly to users. Claiming a tool works generally, when it was only tested on a narrow slice of inputs, is a fairness failure, because it invites users from missing groups to rely on a tool that was never built for them.

Representation also applies to people. Who wrote gold answers, judged outputs, and tested the tool? If they share a background, their shared blind spots become the tool's blind spots, as Chapter 21 warned. Including diverse judges and testers, where possible, even informally, by asking a few people with different backgrounds to try the tool on their own files, can reveal gaps that data counts alone miss, such as a category definition that feels clear to one group but confusing to another, or a summary style that feels helpful to experts but patronising to beginners.

Be careful, however, not to treat representation as mere counting. Having a few examples of a group in your data does not guarantee fair performance for that group, especially if those examples are stereotyped, low-quality, or unrepresentative of the group's diversity. Representation must be meaningful: examples should reflect the real variety within a group, not just its most common or most convenient form. A dataset with ten examples of a dialect, all written by one person in one style, does not represent that dialect's speakers generally, and a tool trained on it may still fail for most of them. Quality and variety within representation matter as much as quantity.

Finally, report representation honestly, with its limits. State what your data and tests covered, what they did not, and what you do not know about performance for missing groups, rather than implying general fairness from narrow testing. That honesty protects users from missing groups, who can then decide whether to use the tool cautiously, to test it themselves, or to wait for a version that covers them. It also guides future work, by naming the gaps to fill next, in priority order, based on who is most affected. Representation, checked and reported honestly, is the first step toward fairness, because you cannot fix a gap you have not named.

missing = ["notes with typos", "notes in dialect"]

Section 5: Labels and Human Judgement

Labels are the gold answers humans assign to examples, such as "spam" or "not spam," "urgent" or "routine," or "positive" or "negative." Tools learn from labels, so biased labels produce biased tools, even when the data itself is diverse. Label bias happens when different labellers, or the same labeller at different times, apply labels inconsistently, or when labels reflect a particular viewpoint as if it were universal. For example, what counts as "rude" in a message, "important" in a note, or "relevant" in a search result can vary by culture, context, and personal experience, and a label set built by one group may systematically mislabel another group's ordinary expression as rude, unimportant, or irrelevant.

One source of label bias is unclear definitions. If a category is defined vaguely, such as "flag harmful content," labellers will fill the vagueness with their own assumptions, which may differ by background. The result is a label set that is inconsistent, not because labellers are careless, but because the task was underspecified. Clear definitions, with examples for each category and for borderline cases, as in Chapter 21, reduce this bias, by giving all labellers the same standard to apply. Borderline examples are especially important, because they show where categories meet, and they prevent labellers from inventing their own boundaries in the heat of labelling many examples quickly.

Another source is labeller background. Labellers bring their own norms, expectations, and blind spots, and those shape their judgements, especially for subjective categories like tone, politeness, or quality. A message that feels direct to one labeller may feel rude to another, depending on their communication norms. If all labellers share a background, their shared norms will become the tool's norms, and users from other backgrounds will find their ordinary expression misclassified. Including labellers with different backgrounds, and tracking disagreements between them, as in Chapter 21, reveals where labels are contested, rather than universal, and those contested cases deserve special care: clearer definitions, human review, or a decision not to automate that category at all.

Disagreement itself is valuable information, not a problem to be averaged away. If labellers disagree often on a category, that category may be genuinely ambiguous, or it may be measuring something that should not be automated without human judgement. Builders should report disagreement rates, investigate high-disagreement categories, and consider whether a tool should refuse or ask a human for those cases, rather than forcing a label that no one agrees on. A tool that automates a contested judgement, with a single label presented as fact, can cause unfair harm, because it hides a genuine disagreement behind a confident output.

Finally, audit labels regularly, especially when a tool's fairness breakdowns suggest a problem. Sample labelled examples, check them against the definitions, and look for patterns: are certain kinds of inputs labelled more harshly, or more leniently, than others? Are borderline cases handled consistently? If you find label bias, correct the labels, clarify the definitions, and retrain or re-evaluate, rather than merely adjusting the tool to compensate for biased labels, because compensation without correction leaves the biased standard in place for future work. Labels are the foundation of supervised tools, and a fair foundation is worth the time it takes to build and audit carefully.

Section 6: Measuring Bias Fairly

Measuring bias starts with breaking scores down by group, as in Chapter 21, but doing it fairly requires care about how groups are defined, how large each group sample is, and what differences actually mean. A gap between two groups on a small sample may be noise, not bias, while a small gap on a large sample may be a real, systematic disadvantage. Reporting gaps without sample sizes therefore misleads in both directions: it can turn noise into an accusation, or hide a real disadvantage behind a reassuringly small number. Always report the gap, the sample size for each group, and the kind of errors involved, so readers can judge whether the gap is meaningful.

Define groups carefully and only where relevant. For a local tool, relevant groups might be input types, topics, writing styles, or lengths, rather than personal characteristics, which you should not collect or guess without good reason and permission. For example, breaking scores down by "short notes versus long notes" or "formal versus informal style" can reveal unfair performance without labelling people. If you do break down by personal characteristics, do so only with appropriate consent, with a clear purpose, and with privacy protections, as in Chapter 24, and never guess a person's group from their name or other proxy, which can itself be unfair and inaccurate.

Also measure different kinds of bias, not just accuracy gaps. A tool might be equally accurate for two groups but make different kinds of errors for each: blocking innocent messages more often for one group, while missing important messages more often for another. Those error patterns matter, because they distribute harm unequally even when accuracy looks equal. Report precision and recall by group, as in Chapter 21, and report the most harmful error type for each group separately. A fairness report that only gives accuracy by group is incomplete, however neat it looks, because it hides the distribution of harm behind a single number per group.

Be careful about small groups. With only a handful of examples, a group's score will swing wildly with one or two examples, and a confident claim about that group would be misleading. Report small-group scores as uncertain, with a note like "too few examples to judge reliably," rather than presenting a precise percentage that will change with the next few examples. That honesty protects small groups from both false reassurance and false alarm, and it guides future work: groups with too few examples need more test cases before any fairness conclusion can be drawn, which is itself a finding worth reporting.

Finally, measure bias regularly, not once. Bias can appear, disappear, or shift as data, design, and users change, as in Chapter 21's discussion of drift. A tool that was fair on an old test set may become unfair on new inputs, and only regular breakdown reporting will reveal that change. Schedule fairness checks alongside ordinary evaluations, keep the breakdowns in your evaluation log, and act when a gap grows, even if the overall score stays steady. Fairness measurement is not a one-time audit; it is a routine, like accuracy measurement, and it earns its place by catching unequal harm early, while it is still fixable.

Section 7: Fairness Is Not One Number

Fairness cannot be reduced to a single number, because different definitions of fairness can conflict, and a tool that satisfies one definition may violate another. For example, a tool might give each group the same accuracy, yet still block innocent messages more often for one group, because the groups have different base rates of the thing being detected. Or a tool might give each group the same rate of positive predictions, yet be less accurate for one group, because its predictions are less reliable there. These trade-offs are not a flaw in fairness thinking; they are a feature of real decisions, where different kinds of equality matter in different contexts, and builders must choose, openly, which kind matters most for their tool.

Consider a simple classifier that flags messages as urgent. One fairness idea is equal accuracy: the tool should be right equally often for each group. Another is equal false-positive rate: it should wrongly flag innocent messages equally often for each group. A third is equal false-negative rate: it should miss truly urgent messages equally often for each group. In many real situations, you cannot satisfy all three at once, especially when groups differ in how common urgent messages are. A builder who claims their tool is "fair" without saying which definition they used has therefore said very little, because a different definition might tell a different story about the same tool.

The honest response is to report several fairness measures, and to explain which one you prioritised and why, for your specific task and users. For a blocking tool, where false positives harm innocent users, equal false-positive rates might matter most. For an urgent-message tool, where false negatives delay help, equal false-negative rates might matter most. For a general tool, accuracy by group might be the starting point, with error breakdowns alongside it. That prioritisation, with reasoning, is part of responsible building, not an extra decoration, because it shows users and reviewers that fairness trade-offs were considered, not ignored or hidden behind a single reassuring number.

Also consider individual fairness, whether similar individuals are treated similarly, versus group fairness, whether groups are treated equally on average. A tool can be group-fair while treating two similar individuals very differently, due to a brittle feature. Conversely, a tool can treat similar individuals similarly while still producing unequal group outcomes, because the groups differ in their inputs. Both levels matter, and checking both, by looking at individual borderline cases as well as group averages, gives a fuller picture than either alone. Borderline cases, where a small change in input flips the output, are especially informative for individual fairness.

Finally, do not let the complexity of fairness definitions become an excuse for doing nothing. You do not need to satisfy every definition perfectly to make progress. Start by reporting accuracy and error breakdowns by group, choose one fairness priority that fits your task's harms, and improve it while watching the others for serious deterioration. That practical approach, reported honestly with its trade-offs, is far better than either claiming perfect fairness from a single number, or abandoning fairness work because perfect fairness is impossible. Fairness is a direction of improvement, guided by measures, not a single threshold that, once passed, ends the work.

Section 8: Equal Treatment Versus Equal Outcomes

Equal treatment means applying the same rule to everyone, without regard to group. Equal outcomes means achieving similar results for each group, even if that requires different treatment. These two ideas sound similar, but they can lead to very different tools, and confusing them is a common source of fairness mistakes. A tool that applies the same threshold to everyone is treating groups equally, in a procedural sense, yet it may still produce unequal outcomes, if groups differ in their inputs or in how well the tool models them. A tool that adjusts thresholds by group to equalise outcomes is treating groups differently, in order to achieve equal results, which some will see as fair and others as unfair. Builders must understand this tension and decide, openly, which approach fits their task.

Consider a search tool ranking by word overlap. Applying the same rule to all is equal treatment, but if one group's notes use different vocabulary, they may rank lower, producing unequal outcomes. Adjusting the ranking to account for vocabulary differences might improve outcomes for that group, but it would mean treating groups differently, which requires justification and transparency. Neither approach is automatically right; the right choice depends on whether the input differences are relevant to the task, or whether they are artefacts of data gaps and historical patterns that should be corrected, rather than faithfully reproduced.

In many local tools, the fairest approach is to fix the underlying cause, rather than adjusting outputs by group. If one group's notes rank lower because search only matches exact words, improving search to handle synonyms will help that group without group-specific rules. If one group's messages are misclassified because training data underrepresents their style, collecting more diverse examples will improve their performance without group-specific thresholds. Fixing causes is usually preferable to group-specific adjustments, because it improves the tool for everyone, it does not require labelling users by group, and it avoids the controversy and privacy risks of group-based treatment.

There are cases, however, where cause-fixing is not enough, or not possible in the short term, and where a temporary, transparent adjustment may be justified to prevent serious harm. For example, a human review step for borderline cases affecting a disadvantaged group might reduce unfair blocks while data is being improved. Such adjustments should be explicit, time-limited, reviewed regularly, and justified by evidence of harm, not applied silently or permanently without review. Silent group adjustments, discovered later, damage trust, even when they were well-intentioned, because users cannot tell whether they were fair corrections or hidden favouritism.

Finally, communicate your approach plainly to users and reviewers. State whether your tool uses the same rules for everyone, whether it makes any group-aware adjustments, and why, with evidence of the problem the adjustment addresses and the plan to remove it if causes are fixed. That transparency lets users judge fairness for themselves, and it protects builders from accusations of hidden bias, in either direction. Equal treatment and equal outcomes are both valuable ideals, but they are not the same, and a builder who names which one they prioritised, and why, is practising responsible AI, while a builder who claims both without explanation is likely hiding a trade-off they have not examined.

Section 9: Bias in Language Tools

Language tools, such as summarisers, search tools, and text classifiers, are especially prone to bias, because language itself varies by group, region, age, and context, and because tools learn language patterns from data that overrepresents some varieties and underrepresents others. A summariser trained mostly on formal writing may struggle with informal notes, shortening them poorly or dropping their key points. A classifier trained mostly on one dialect may mislabel another dialect's ordinary expressions as negative, rude, or irrelevant. A search tool that matches exact words may fail for users who use synonyms, slang, or different spellings, even when their notes contain exactly the information they need. Language bias is therefore a central fairness issue for any tool reading human text.

One pattern is style bias: tools favouring long, formal sentences over short, informal ones. It can arise from data dominated by formal examples, from features using length as a proxy for quality, or from judges rating formal outputs as clearer even when informal ones are equally accurate. The result is a tool working well for favoured styles and poorly for others, though those styles are legitimate writing, not defects. Builders should therefore test across styles, as in Section 6, and avoid features that penalise style itself, rather than task-relevant content.

Another pattern is topic bias: tools performing well on common topics and poorly on rarer ones, because common topics dominate data. A summariser might handle meeting notes well but struggle with creative or technical notes, having seen fewer examples. Topic bias harms users whose interests are less common, by giving them a worse tool for the same task, and it is often hidden by overall scores, because common topics dominate the average. Breaking scores down by topic, as well as by group, reveals topic bias, and collecting examples across topics, or restricting a tool's claimed scope to topics it handles well, are honest responses.

A third pattern is association bias: tools may associate certain groups or occupations with stereotyped attributes, because those associations appear in training data. For example, a tool that completes sentences or suggests words might associate certain roles with one gender, or certain topics with negative language, reflecting patterns in its data rather than facts about the world. For local rule-based tools in this book, this risk is smaller than for large language models, but it still appears in word lists, example sentences, and default suggestions, which builders choose. Auditing those lists and examples for stereotyped associations, as in Section 27, is a practical, local defence.

Finally, reduce language bias by designing for variety: collect examples in different styles and topics, with permission, test across them, and avoid penalising style. Use simple, robust methods, such as word overlap with synonyms, or case-insensitive matching, that are less sensitive to style differences than brittle exact-match rules. And invite users with different writing styles to test the tool on their own notes, as in Chapter 21, because their experience will reveal style bias faster than any data count. A language tool that serves diverse styles well is not just fairer; it is also more robust, because real users write diversely, and a tool that only handles one style will fail in real use, however well it scores on narrow tests.

Section 10: Bias in Classification

Classification bias appears when a tool puts items from one group into the wrong category more often, or with more harmful consequences, than items from another group. Because classification is used for sorting, flagging, and prioritising, its biases have direct effects: innocent messages blocked, urgent messages delayed, relevant items buried, or people mislabelled. Understanding classification bias therefore requires looking beyond overall accuracy, to the pattern of errors for each category and each group, as in Chapters 21 and 23's earlier sections, and asking who bears the cost of each kind of error.

A common pattern is unequal false-positive rates. A spam filter, for example, might block innocent messages from one group more often, because that group's writing style, vocabulary, or formatting resembles spam in the training data, or because their messages are rarer and therefore less well modelled. Those users experience the tool as censorious and unreliable, even if its overall accuracy is high, because they are disproportionately silenced. A builder who only reports overall accuracy will miss this harm entirely, while a builder who reports false-positive rates by group will see it immediately, and can then investigate whether the cause is data, features, thresholds, or labels, and fix the most likely cause first.

The opposite pattern, unequal false-negative rates, is equally harmful. A tool flagging urgent messages might miss them more often for one group, because urgency is expressed differently, such as indirectly or in a dialect modelled poorly. Those users experience the tool as neglectful, because their urgent needs are delayed, even though the tool works well for others. In safety-related classification, false negatives can be especially serious, because a missed urgent case may cause harm that a false positive, which merely causes inconvenience, would not. Builders must therefore weigh false positives and false negatives by their real harms, for each group, not just by their counts, when judging whether a classifier is fair.

Threshold choice, as in Section 3, is a key lever for classification fairness. Moving a threshold changes the balance between false positives and false negatives, and that balance may affect groups differently. A threshold that is optimal on average may be unfair for a group whose scores cluster near the threshold, causing many borderline cases to be misclassified for that group alone. Testing thresholds by group, and considering human review for borderline cases, can reduce this unfairness without sacrificing much overall performance. Human review for borderline cases is especially valuable, because it catches the cases where the tool is least certain, which are often the cases where bias is strongest, since uncertainty is higher for underrepresented inputs.

Finally, classification bias can be reduced by improving the underlying representation and labels, rather than merely tuning thresholds. Collecting more diverse examples, clarifying category definitions with borderline examples, and including diverse labellers, as in Sections 4 and 5, address the root causes of misclassification, while threshold tuning only adjusts its symptoms. Both approaches have a place: root-cause fixes for long-term fairness, and threshold or review adjustments for immediate harm reduction while root causes are being addressed. Reporting both, with their trade-offs, is the honest approach, because it shows users that fairness is being pursued at multiple levels, not merely by adjusting a number to look fair on one metric.

Section 11: Bias in Search and Ranking

Search tools decide what users see first, and what they may never see, so their biases shape access to information. A search tool that ranks by popularity will surface popular topics first, burying newer, rarer, or minority topics, even when those topics are exactly what the user asked for. A search tool that matches only exact words will favour users whose wording matches the documents' wording, and disadvantage users who phrase the same need differently. Ranking bias is therefore not just a technical detail; it shapes access to information, and builders must evaluate ranking fairness as carefully as they evaluate classification fairness.

One ranking pattern is position bias: users pay far more attention to the first few results than to later ones, so a small ranking disadvantage can become a large visibility disadvantage. If relevant notes for one group consistently rank fifth instead of first, while relevant notes for another group rank first, the first group's users will find their information more slowly, or not at all, even though the search technically "found" it. Evaluating ranking must therefore look at typical rank, not just whether a relevant document appears somewhere in a long list, as Chapter 21 warned for retrieval evaluation. A tool that finds relevant documents but buries them is not a fair tool, however high its overall recall looks.

Another pattern is feedback-driven ranking bias, related to Section 12: ranking by clicks or popularity rewards documents that were already visible, because visible documents get more clicks, which makes them rank higher, which makes them more visible. That loop can entrench early advantages, whether they came from quality, from timing, or from bias in the original ranking. Newer or minority documents, which start with low visibility, may never accumulate enough clicks to rise, even if they are more relevant. Builders who rank by popularity should therefore be careful: popularity is not the same as relevance, and ranking by it can systematically disadvantage newcomers and minorities, in a way that looks like user choice but is really a self-reinforcing loop.

A third pattern is query bias: tools often work better for well-formed queries than for vague or misspelled ones, more common among beginners or second-language users. A tool that only handles standard queries well will serve those users poorly, through no fault of theirs, because their queries are legitimate expressions of real needs. Testing search with diverse queries, including vague, short, misspelled, and differently phrased versions of the same need, reveals query bias, and improving robustness to query variety, for example by handling synonyms and spelling variation, is a fairness improvement as well as a quality improvement.

Finally, report ranking fairness by group or query type, with typical ranks, not just overall recall. If a tool ranks well for common queries but poorly for rare ones, say so, and either improve rare-query handling or limit the tool's claimed scope honestly. Users whose queries are rare deserve to know that the tool may serve them worse, so they can check lower-ranked results, rephrase, or seek help elsewhere, rather than assuming no relevant document exists because it did not appear first. Honest ranking evaluation, with rank breakdowns, is what turns a search tool from a popularity contest into a fair information aid.

Section 12: Feedback Loops That Amplify Bias

A feedback loop happens when a tool's outputs influence its future inputs, so that an initial bias grows larger over time, rather than staying constant. For example, a tool that recommends certain notes, topics, or messages more often will cause users to interact with those items more, generating more data about them, which the tool then uses to recommend them even more. Meanwhile, items that were initially recommended less receive less interaction, less data, and even less recommendation, in a downward spiral. Feedback loops turn small initial differences into large, entrenched inequalities, often without any builder intending that outcome, which is why they deserve special attention in fairness work.

Feedback loops appear in many local tools, not just large recommendation systems. A search tool that ranks by past clicks will, over time, favour documents that were clicked before, as in Section 11. A classifier whose outputs are used to label new training data will, over time, reinforce its own past decisions, including its past mistakes, because new labels will reflect old predictions rather than independent truth. A summariser whose summaries are saved as notes, as in Chapter 22's snowballing discussion, will, over time, treat its own past summaries as sources, amplifying any bias in those summaries. Each of these loops shares the same structure: output becomes input, and bias compounds.

Breaking feedback loops starts with separating outputs from inputs. Keep generated outputs, predictions, and recommendations in a different store from trusted source data, so that a tool does not learn from its own guesses as if they were facts. When you do use past outputs as training or evaluation data, label them as outputs, and check them independently before reuse, rather than treating them as gold answers. That separation is simple to implement for local tools, by using different folders for sources and outputs, and it prevents the most common feedback loop: a tool teaching itself its own biases, round after round, until they feel like objective truth.

Another defence is to introduce exploration: deliberately surfacing some less popular, newer, or diverse items, so they have a chance to be seen, judged, and, if good, promoted on merit, rather than being buried forever by early disadvantage. Exploration does not mean promoting poor items; it means giving unfamiliar items a fair chance to be evaluated, by showing them occasionally alongside popular ones, and by measuring their quality independently, not just by clicks, which are themselves biased by visibility. For local tools, exploration can be as simple as occasionally showing a random relevant note, or a note from a less common topic, alongside top-ranked ones, with a note explaining why.

Finally, monitor feedback loops over time, by tracking group breakdowns regularly, as in Section 6. If a gap between groups grows over months, even though the tool's code did not change, a feedback loop may be the cause, and investigating it, by checking whether outputs are becoming inputs, is the right response. Feedback loops are slow, which makes them easy to miss in a single evaluation, but also means they can be caught by trend monitoring before they become entrenched. A builder who watches trends, separates outputs from inputs, and introduces fair exploration will keep a tool's initial fairness from decaying into entrenched bias over time.

Section 13: Stereotypes in Generated Text

Stereotypes are oversimplified, often negative or limiting, ideas about a group, and generated text can reproduce them, even when a builder never intended it. A tool that suggests completions, examples, or descriptions might associate certain occupations, traits, or roles with one group more than others, because those associations appear in its training data or in its example lists. For local rule-based tools, the risk is smaller than for large language models, but it is still real, because builders choose example sentences, word lists, default categories, and illustration cases, and those choices can encode stereotypes that users will then see repeated as if they were neutral facts about the world.

One way stereotypes enter local tools is through examples. If all examples of a "manager" in a tool's documentation use one kind of name, or all examples of a "helper" use another, users may absorb the association, even if the builder never stated it explicitly. If a classifier's example messages associate a particular dialect with rudeness, or a particular topic with low importance, those associations can shape both the tool's behaviour and users' expectations. Auditing examples, as in Section 27, by listing them and asking "what associations do these examples, taken together, suggest?" is a simple, powerful check that catches stereotypes before they reach users, because stereotypes are often invisible in a single example, but obvious across a set.

Another way stereotypes enter is through word lists and categories. A word list for "positive" words that includes words associated with one group, or a category definition that treats one group's normal expression as an exception, can systematically disadvantage that group. Builders should therefore audit word lists and categories for group associations, asking whether a word or category would be judged differently if it appeared in a different group's context. If the answer is yes, the list or category needs revision, with clearer, context-based definitions, rather than word-based shortcuts that smuggle in stereotypes. That revision work is fairness work, even though it looks like ordinary editing.

Stereotypes also harm through omission, not just through negative association. If generated examples never show a group in a particular role, such as leadership, technical skill, or creativity, users may come to see that absence as normal, even though it merely reflects the builder's example choices. Inclusive examples, showing diverse people in diverse roles, in ordinary, non-stereotyped situations, counteract omission, without needing to make a political statement. The goal is not to force a particular viewpoint, but to avoid presenting a narrow slice of humanity as if it were the whole, which is what a biased example set does, whether intentionally or not.

Finally, when you find a stereotype in your tool's outputs or examples, correct it transparently, as in Chapter 22's correction habits: change the example, note the change, and add a test or checklist item to prevent recurrence. Do not dismiss stereotypes as "just examples" or "just words," because examples and words are how users learn what a tool expects and what the world it describes looks like. A tool whose examples are diverse, ordinary, and free of limiting associations will be trusted more, and will serve more users well, than one whose examples quietly reinforce narrow ideas about who does what, and who matters.

Section 14: Testing With Diverse Examples

Testing with diverse examples means deliberately including, in your test set, the full variety of inputs your tool will meet in real use, rather than testing only on convenient, familiar, or typical cases. Diverse testing is the practical counterpart to representation in data: it ensures that fairness gaps are found before release, not by users after release, when they are more costly and more harmful. A test set that includes different topics, styles, lengths, formats, and levels of formality, with each kind labelled, lets you report breakdowns by kind, as in Section 6, and lets you fix the weakest kinds first, rather than celebrating an average that hides them.

Start diverse testing by listing the dimensions of diversity that matter for your tool. For a note summariser, dimensions might include note length, topic, style, and whether the note contains dates, numbers, or lists. For a search tool, dimensions might include query length, query phrasing, spelling variation, and topic rarity. For a classifier, dimensions might include message length, tone, dialect features, and category rarity. That list, written down before you build the test set, prevents the common mistake of testing only on the dimensions you happen to think of first, which are usually the dimensions closest to your own experience, and therefore the least likely to reveal gaps for other users.

Next, build test cases for each dimension, including edge cases that are rare but important. Edge cases, such as very short notes, notes with only a title, messages with typos, or queries with synonyms, often reveal fairness and robustness problems that typical cases hide, because tools are usually optimised for typical inputs. A tool that handles typical inputs perfectly but fails on edge inputs will disappoint exactly the users whose inputs are atypical, which is a fairness problem as well as a quality problem. Including edge cases, labelled as such, in your test set, and reporting their scores separately, ensures they are not averaged away by typical-case success.

Also include cases written by different people, or in styles unlike your own, rather than writing all cases yourself. One person's cases share their phrasing and blind spots, flattering the tool and hiding weaknesses with other voices. Even a small number of cases from other writers, or cases you have deliberately written in a style unlike your own, can reveal surprising gaps, such as a search tool that fails on a synonym you would never use, or a classifier that mislabels a tone you would never write in. Diversity in test authorship is therefore as valuable as diversity in test content.

Finally, report diverse testing honestly, with breakdowns by dimension and by edge versus typical cases, and with limits, including dimensions you could not test. That report lets users judge whether the tool was tested on inputs like theirs, and it guides future testing, by naming the dimensions still to cover. A diverse test set is never finished; it grows as you learn about new kinds of inputs from real use, and each new kind, added with its gold answers and breakdown, makes the tool fairer and more robust. Diverse testing is not an extra fairness step; it is the core of honest evaluation, applied to the full variety of real users.

Section 15: Inclusive Test Sets

An inclusive test set is a test set built deliberately to include inputs from, and relevant to, diverse users, with care about how those inputs were obtained and how they are used. Inclusivity in test sets is not just about counting diverse examples; it is about respecting the people whose examples they are, obtaining them fairly, and using them in ways that do not expose or exploit them. For local tools, inclusive test sets will usually be built from your own files, plus, with permission, files from a few volunteers, rather than from large scraped collections, which makes inclusivity both more personal and more manageable, if done with care.

Start by asking who your tool is for, and whose inputs are currently missing from your test set, as in Section 4. Then seek test cases that fill those gaps, through voluntary contributions, with clear explanations of how the cases will be used, stored, and shared, and with an easy way for contributors to withdraw their cases. Do not use other people's private notes, messages, or files without explicit permission, even if they are publicly visible somewhere, because public visibility is not the same as consent to be used as test data. Consent, in test set building as in data collection generally, is a fairness and responsibility issue, not just a legal one, and it is covered further in Sections 32 and 33.

When you receive diverse cases, handle them as personal data, as in Chapter 24: remove unneeded private details, store files securely, and do not share raw cases publicly without permission, even if anonymised, because anonymisation can fail for small examples. If a test case contains sensitive information, either remove it, replace it with a synthetic example that preserves the relevant pattern, such as a particular spelling variation or format, without the sensitive content, or exclude it and note the gap it leaves. Synthetic examples, built to mimic a pattern without copying a real person's data, are a valuable tool for inclusive testing, because they let you test diversity without exposing anyone.

Also be careful not to treat diverse test cases as tokens. A single example from a group does not represent that group, and a test set that includes one token example per group, while claiming to be inclusive, misleads users and builders alike. Inclusivity requires variety within groups, as in Section 4, and it requires reporting that variety honestly, with sample sizes, rather than implying that a group's performance is known from one or two cases. If a group has too few cases to judge, say so, and prioritise collecting more, or synthetic, cases for that group, rather than presenting a fragile score as a fairness verdict.

Finally, involve contributors by sharing results for their kinds of inputs, and asking whether performance feels fair and useful to them. Contributors who see their cases used respectfully, and who see results reported honestly, are more likely to contribute again, and to help improve the tool, than contributors whose cases disappear into a dataset they never hear about again. Inclusive test sets, built with consent, privacy, variety, and feedback, are not just fairer; they are also better test sets, because they reflect real diversity more accurately, and because they build the trust that makes future collaboration possible.

Section 16: Fixing Data Gaps Honestly

Fixing a data gap means adding examples, or improving existing ones, so that a tool serves a previously missing or underrepresented kind of input better. Honest gap-fixing starts by naming the gap precisely: which kind of input is missing, how many examples exist now, and how the tool currently performs on that kind. A vague statement like "we need more diverse data" does not guide action, while a precise statement like "we have only five examples of short, informal notes, and the summariser covers key facts in only two of them" tells you exactly what to collect, and how you will know when the gap is closing. Precision in naming gaps is the first step in fixing them honestly.

Next, collect new examples fairly, with permission, and with variety within the gap, not just quantity. Ten examples from one person do not fill a gap for a whole kind of input, and may create a narrower bias. Aim for different writers, topics, and formats, even if the total is small, because variety teaches general patterns. Synthetic examples, mimicking the pattern without copying private data, can help if labelled as synthetic.

Also fix gaps by improving existing data, not only by adding new data. Sometimes the gap is quality, not quantity: mislabelled, outdated, or unrepresentative examples. Auditing and correcting those can improve performance without adding a case. Data cleaning, done with fairness in mind, as in Section 2, is therefore part of gap-fixing, not a separate chore, and it should be tracked, with before-and-after breakdowns, so you can see whether cleaning helped the gap or accidentally widened it by removing too much.

Test gap fixes on fresh examples of the missing kind, not on the examples you just added, because testing on added examples will flatter the result, as Chapter 21 warned about training-test overlap. Keep a small, held-out set for the missing kind, separate from the examples used to improve the tool, and report performance on that held-out set before and after the fix. If performance improves on held-out examples, the fix generalises, which is real progress. If it improves only on added examples, the tool has memorised the new cases, not learned the kind, and more varied examples, or a different approach, are needed.

Finally, report gap fixes honestly, including gaps that remain. State what was added, from whom and with what permission, what improved and by how much on held-out examples, and what still performs poorly. A report that says "short informal notes improved from forty to seventy percent, but still lag formal notes at ninety percent, and we need more varied examples" is honest and useful, because it shows progress without claiming the gap is closed. Gap-fixing is rarely finished in one round; it is a cycle of naming, collecting, testing, and reporting, repeated as new gaps are discovered from real use. Honest reporting keeps that cycle grounded in evidence, rather than in the comforting story that a gap, once noticed, has been fully fixed.

Section 17: When Fixing Data Is Not Enough

Sometimes adding more data does not fix bias, because the problem lies not in quantity, but in the task, the labels, the features, or the world the data reflects. For example, if a category definition itself is unfair, such as defining "professional" writing in a way that excludes a legitimate dialect, then adding more examples of that dialect, labelled under the unfair definition, will teach the tool to reproduce the unfair definition more accurately, not to overcome it. In such cases, the fix is to revise the definition, with input from affected users, not to add data, because data cannot correct a flawed standard; it can only entrench it more thoroughly.

Another case is measurement bias, as in Section 2: if the measured thing is a flawed proxy, more data measured the same way will not help. A tool using reply speed as a proxy for importance will undervalue groups receiving slower replies, however many examples it sees. The fix is to change what is measured, to a closer proxy, or to add human judgement for borderline cases, rather than to collect more flawed measurements. Recognising measurement bias requires stepping back from data quantity and asking, "are we measuring the right thing, for all groups, in the first place?"

A third case is when the world itself produces unequal data for reasons outside the tool's control. If one group's notes are systematically shorter, because they write on phones with limited time, a summariser that favours long notes may still serve them worse, even with balanced training data, because its features or scoring still favour length. The fix may be to change features or scoring, to value completeness over length, or to design a different workflow for short notes, such as asking a clarifying question, rather than simply adding more short notes and hoping the tool learns to value them. Data alone cannot overcome a design that systematically disadvantages a kind of input; design must change too.

A fourth case is feedback loops, as in Section 12: adding diverse examples to a system still learning from its own biased outputs may be overwhelmed by the loop amplifying the original bias faster than new data corrects it. Breaking the loop, by separating outputs from inputs, or by changing ranking rules, is necessary before data fixes can take hold. Builders who only add data, without addressing loops, definitions, measurements, or design, will be frustrated when bias persists, and may wrongly conclude that fairness is impossible, when in fact they have only tried one kind of fix.

Finally, when data fixes are not enough, consider whether the tool should be redesigned, restricted, or not built for that task at all, as in Section 31. A tool that cannot be made fair for a particular use, despite data, design, and review fixes, should not be deployed for that use, however useful it is for other uses. Restricting scope honestly, to the uses where fairness can be demonstrated, is a responsible outcome, not a failure, because it protects users from a tool that would harm them, while preserving its benefits where it works fairly. Knowing when data is not enough, and acting on that knowledge, is a hallmark of mature, responsible building.

Section 18: Fairness and Privacy Tension

Fairness and privacy can pull in opposite directions, and builders must navigate that tension carefully. To measure fairness by group, you often need to know group membership, or at least to break down performance by group, which requires collecting or inferring group information. But collecting group information, especially sensitive characteristics, creates privacy risks: it can expose people, enable misuse, or violate trust, especially if the information is stored insecurely, shared broadly, or used for purposes beyond fairness measurement. A builder who collects sensitive data carelessly, in the name of fairness, may cause the very harm fairness work is meant to prevent, which is why the tension must be handled with explicit care, not ignored.

One way to navigate the tension is to measure fairness without personal labels, by breaking down by input characteristics instead, as in Section 6. For a local tool, breakdowns by length, topic, or style often reveal the same gaps as group breakdowns, without labelling people. Input-based breakdowns are therefore the preferred first step for local builders: they are less intrusive, they do not require sensitive data, and they point directly to fixable causes, such as a search tool's weakness with short queries, rather than to a group label that may be hard to act on without further investigation.

When group breakdowns are genuinely needed, collect the minimum information necessary, with explicit consent, for a specific fairness purpose, and with a clear retention and deletion plan, as in Chapter 24. Explain to contributors why the information is needed, how it will be stored, who will see it, and when it will be deleted, and give them an easy way to withdraw. Store group information separately from other data where possible, limit access to those who need it for fairness measurement, and report only aggregated results, never individual group membership, in any shared report. Those practices do not eliminate privacy risk, but they reduce it to a level that is proportionate to the fairness benefit, which is the standard responsible builders should aim for.

Never infer group membership from proxies, such as names or writing style, without consent. Proxy inference is often wrong, can mislabel people harmfully, and produces misleading fairness measurements, because the inferred groups are not the real groups. If you cannot obtain group information fairly and accurately, it is better to report fairness by input type, with that limit stated, than to present proxy-inferred group fairness as if it were reliable. Honest limits are preferable to precise-looking but false fairness claims.

Finally, review the fairness-privacy balance regularly, as data, tools, and user expectations change. A measurement that was justified when a tool was new may no longer be justified when input-based breakdowns reveal the same gaps, or when the tool's user base changes. Conversely, a privacy protection that once seemed sufficient may become inadequate as data is combined in new ways. Regular review, with input from affected users where possible, keeps the balance honest and proportionate, rather than fixed by an early decision that no longer fits. Fairness and privacy are both responsibilities, and responsible builders hold both, even when they tension, by choosing the least intrusive measurement that still reveals real gaps, and by being transparent about the trade-off.

Section 19: Transparency About Limits

Transparency about limits means telling users, plainly, what your tool does well, what it does poorly, for whom it was tested, and what it was not tested on. It is a core part of responsible AI, because users cannot use a tool safely if they do not know its limits. A tool described as generally accurate, without limits, invites users to trust it in situations where it was never tested, including situations where it performs poorly or unfairly. A tool described with its limits, such as "tested on formal notes; performance on informal notes is lower, at seventy percent versus ninety percent," lets users decide when to trust it, when to check extra carefully, and when to use a different approach. Limits are not apologies; they are information that makes a tool usable.

Transparency should cover five limits. First, scope: what tasks and formats the tool was tested for, and what falls outside. Second, performance: scores by group, topic, and difficulty, including the weakest groups. Third, fairness: which groups were not tested, or tested with too few examples. Fourth, safety: what safety cases passed and what risks remain. Fifth, data: what data was used, how old it is, and what gaps it has. A one-page note covering those areas is far more useful than a long report users will not read.

Transparency also means honesty about uncertainty. If a score is based on a small sample, say so. If a kind of input was not tested, say "not tested" rather than implying it works. If a tool's performance changed after an update, update the transparency note, with the date, rather than leaving an outdated claim in place. Users trust builders who update their limits, because updating shows that limits are being monitored, not merely declared once and forgotten. A transparency note is a living document, like an evaluation log, and it should be revised whenever the tool or its tests change.

Be careful that transparency does not excuse unfairness. Stating "this tool performs poorly for group X" does not make deploying it for group X acceptable without further action. Transparency must be paired with action: fix the gap, restrict use, add human review, or warn in a way that actually prevents harm. Disclosure without action can become a shield for builders, who claim users were warned, while users, who had no real alternative, still bear the harm. Responsible transparency therefore includes what you are doing about each limit, and by when, not just a list of limits themselves.

Finally, make transparency accessible. Put limits where users will see them before they rely on the tool: in its description, in its output footer for important cases, or in a short guide that accompanies it, rather than buried in a file users will never open. Use plain language, not technical jargon, and give concrete examples of limits, such as "works best on notes longer than three sentences; very short notes may be summarised poorly," rather than abstract statements like "performance may vary." Concrete, visible, actionable transparency helps users use tools safely, and it builds the trust that makes users willing to report problems, which in turn helps builders improve. Transparency, done well, is not a burden; it is a partnership with users.

Section 20: Responsible AI in Plain Words

Responsible AI, in plain words, means building tools that are honest, fair, safe, and accountable to the people they affect. Honest means telling the truth about what a tool does, how well it does it, and where it fails, as in Chapters 21 and 22. Fair means checking that a tool serves diverse users reasonably equally, and fixing gaps when it does not, as this chapter teaches. Safe means designing so that errors, especially serious ones, are caught, limited, and corrected, rather than allowed to cause harm. Accountable means that someone, namely the builder, takes responsibility for the tool's effects, answers for its failures, and acts to fix them, rather than blaming the tool, the data, or the users.

Responsible AI also means thinking beyond accuracy. A tool can be accurate on average yet irresponsible, if it exposes private information, cannot be understood or corrected, entrenches unfairness, or is deployed for an untested purpose. Responsible builders therefore ask broader questions, before and after building: who will use this tool, who might be harmed by it, even unintentionally, what will happen when it fails, and who will notice and fix the failure? Those questions, asked early and revisited regularly, prevent many irresponsible outcomes, because they surface risks while they are still cheap to address, rather than after users have been harmed.

For local builders, responsible AI is practical. It means using your own files, with permission, not other people's private data. It means testing on diverse examples, reporting breakdowns, and fixing the biggest fairness gap first. It means showing sources, admitting uncertainty, and refusing to guess on high-stakes questions. It means keeping evaluation records, correcting errors transparently, and updating transparency notes when tools change. None of those practices requires a large team, a big budget, or advanced theory; they require only care, discipline, and a willingness to put users' interests ahead of impressive scores or quick releases. That practical version of responsibility is within reach of every builder in this book.

Responsible AI also means knowing your limits. If you do not understand a tool's fairness implications for a use, seek input, or restrict the tool to uses you can evaluate honestly. If you cannot test it for a group it will serve, do not claim it serves that group fairly. If you discover a harm you cannot fix, pause or restrict the tool, and tell users, rather than hoping the harm will go unnoticed. Humility about limits is not weakness; it is a core responsible practice, because overconfident builders cause more harm than cautious ones, by deploying tools beyond what they can safely support.

Finally, responsible AI is a habit that grows with practice. Each responsibly built tool teaches more about fairness and safety, and each honest evaluation builds trust, so users help you improve by reporting failures. That virtuous cycle, responsibility building trust, trust enabling feedback, feedback improving responsibility, is the long-term reward for the work this chapter describes. Responsible AI, in plain words, is simply building tools you would be proud to have used on you, by people you trust, with limits stated, errors corrected, and fairness checked, and that standard, applied consistently, is enough to guide every builder, from beginner to expert.

Section 21: Who Is Affected? Stakeholder Thinking

Stakeholder thinking means asking, before you build or release a tool, who will be affected by it, in what ways, and whether those effects are fair. Stakeholders are not just direct users; they include people whose data is used, people whose work is sorted or judged by the tool, people who receive its outputs second-hand, and people who cannot use it at all because of its design. A summariser's stakeholders include its readers, the authors of summarised notes, and anyone who acts on a summary. A classifier's stakeholders include those it labels correctly, those it mislabels, and those who must appeal a wrong label. Listing stakeholders, even briefly, reveals harms that a focus on users alone would miss.

Start stakeholder thinking with a simple list: who uses the tool, who provides its data, who is judged or sorted by it, who receives its outputs, and who is excluded by its design? For each group, ask what they gain if the tool works well, and what they lose if it fails, especially if it fails unfairly. A group that gains little but loses a lot from failures deserves extra protection, such as human review or a lower-stakes use, even if they are not the tool's primary users. That asymmetry, low benefit plus high harm, is a warning sign that a tool's design or deployment needs rethinking, because it concentrates risk on people who did not choose it and do not benefit from it.

Also consider stakeholders over time. A tool that works fairly today may affect future users differently, as its outputs become inputs, as in Section 12, or as its user base grows to include groups it was never tested for. A tool built for one community, then shared more widely, gains new stakeholders whose needs and vulnerabilities were never considered in its original design. Stakeholder thinking therefore needs revisiting whenever a tool's scope, audience, or data changes, not just at the start. A short stakeholder review, done at each major version, catches new affected groups before they are harmed, rather than after they complain.

Finally, involve stakeholders where you can, especially those most affected by failures. Asking affected people how a tool's errors feel, which errors matter most, and what safeguards they would trust, gives builders information no metric can provide, because it captures lived experience of harm, not just counts of errors. Involvement need not be formal: a short conversation, a test with a few affected users, or feedback on a draft transparency note, as in Section 19, can reveal priorities that builders would otherwise miss. Stakeholder thinking, practised honestly, turns fairness from an abstract principle into a concrete habit: name who is affected, weigh their gains and losses, revisit as scope changes, and listen to those who bear the most risk.

Section 22: Harms: Allocation, Representation, Quality

Fairness harms can be sorted into three broad kinds, which helps builders name and address them precisely, rather than talking vaguely about "bias." Allocation harms happen when a tool distributes something valuable, or withholds it, unfairly: jobs, attention, information, priority, or access. A search tool that buries one group's relevant notes allocates visibility unfairly. A classifier that blocks one group's innocent messages allocates voice unfairly. Allocation harms are concrete and measurable, because they concern who gets what, and they are often the first harms users notice, because they directly affect outcomes they care about.

Representation harms happen when a tool presents a group in a misleading, stereotyped, or demeaning way, or omits them altogether, as in Section 13. A tool whose examples never show a group in a skilled role, or whose generated text associates a group with negative traits, causes representation harm, even if it never denies anyone a concrete resource. Representation harms matter because they shape how people see themselves and each other, and because they can lead to allocation harms later, when decision-makers internalise stereotyped presentations as facts. Builders sometimes dismiss representation harms as "just words," but words in tools that many people use shape shared understanding, and shared understanding shapes decisions, so representation is a real, consequential harm, not a cosmetic one.

Quality harms happen when a tool simply works worse for one group, giving them less accurate, less clear, or less useful outputs, even if no resource is explicitly denied and no stereotype is explicitly stated. A summariser that covers key facts for one group at ninety percent and for another at sixty percent causes a quality harm, because the second group receives a worse service for the same task. Quality harms are often hidden by overall averages, as in Section 1, and they are sometimes excused as "the tool is still useful," but usefulness is relative: a tool that is much less useful for one group is unfair, even if it is somewhat useful for everyone, because it gives unequal value for equal need.

These three harms often occur together, and fixing one may not fix the others. Improving allocation, by ranking fairly, does not automatically fix representation, if examples remain stereotyped. Improving representation, by diversifying examples, does not automatically fix quality, if performance gaps remain. Builders should therefore check for all three, separately, in evaluation: allocation by who gets what, representation by what examples and outputs suggest, and quality by breakdown scores. That three-part check gives a fuller fairness picture than any single measure, and it guides fixes precisely: allocation fixes target ranking and thresholds, representation fixes target examples and wording, and quality fixes target data, features, and design. Keeping a short written note of each harm type you checked also makes later reviews faster and far more honest.

Section 23: Accountability: Who Answers

Accountability means that when a tool causes harm, or fails, someone identifiable takes responsibility for understanding what happened, fixing it, and answering to those affected. Without accountability, harms become nobody's problem: the builder blames the data, the data provider blames the tool, the user blames themselves for trusting it, and nothing changes. With accountability, a failure triggers a clear process: acknowledge it, investigate its cause, fix the cause, correct affected outputs where possible, and report what was learned, as in Chapter 22's correction habits. Accountability is therefore not about blame for its own sake; it is about ensuring failures lead to learning and repair, rather than to shrugs and repetition.

For local builders, accountability starts with ownership. Put your name on your tool and transparency note, with a way for users to report problems, such as an email address or feedback form. That contact point may feel like an invitation to complaints, but it is actually an invitation to improvement, because user reports reveal failures that testing missed, especially fairness failures affecting groups you did not test. A tool with no contact point, and no stated owner, tells users that failures will not be heard, which discourages reporting and allows harms to persist unnoticed, which is the opposite of accountability.

Accountability also requires records. Keep evaluation logs, fairness breakdowns, and correction records, so that when a failure occurs, you can trace what was tested and what changed. Records protect both users and builders: users can see that a failure was investigated seriously, and builders can show that they acted on what they knew, rather than ignoring warnings. A builder without records cannot demonstrate accountability, even with good intentions, because there is no evidence of what was checked, what was found, and what was done. Records turn good intentions into demonstrable responsibility.

Be careful not to outsource accountability to the tool itself, or to users. Saying "the AI decided" does not answer for a decision, because the tool is not a moral agent; its builder, who designed, tested, and deployed it, is. Similarly, saying "users should have checked" does not fully answer for a harm, if the tool was designed in a way that made checking hard, or if its limits were not stated clearly. Users share some responsibility for checking, as Chapter 22 teaches, but builders retain responsibility for designing checkable tools, stating limits, and fixing known failures. Accountability is shared in practice, but it is not diluted by sharing: each party answers for their part, and builders answer for theirs, which is the largest part, because they control design and deployment.

Finally, accountability includes acting on what you learn, even when action is costly. If a fairness evaluation reveals a serious gap, the accountable response is to fix it, restrict the tool, or pause it, as in Section 31, not to hide the evaluation or to hope users will not notice. Costly accountability builds long-term trust, because users learn that reported problems lead to real changes, which encourages more reporting and more improvement. Cheap accountability, which acknowledges problems without fixing them, destroys trust faster than silence, because it shows users that their reports were heard and then ignored. True accountability is measured by changes made, not by statements issued.

Section 24: Human Oversight That Works

Human oversight means a person reviews, checks, or can override a tool's outputs, especially for important or high-stakes decisions. Oversight is often proposed as a fairness and safety safeguard, but it only works if it is designed well. Oversight that is rushed, uninformed, or merely rubber-stamping outputs, where a human approves without really checking, provides no protection, while creating a false sense of safety, because everyone assumes someone else checked. Effective oversight requires time, information, authority to override, and a clear understanding of what to check, all of which must be designed into the workflow, not merely asserted as a principle.

Start by deciding which outputs need human review, and which can run automatically. Not every output needs review; reviewing everything would be slow and would train reviewers to approve quickly without checking, which defeats the purpose. High-stakes outputs, borderline cases, cases affecting disadvantaged groups, and cases where the tool is uncertain, as in Chapter 22, deserve review first. Low-stakes, high-confidence, well-tested outputs may run automatically, with periodic sampling for quality checks, so that automation does not drift unchecked. That triage, review where risk is highest, sampling where risk is lower, makes oversight feasible for local builders, who cannot review everything, while still protecting users where it matters most.

Next, give reviewers information to check effectively. A reviewer seeing only a conclusion, without source or uncertainty, cannot check meaningfully, and will tend to approve it, especially if it looks fluent. A reviewer who sees the source passage, the key facts, the confidence signals, and the fairness-relevant breakdown, can check claim by claim, as in Chapter 22, and can spot errors that a bare conclusion would hide. Designing outputs for review, with sources and uncertainty visible, as in Section 38 of Chapter 22, is therefore part of oversight design, not a separate feature. A tool that cannot be reviewed cannot be overseen, however many humans are nominally in the loop.

Also give reviewers real authority and time. A reviewer approving hundreds of outputs per hour, or penalised for disagreeing, will not override it, even when spotting an error. A reviewer with a manageable workload, with explicit permission to override, and with feedback on their overrides, so they learn whether they were right, will review genuinely. For local tools, oversight may mean you, the builder, reviewing your own tool's outputs before sharing them, with a checklist and a pause, rather than sharing automatically. That self-review, done seriously, is real oversight, while automatic sharing with a nominal "human in the loop" who never actually checks is not.

Finally, monitor oversight itself. Track how often reviewers override the tool, on which kinds of cases, and whether overrides improve outcomes, by checking overridden cases against gold answers where possible. If overrides are rare, ask whether reviewers are really checking, or merely rubber-stamping, and redesign the workflow to encourage genuine review, for example by sampling, by rotating reviewers, or by requiring a brief reason for approval on high-stakes cases. Oversight that is monitored and improved, like any other part of a tool, becomes a genuine safeguard. Oversight that is assumed to work, without measurement, is a slogan, and slogans do not protect users from unfair or unsafe outputs.

Section 25: Avoiding Bias in Prompts

Prompts, or instructions given to a tool, can introduce bias, even when the tool itself is fair, because instructions shape what the tool does, what examples it follows, and what it treats as normal. For local toy tools, prompts are usually short instructions you write, such as "summarise this note" or "sort these messages," plus any examples you include to illustrate the task. If those instructions or examples contain stereotyped language, narrow examples, or loaded assumptions, the tool will follow them, reproducing bias that was never in its data or design, but was in its prompt. Prompt bias is therefore a builder responsibility, and it can be checked and fixed with the same habits as other bias sources.

One pattern is example bias. If examples all feature one kind of person, topic, or style, the tool may learn that kind is normal, handle other kinds less well, or reproduce the examples' associations. To avoid example bias, vary your examples deliberately, across topics, styles, and roles, as in Section 13, and check the set as a whole for associations, not just each example alone. A prompt with diverse, ordinary examples teaches a tool to handle diversity, while a prompt with narrow examples teaches it to expect narrowness, which then looks like a tool limitation, when it was really a prompt limitation.

Another pattern is wording bias. Instructions that use loaded, absolute, or stereotyped language, such as describing one group's writing as "proper" and another's as "messy," can bias the tool's judgements, by framing one style as correct and another as defective. Instructions should use neutral, task-based language: describe what to do, such as "keep key facts, dates, and decisions," rather than judging styles, such as "make it sound professional," which may be interpreted as favouring a particular style. Neutral wording does not mean vague wording; it means wording that defines the task by its purpose and its facts, not by a particular group's norms presented as universal standards.

A third pattern is assumption bias, similar to false-premise questions in Chapter 22. An instruction that assumes a fact, such as "list the five reasons," when only three exist, or "summarise the manager's decision," when no manager is mentioned, will push the tool to invent or to misattribute, in order to satisfy the assumption. To avoid assumption bias, phrase instructions openly, such as "list the reasons documented in the note, however many there are," and test prompts with cases that violate their assumptions, to see whether the tool questions the assumption or invents to satisfy it. Prompts that survive assumption-violation tests are safer prompts, because they guide tools toward honesty rather than compliance.

Finally, test prompts for bias: run the same tool with different prompts, on diverse cases, and compare breakdowns by group, topic, and style. If changing a prompt's examples or wording changes fairness breakdowns significantly, the prompt, not just the tool, needs fixing, and the fairer prompt should be kept, with its reasoning recorded. Prompt testing should be documented, so future changes can be judged against past fairness results, not intuition alone. A prompt is part of a tool, not a separate afterthought, and responsible builders treat it as such, testing and revising it with the same care as code and data.

Section 26: Local Toy: Auditing a Classifier

Auditing a classifier means checking its performance by group, by category, and by error type, using a small script you can run on your own data. Building such an audit toy, with only the standard library, teaches you exactly what fairness measurement involves, and it gives you a reusable tool for every classifier you build in this book. The toy does not need to be sophisticated; it needs to be honest, readable, and run on test cases you control, with gold answers you wrote, so that its results mean something for your tool and your users, not just for a benchmark you did not design.

Start the audit with test cases grouped by input type. For a message classifier, group cases by length, such as short versus long, by style, such as formal versus informal, and by topic, if topics matter for your tool. For each group, store the gold label and the tool's predicted label, in a simple list of pairs, and compute accuracy, false positives, and false negatives per group, using the formulas from Chapter 21. Print the results as a small table, with group name, sample size, accuracy, and error counts, so that gaps are visible at a glance. A table that shows one group at ninety percent and another at sixty percent, with sample sizes beside each, is a fairness finding, not just a number, because it names who is served worse and by how much.

Next, add error breakdowns by category: which categories are confused with which, per group. That reveals whether a group is misclassified in a specific direction, such as urgent labelled routine, which matters because different misclassifications cause different harms. A group that suffers mostly false positives needs a different fix, such as a higher threshold or human review, from a group that suffers mostly false negatives, which needs better recall for that group's expressions of urgency. Error direction, not just error count, guides fair fixes.

Then add a simple fairness summary to the audit output: the largest accuracy gap between groups, the largest false-positive gap, and the group with the worst performance, each with its sample size. That summary helps you prioritise, as in Chapter 21, by naming the biggest gap first, rather than leaving you to hunt through a table. But do not let the summary replace the table: the summary highlights, the table explains, and both are needed for honest reporting. If a group has too few examples to judge, the audit should say so, rather than presenting a fragile gap as a firm finding, because small-sample gaps, as in Chapter 21, are uncertain and should be treated as prompts for more testing, not as verdicts.

Finally, run the audit after every change, and keep outputs in your evaluation log, so you can track whether gaps shrink, grow, or shift. A one-off audit will miss drift and new gaps; a regular audit catches them early. Sharing the audit script and its results, if you share the classifier, lets others verify your fairness claims and add test cases for groups you missed, which is how a local audit toy becomes a community fairness tool. Auditing, in this simple, repeatable form, is the practical core of fairness work for local builders.

def audit(pairs):
    correct = sum(1 for p, g in pairs if p == g)
    return correct, len(pairs)

print(audit([("spam", "spam"), ("mail", "spam"), ("mail", "mail")]))

Section 27: Checking Word Lists for Bias

Word lists are used in many local tools: lists of positive words, negative words, spam keywords, urgent words, or stop words to ignore. Because word lists are simple, builders often assume they are neutral, but word lists can encode bias in subtle ways, by including words associated with one group, by excluding words used by another group, or by assigning a sentiment or category to a word that depends on context. Checking word lists for bias is therefore a practical, local fairness task, one that requires no advanced tools, only careful reading and testing, and it can prevent a whole class of classification and ranking errors that would otherwise look like mysterious tool failures.

Start by reading your word list as a whole, not word by word in isolation. Ask what kinds of words it contains, what kinds it omits, and what associations the set, taken together, suggests. A spam keyword list that includes slang used by one group, but not equivalent slang used by another, will flag the first group's messages more often, for no task-relevant reason. A positive word list that includes formal praise words, but not informal praise words, will rate formal messages as more positive, even when informal messages express the same sentiment. Reading a list as a set reveals these patterns, because a single word may look innocent, while the set's imbalance is obvious once you step back and look at what is included and what is missing.

Next, test your list on diverse examples. Run a scorer using the list on cases from different styles and topics, and compare breakdowns. If one style scores differently, check whether list words, rather than content, cause it, and whether those words are truly task-relevant. A word that appears mostly in one style's examples, and that drives many classifications, is a candidate for bias, especially if its task relevance is weak or context-dependent. Removing or contextualising such words, for example by requiring a second signal before flagging, can improve fairness without losing much overall accuracy.

Also check word lists for context dependence. Many words change meaning with context: a word that is negative in one context may be neutral or positive in another, and a list that treats it as always negative will misclassify messages where it appears in its other sense. Context dependence often varies by group, because different groups use the same word in different senses, or in different phrases, so a context-blind list can systematically misclassify one group's usage. Where context matters, prefer rules that consider surrounding words, or human review for borderline cases, over a single-word list applied blindly. That preference, for context over keywords, is a fairness improvement as well as an accuracy improvement, because it respects how language actually works.

Finally, document your lists, with purpose, sources, and limits, and review them regularly. A list built last year may contain words that changed meaning, introducing new biases over time, as in Section 35. A documented, reviewed word list, tested on diverse examples, is a fairer tool than an undocumented list inherited without question, however long it has been used. Word lists are small, but they are powerful, because they shape many decisions, and checking them carefully is a high-value, low-effort fairness habit for every local builder.

Section 28: Fairness Across Languages and Dialects

Fairness across languages and dialects is a major challenge for language tools, because tools usually perform best in the language or dialect that dominates their data, and worse in others, even when the task is the same. A summariser built on standard English notes may struggle with notes in another language, or in a dialect with different grammar, vocabulary, or spelling, not because those notes are worse, but because the tool was never built or tested for them. Claiming such a tool works generally, without stating its language limits, is unfair to users who write in other languages or dialects, because it invites them to rely on a tool that was never designed for their writing.

The first step is to state language scope honestly. List the languages and dialects your tool was built and tested for, with performance breakdowns for each, where you have data, and state plainly which languages were not tested. For a local tool built on your own notes, the scope will usually be narrow: perhaps one language, in a few styles, which is perfectly respectable, provided it is stated. A narrow, honestly stated scope helps users decide whether the tool fits their needs, while a broad, unstated scope misleads users whose language was never considered, and who will discover the tool's weakness only after relying on it and being disappointed or harmed by its errors.

Next, test across languages and dialects where you can, even with small samples. Use cases from different languages or dialects, with permission, to measure breakdowns, and report them with sample sizes. Small, honestly labelled results still reveal whether a tool fails completely or works partially on a new variety. Complete failure on an untested language is a scope limit to state; partial success is a starting point for improvement, with more diverse data and testing.

Be careful not to treat dialects as errors. A dialect is a legitimate variety of a language, with its own grammar and vocabulary, not a collection of mistakes in the standard variety. Tools that "correct" dialect features, penalise them in scoring, or treat them as typos, will systematically disadvantage dialect speakers, by forcing them to write in a variety that is not their own, or by misjudging their content. Fair tools should handle dialect variation as variation, not as defect, by using robust matching, such as synonym handling and spelling tolerance, and by avoiding features that penalise dialect-specific words or structures. That design choice, to treat variation as normal, is a fairness choice, as in Section 3, and it should be made deliberately.

Finally, involve speakers of those varieties in testing and gold answers, where possible, because only speakers can judge correctness for their variety. A gold answer written by a non-speaker may misrepresent meaning, and a tool tested on it will learn that misrepresentation as truth. Speaker involvement, with consent and credit, is therefore not just inclusive; it is necessary for accuracy, because language judgement requires language knowledge. Fairness across languages begins with respecting languages as they are actually used, by the people who use them.

Section 29: Age, Disability and Access

Fairness also concerns age, disability, and access, because tools designed for one kind of user, such as a young, technically skilled user with a fast computer and good vision, may exclude or disadvantage others, through no fault of those others. An interface that requires precise mouse control, small text, or complex steps will be harder for users with motor, visual, or cognitive differences, however fair its predictions are. A tool that requires a powerful computer, a stable internet connection, or a paid service will exclude users without those resources, however good its outputs are for those who can run it. Access fairness is therefore part of responsible AI, not a separate accessibility topic to be added later, because a tool that some users cannot use is not a fair tool for them, whatever its scores say.

Start access fairness by considering the simplest, most inclusive design that serves your task. For local tools in this book, that often means plain text interfaces, clear instructions, large enough text, keyboard-friendly operation, and no reliance on colour alone to convey meaning, because colour alone excludes users who cannot distinguish certain colours. It also means keeping tools lightweight, using only the standard library, so they run on modest computers without paid services, as this book's design already does. Those choices, made early, cost little, but they widen the group who can use a tool successfully, which is a fairness gain that no accuracy improvement can replace, because accuracy is irrelevant to someone who cannot run the tool at all.

Also consider cognitive access. Long, jargon-heavy outputs are harder for beginners, rushed users, and users with cognitive differences, even if accurate. Clear outputs, with key points first and uncertainty marked, improve access for everyone, especially those needing clarity most. Testing outputs with users who are new to the topic, or who are using the tool under realistic time pressure, reveals cognitive access problems that expert testing misses, because experts fill gaps from background knowledge that beginners do not have.

Age fairness deserves specific attention, because tools often assume a user's age implicitly, through examples, topics, or required knowledge, without stating it. A learning tool that assumes adult background knowledge will confuse younger learners, while a tool that assumes child-like simplicity will patronise adult learners. Stating the intended age or experience level, testing with users from that group, and avoiding age-stereotyped examples, as in Section 13, are practical steps toward age fairness. Similarly, avoid requiring personal information, such as age or disability status, to use a tool, unless it is genuinely needed, because requiring such information creates privacy risks, as in Section 18, and can deter users who do not wish to disclose it.

Finally, invite feedback from users with different access needs, and fix the most exclusionary barriers first. Access fixes, such as larger text or clearer steps, often help all users, because clarity is a universal benefit. A tool that is accessible, lightweight, and clear is not just fairer; it is also more robust and more widely usable, which is why access fairness should be treated as core design work, from the first version, rather than as a retrofit after release, when exclusionary choices are harder to undo.

Section 30: Bias in Summaries

Summaries can be biased even when factually accurate, because choosing which facts to keep can systematically favour one perspective or group. A summary that keeps facts important to one group, while dropping facts important to another, presents a skewed picture of the original, even if every kept fact is true. A summary that uses loaded language for one group's actions, while using neutral language for another's, similarly skews perception, without inventing any fact. Summary bias is therefore about selection and framing, not just about invention, which makes it harder to detect than hallucination, because the summary may pass a fact check while still being unfair.

Selection bias often reflects the builder's sense of importance, which may not match all readers'. If importance is judged by frequency, repeated facts are kept, while a rare but crucial warning, appearing once, may be dropped, harming readers needing it. If importance is judged by the builder's interests, facts relevant to the builder's group may be kept, while facts relevant to other groups are dropped. To reduce selection bias, define importance for the intended reader, as in Chapter 22's reader-centred summarising, and check summaries against a fact list that includes facts important to diverse readers, not just to the builder, so that drops are visible and can be questioned.

Framing bias in summaries involves wording choices that, while not factually wrong, present facts in a way that favours one interpretation. For example, describing one group's action as "demanding" and another's identical action as "requesting" frames the first group more negatively, even though the action is the same. Summarisers, human or machine, often introduce framing bias unconsciously, by copying loaded words from the original, or by choosing synonyms that carry different connotations. Checking summaries for framing, by comparing their wording with the original's wording, and by asking whether the same wording would be used for a different group in the same situation, reveals framing bias that fact checks alone would miss, because framing is about connotation, not just denotation.

Another summary bias pattern is omission of context that changes meaning. A summary that drops a qualifier, a condition, or a background fact, as in Chapter 22, can make a conditional statement look absolute, a suggestion look like a decision, or a disputed claim look like consensus. Omission bias harms readers who rely on the summary alone, because they cannot see what was dropped, and they may act on the skewed version as if it were complete. To reduce omission bias, preserve qualifiers, conditions, and crucial context, even at the cost of a slightly longer summary, and label summaries with their source and scope, so readers know what was summarised and what was left out.

Finally, test summaries for bias as for accuracy: use diverse fact lists to judge whether important facts for different readers are kept, framing is neutral, and context preserved. A summary passing fact checks but failing bias checks is not yet good, because its job is to represent fairly, not merely contain true statements selected unfairly. Fair summarising, like honest summarising, requires attention to selection, framing, and context, alongside accuracy, and builders who check all four will produce summaries readers can trust to represent, not just to inform.

Section 31: When a Tool Should Not Be Built

Not every tool should be built, even if it is technically possible and even if someone asks for it. A tool should not be built, or should not be deployed for a particular use, when it cannot be made fair, safe, or accurate enough for that use, despite reasonable effort, or when its foreseeable harms outweigh its benefits for the people it affects. That judgement, to not build or not deploy, is a responsible decision, not a failure of ambition, because building a harmful tool is worse than building nothing, and a builder who recognises that limit protects users in a way that no amount of clever engineering after deployment can undo.

Several warning signs suggest a tool should not be built for a given use. First, the task requires judging people on contested, subjective grounds, such as character, worth, or trustworthiness, where no fair, agreed definition exists, and where automation would hide disagreement behind a confident label. Second, the tool would need sensitive personal data that cannot be obtained fairly, stored safely, or used without exposing people to risk. Third, the tool's errors would fall hardest on people who already face disadvantage, and who have no realistic way to appeal or correct those errors. Fourth, the tool cannot be tested for the groups it will affect, because those groups cannot be reached, or because testing would itself expose them to risk. Any one of these signs deserves serious pause; several together are strong evidence against building for that use.

When you encounter such signs, consider alternatives before abandoning the idea entirely. A tool that cannot fairly automate a decision might still fairly support a human decision, by organising information, showing sources, and flagging uncertainty, while leaving the final judgement to a person who can weigh context the tool cannot. A tool that cannot serve all groups fairly might still serve a narrower, well-tested scope fairly, with its limits stated, as in Section 19. A tool that needs sensitive data might be redesigned to use only non-sensitive input characteristics, as in Section 18. Alternatives that narrow scope, add human judgement, or reduce data needs often preserve most of a tool's benefit while removing most of its harm, which is a better outcome than either building the harmful version or building nothing at all.

If no safe alternative exists, the responsible choice is to not build, or to not deploy, and to explain why, in plain language, to anyone who asked for the tool. That explanation should name the specific fairness, safety, or accuracy problem, the efforts made to address it, and what would need to change for the tool to become buildable in future, such as better data with consent, clearer definitions, or a workable appeal process. An honest "not yet" or "not for this use," with reasons, is more useful than a silent refusal, because it guides future work toward the conditions that would make the tool safe, and it models the judgement that responsible building requires: knowing when to stop is part of knowing how to build well.

Section 32: Responsible Data Collection

Responsible data collection means gathering examples for building and testing in ways that respect the people whose data they are, that are honest about purpose and use, and that minimise risk to those people. It starts with purpose: collect only data you genuinely need, for a specific, stated purpose, rather than collecting everything available in case it might be useful later. Data collected without a clear purpose tends to accumulate, to be used for purposes its providers never agreed to, and to create privacy and fairness risks that grow with its size. A small, purposeful dataset, collected for a known task, is therefore more responsible, and often more useful, than a large, purposeless one.

Next comes consent. For data from identifiable people, obtain explicit, informed consent, explaining what data will be collected, how it will be used, who will see it, how long it will be kept, and how a provider can withdraw it. Consent must be freely given, not assumed from public visibility, from a vague terms page, or from the fact that data was easy to copy. For local tools, consent will usually be simple: ask friends or colleagues directly, in plain language, before using their notes or messages as examples, and respect a refusal without pressure. That direct, respectful approach builds trust, and it produces better data, because providers who understand the purpose are more likely to provide relevant, accurate examples, and to flag cases that should not be used.

Also minimise data at collection. Do not collect personal details that are not needed for the task, such as names, contact details, or sensitive content, even if they appear in the original material. Where a needed pattern can be shown with a synthetic example, or with an anonymised version that preserves the pattern without the personal detail, prefer that version, as in Section 15. Minimisation reduces the harm if data is later leaked, shared, or misused, because there is simply less sensitive material to leak. It also simplifies fairness work, because a dataset with fewer personal details is less likely to encode personal biases in ways that are hard to detect and correct.

Finally, document collection honestly. Keep a short record, with each dataset, stating its source, its consent basis, its purpose, its size, its known gaps, and its retention plan, including when it will be deleted or reviewed. That record lets future builders, reviewers, and data providers understand what was collected and why, and it lets you audit your own practices over time, as in Section 35. Data collected responsibly, with purpose, consent, minimisation, and documentation, is a foundation for fair tools, because it respects the people behind the examples, and because it produces datasets whose limits and biases are known, rather than hidden behind a claim of "big data" that obscures how the data was actually obtained.

Section 33: Consent and Community Data

Community data means data that belongs to, or comes from, a community, such as a learning group, a workplace team, or a cultural group, rather than from isolated individuals. Collecting community data responsibly requires more than individual consent, because community data can reveal patterns about the community as a whole, can be used in ways that affect all its members, and can be shared beyond the community in ways that harm it, even if each individual contributor agreed to share their own piece. Builders working with community data must therefore consider community-level consent, benefit, and control, not just individual permission, especially when the community is small, vulnerable, or historically misrepresented.

Start by asking who speaks for the community, and how decisions about data are made within it. That may be a formal leader, a group discussion, or a shared norm, depending on the community, and builders should respect that process, rather than bypassing it by collecting from individuals one by one, in a way that fragments community control. Explain the proposed use, in plain language, to the community as a whole, or to its representatives, listen to concerns, and be prepared to change the plan, narrow the scope, or abandon collection if the community does not support it. That respect for collective decision-making is not just polite; it is necessary for accuracy too, because a community that does not trust a builder will not provide representative data, and a builder who bypasses community process will build on incomplete, distrustful data.

Also consider benefit and control. Who benefits from a tool built on community data, and who controls future uses? If a builder benefits while the community bears exposure risks, without sharing benefit or control, that arrangement is unfair, however individual consents were obtained. Fair arrangements share benefits, such as giving the community access to the tool, credit for its data, and a say in future uses, including a veto over uses the community finds harmful. Those arrangements should be agreed in advance, in writing where possible, in plain language, so that expectations are clear and enforceable, not left to goodwill alone.

Be especially careful with data from communities misrepresented in the past, because historical harm makes collection more sensitive, and stereotyped data about them can cause disproportionate harm. In such cases, extra care is warranted: smaller, more purposeful collection, stronger anonymisation, community review of outputs before sharing, and a clear, easy withdrawal process for the community as a whole, not just for individuals. That extra care is not special treatment; it is proportionate to the higher risk, and it is the responsible response to a history that makes trust harder to earn and easier to break.

Finally, when community data is used, credit the community appropriately, report results back to it, and keep the relationship open for future feedback and correction. A builder who reports back, shares findings, and acts on community feedback builds a lasting, trustworthy collaboration, while a builder who collects and disappears damages trust for future builders as well as for themselves. Community data work, done responsibly, is therefore a long-term relationship, not a one-time extraction, and its success should be judged by whether the community would willingly collaborate again, not merely by whether data was obtained.

Section 34: Openness Without Exposing People

Openness means sharing your tool, its evaluation, its data practices, and its limits, so that others can check, learn, and improve on your work. Openness is a responsible practice, because it enables scrutiny, which catches fairness and safety problems that private building would miss, and because it lets users judge tools for themselves, rather than trusting blindly. But openness must be balanced against privacy: sharing raw data, test cases, or outputs that contain personal information can expose people, even if the sharing was intended to promote transparency. Responsible openness therefore shares what helps scrutiny, while protecting what could harm individuals, which requires careful choices about what to share, in what form, and with whom.

A practical approach is to share in layers. Share fully, and publicly, the tool's code, its evaluation methods, its aggregate scores and breakdowns, its transparency note, and its checklists, because those materials help scrutiny without exposing anyone. Share test cases and examples in anonymised or synthetic form, where personal details have been removed or replaced, with a note explaining how anonymisation was done and what patterns were preserved, as in Section 15. Share raw, personal data only with explicit consent, only with people who need it for a specific, agreed purpose, and only under protections such as secure storage and a deletion plan, as in Chapter 24. Layered sharing gives scrutineers most of what they need, while giving data providers most of the protection they deserve.

Be careful about re-identification. Anonymised examples can sometimes be re-identified, especially when distinctive, when several come from one person, or when combined with other public information. Small test sets are especially vulnerable, because a distinctive style may be recognisable to people who know the author. To reduce re-identification risk, prefer synthetic examples for public sharing, aggregate results rather than individual cases where possible, and avoid sharing combinations of details, such as a rare topic plus a rare style plus a date, that together could identify someone even when each detail alone would not.

Also be transparent about what you are not sharing, and why. A note like "test cases are not shared publicly, because they contain personal notes shared for testing only, but aggregate scores and synthetic examples are available" is honest, and does not undermine scrutiny. Secrecy without explanation, by contrast, invites suspicion that fairness problems are being hidden, even when the real reason is privacy protection. Explaining the privacy reason, and offering aggregate or synthetic alternatives, preserves both openness and privacy, by showing that the limit is principled, not evasive.

Finally, review openness decisions regularly, as data, tools, and risks change. Material that was safe to share last year may become risky if new information becomes public that enables re-identification, or if a tool's user base grows to include people who could recognise anonymised cases. Conversely, material that was once sensitive may become shareable after further anonymisation or after consent is obtained for a broader use. Regular review, with input from data providers where possible, keeps openness and privacy in balance over time, rather than fixing them by an early decision that no longer fits. Responsible openness is not maximal sharing; it is thoughtful sharing, guided by scrutiny needs and privacy risks together.

Section 35: Fairness Over Time: Drift

Fairness, like accuracy, can drift over time, as data and users change, even if code stays the same. A tool that was fair on its original test set may become unfair as new kinds of inputs appear, as user groups shift, or as feedback loops, as in Section 12, amplify small initial gaps into larger ones. Drift is slow and often invisible in overall scores, because overall performance may stay steady while group breakdowns diverge, which is why regular fairness monitoring, as in Section 6, is essential, not optional, for tools that will be used over months or years. A tool's fairness is not a property earned once; it is a condition maintained through ongoing attention.

Several kinds of drift affect fairness. Input drift means new topics, slang, or formats appear, untested and possibly more common among certain groups. Population drift means a new user base adopts the tool, whose needs were never considered. Label drift means category meanings shift over time, so old labels no longer match current judgements. Each kind of drift can create or widen fairness gaps, even without any code change, because the tool's world has moved while its assumptions stayed fixed.

The defence is regular, scheduled re-evaluation, with the same breakdowns, plus fresh examples from recent use, with private details removed. Compare current breakdowns with past breakdowns, looking not just at overall scores, but at gaps between groups, at the worst-performing group, and at new groups or input types that were not in the original test set. If a gap has grown, investigate its cause, as in Chapter 21's error analysis, and fix it, whether the cause is new data, a feedback loop, or a changed category meaning. If a new group has appeared, add test cases for it, with consent where needed, and report its performance honestly, even if it is poor, rather than leaving it unmeasured.

Also update transparency notes and scope statements when drift is found, so users know that fairness has changed, and what is being done about it. A transparency note that still claims last year's fairness, while current breakdowns show a new gap, misleads users, however accurate it once was. Updating notes, with dates, shows that fairness is being monitored, and it gives users the information they need to decide whether to continue using the tool, to check extra carefully, or to wait for a fix. That updating habit, like evaluation logging, turns drift from a hidden risk into a managed one, because it ensures that fairness claims keep pace with fairness reality.

Finally, plan for drift by designing tools easy to re-evaluate. Keep test sets, audit scripts, and templates together, so re-evaluation takes minutes, not a fresh project. Keep data collection channels open, with consent, so that new kinds of inputs can be added to test sets as they appear. And keep a regular review date, for example every few months, for fairness, as well as for accuracy and safety, so that drift checks happen by schedule, not only when a user complains. A tool designed for easy re-evaluation will stay fairer longer, because its builders will actually re-evaluate it, while a tool that is hard to re-evaluate will drift unchecked, until a serious fairness failure forces attention, at much greater cost.

Section 36: Trade-offs: Accuracy Versus Fairness

Accuracy and fairness sometimes trade off against each other, meaning that improving one can worsen the other, at least in the short term, and builders must make that trade-off explicitly, rather than pretending it does not exist. For example, a classifier that maximises overall accuracy might do so by favouring the majority group, whose examples dominate the average, while a classifier adjusted to improve the minority group's performance might lose a few points of overall accuracy, because its threshold or features are no longer optimised purely for the average. That trade-off feels uncomfortable, because accuracy is easy to measure and fairness is harder, so accuracy often wins by default, unless builders deliberately protect fairness in their decisions.

The first step in handling the trade-off honestly is to measure both, side by side, for every change you consider. Report overall accuracy, accuracy by group, and the largest gap, before and after the change, so that the trade-off is visible in numbers, not just in feelings. A change that gains two points overall but widens the gap by ten points is not an unambiguous improvement; it is a trade-off that needs justification, and that justification should consider who benefits and who is harmed. Conversely, a change that loses one point overall but closes a fifteen-point gap may be a good trade, because it makes the tool much fairer at a small overall cost, especially if the overall loss falls on easy cases while the fairness gain helps users who were previously served very poorly.

Next, look for changes improving both accuracy and fairness, before accepting a trade-off. Often a gap is caused by a data gap, flawed feature, or unclear definition, and fixing it improves both, because the tool becomes better at inputs it handled poorly. Data fixes, feature fixes, and definition clarifications, as in Sections 16, 17, and 5, are win-win improvements, and they should be tried first, before resorting to threshold adjustments or other trade-off decisions that sacrifice one goal for the other. Builders who jump straight to trade-offs, without trying cause fixes, may accept unnecessary losses on both sides.

When a genuine trade-off remains, decide it by harm, not just points. Ask which errors harm most, and whether accuracy loss affects high-stakes cases or only low-stakes ones. A small loss on low-stakes cases, for a large fairness gain on access or reputation cases, is usually worth it. A fairness gain that comes at the cost of safety, however, as in Chapter 22, should not be accepted, because safety, like fairness, is not tradable against a few accuracy points; both are constraints that must be satisfied, within which accuracy is then maximised. That hierarchy, safety and fairness as constraints, accuracy as a goal within them, helps builders make trade-offs that users would endorse if they understood them.

Finally, report trade-offs transparently, with before-and-after numbers, reasoning, and a plan to revisit as data improves. An openly reported trade-off invites feedback, while one hidden behind an average invites suspicion when users discover the gap. Transparent trade-offs also protect builders over time, because they show that fairness was considered deliberately, not ignored, which is the standard responsible building is judged by, even when perfect solutions are unavailable.

Section 37: Explaining Fairness Decisions

Explaining fairness decisions means telling users and reviewers, in plain language, what fairness problem you found, what you did about it, why you chose that action over alternatives, and what remains to be done. Explanations matter because fairness decisions are often contested: different people may disagree about what is fair, about which group matters most, or about whether a trade-off was justified. An explanation that shows evidence, reasoning, and limits invites informed discussion and improvement, while a bare claim of "we made it fair" invites suspicion, because it hides the very choices that fairness work requires, and because it cannot be checked or challenged on its merits.

A good fairness explanation starts with evidence. State the gap you found, with numbers, sample sizes, and error types, as in Section 6, and state how you found it, such as through breakdowns by input type or by group, with consent where needed. Evidence grounds the explanation in facts, rather than in intentions, which is important because good intentions do not guarantee fair outcomes, and because evidence lets reviewers check whether the gap was real, how large it was, and whether it affected high-stakes cases. An explanation without evidence is a story; an explanation with evidence is an account that can be verified, debated, and improved.

Next, explain the action you took, and why you chose it over alternatives. Did you collect more data, clarify a definition, change a feature, adjust a threshold, add human review, or restrict scope? Why was that action chosen, what alternatives were considered, and what were their drawbacks? That reasoning shows that fairness work was thoughtful, not merely reactive, and it helps future builders facing similar problems, because they can learn from your reasoning, not just from your outcome. It also helps affected users understand that their concerns were weighed, even if the final decision does not fully satisfy everyone, which is often the case in fairness work, where perfect solutions are rare.

Also explain what remains, honestly. State the gaps that persist, the groups or input types still untested, and the plan and timeline for addressing them, if any. An explanation that only celebrates improvements, while hiding remaining gaps, misleads readers into thinking fairness work is finished, when it is really ongoing, as in Section 35. An explanation that names remaining gaps, with reasons and plans, shows that fairness is being pursued as a process, not as a one-time achievement, and it invites help from users and reviewers who may be able to fill those gaps, by contributing test cases, feedback, or expertise.

Finally, tailor the explanation to its audience. Users need a short, plain-language version, with concrete examples and clear implications for how they should use the tool, as in Section 19. Reviewers and collaborators need a fuller version, with numbers, methods, and trade-off reasoning, as in Chapter 21's reporting. Both versions should agree on facts, differing only in detail, so that no audience receives a misleadingly rosier picture than another. Consistent, evidence-based explanations, tailored in detail but not in facts, build trust across audiences, because they show that fairness decisions were made carefully, documented honestly, and communicated openly, which is the standard responsible builders should meet.

Section 38: A Worked Example: Sorting Messages Fairly

This section works through a fair sorting example, so you can see this chapter's methods applied to a concrete local tool. Imagine a message sorter that labels messages as urgent or routine, built on your own messages, with gold labels you wrote. On an overall test set of two hundred messages, it scores eighty-eight percent accuracy, which looks good. But when you break scores down, as in Section 6, you find a gap: short, informal messages score only sixty-five percent, while long, formal messages score ninety-five percent. Short informal messages are also more likely to be urgent messages mislabelled as routine, a false-negative pattern that delays help, as in Section 10. That breakdown, not the overall score, is the real fairness finding, because it names who is served worse and how they are harmed.

Next, investigate the cause. Reading misclassified short messages, you find urgency there is often indirect, such as "please check when you can, it matters," rather than urgent keywords like "urgent," which the classifier relies on. That keyword feature favours formal urgency language, a feature bias, compounded by a data gap: few short informal urgent examples in training. The cause is therefore not mysterious; it is a combination of feature choice and data representation, both fixable, which is encouraging, because fixable causes are the best kind of fairness problem to find.

Now fix the cause, in priority order. First, clarify the urgent definition, with indirect urgency examples, so gold labels are consistent. Second, collect more short informal urgent examples, with permission, or write synthetic ones mimicking indirect urgency, and add them to training and a held-out test set. Third, change the feature: instead of relying only on urgent keywords, add a feature for polite urgency phrases, or add human review for short messages scored near the threshold, as in Section 24, so borderline short messages are checked rather than automatically labelled routine. Each fix targets a specific cause, and together they address definition, data, and design, rather than merely tuning a threshold to hide the gap.

Then re-evaluate on the same set plus fresh held-out short messages. Suppose short informal accuracy rises from sixty-five to eighty-two percent, while long formal stays at ninety-four percent. The gap narrowed from thirty points to twelve, real progress though not equality, and the remaining gap should be reported, with a plan for more varied examples. That report, with before-and-after breakdowns, causes, fixes, and remaining gaps, as in Section 37, is a model fairness explanation, because it shows evidence, action, and limits together, rather than claiming the tool is now perfectly fair.

Finally, note what the example teaches: overall scores hide gaps, breakdowns reveal them, causes mix data, features, and definitions, and fixing causes then re-testing narrows gaps measurably, even if not eliminating them at once. Add this example to your fairness examples, and use its pattern, breakdown, investigate, fix causes, re-test, report, whenever you audit a new tool. That pattern, repeated, is the practical routine that turns fairness from a principle into a habit, and it works for local tools as well as for larger ones, because its steps are simple, its evidence is concrete, and its progress is trackable over time.

after = {"short informal": 0.82, "long formal": 0.94}

Section 39: Fairness Checklist for Builders

A builder's fairness checklist turns this chapter's methods into a routine you can run before releasing or sharing a tool, as in Chapters 21 and 22. Keep it short, on one page, and use it every time, even when busy, because fairness checks skipped under pressure are exactly the ones users need most. The checklist below is a starting point; adapt it to your tool, but keep it short enough to actually use, and update it when you learn a new failure pattern, so it evolves with your tool's fairness risks, rather than becoming a fixed ritual that no longer catches real gaps.

First, data and representation: does your data and test set cover the topics, styles, lengths, and formats your real users will bring, and are gaps named, with sample sizes, rather than hidden? Second, labels: are category definitions clear, with borderline examples, and have diverse labellers or judges checked a sample, with disagreements investigated, as in Section 5? Third, breakdowns: have you reported accuracy and error types by input type, topic, and, where appropriate and consented, by group, with the largest gap and worst group named, as in Section 6? Fourth, harms: have you checked for allocation, representation, and quality harms separately, as in Section 22, and have you weighed which errors harm most, for whom?

Fifth, design: have you checked definitions, features, thresholds, and ranking for fairness effects, and tried cause fixes before accepting trade-offs? Sixth, language: have you audited examples, word lists, and prompts for stereotypes and style bias? Seventh, privacy: have you measured fairness with the least intrusive method that reveals gaps, with consent and protection where group data was used, as in Section 18? Eighth, oversight and safety: does a human review high-stakes, borderline, and uncertain cases, with real authority and time, as in Section 24, and does safety block release for serious failures, as in Chapter 22?

Ninth, transparency: have you written a limits note, named an owner and feedback channel, and explained fairness decisions with evidence? Tenth, correction and monitoring: if a gap was found, was its cause fixed, was it added to test sets to prevent recurrence, and is there a schedule for re-evaluating fairness, as in Section 35, so drift will be caught? If any item fails, fix it or restrict the tool's use until it passes, rather than releasing with a known fairness weakness and hoping users will not notice, because users from affected groups will notice, on the inputs that matter to them, and the cost of post-release correction, in trust and rework, far exceeds pre-release fixing.

Finally, review the checklist itself regularly. If an item never catches anything, make it more specific, or replace it with a check that targets a real risk for your tool. If a failure occurs that the checklist did not catch, add an item that would have caught it, with a concrete test. A checklist that evolves with failures becomes steadily more effective, while a fixed checklist becomes less relevant as tools and risks change. The goal is not a longer list, but a list that reliably catches the fairness gaps your tool actually produces, in the forms they actually take, so that fairness checking becomes a habit, not a hurdle.

Section 40: Fairness Checklist for Users

Users need a fairness checklist too, because builders cannot check every output for every user, and because users are the last line of defence before an unfair output is acted on or shared. A user's checklist must be short, memorable, and focused on questions that reveal unfairness quickly, without requiring technical knowledge. Three to five questions, used consistently, will catch more unfairness than a long list that is never opened, especially when users are busy, which is when unfair outputs are most likely to slip through unnoticed, because checking feels like extra work on top of an already urgent task.

First, who is this output for, and was it tested for people like me? If a tool was tested only on formal notes, and you write informally, expect lower performance, check extra carefully, and report failures, so builders learn about the gap. Second, does this output treat similar cases similarly? If two similar messages, notes, or queries receive very different labels, rankings, or summaries, with no clear reason, that inconsistency may signal bias, a brittle feature, or a threshold effect, as in Section 7, and it deserves investigation, by checking sources and by asking the builder, rather than by accepting the difference as correct. Third, whose perspective is missing? If a summary keeps facts important to one viewpoint, while dropping facts important to another, or if examples show only one kind of person in a role, that selection or representation gap, as in Sections 22 and 30, is a fairness signal, even if every kept fact is true.

Fourth, what happens if this output is wrong, and for whom? If an error would harm you more than a typical user, check more thoroughly, especially the claim your action depends on. If an error would block, delay, or mislabel someone from a group already facing disadvantage, that harm deserves extra care, and possibly human review, rather than automatic acceptance. Fifth, can I correct this output, and will my correction be heard? A fair tool should make correction easy, explain how to appeal a label, and have a feedback channel that actually leads to changes. A tool that cannot be corrected is less fair in practice, however fair its scores look, because users have no recourse.

Teach this checklist to others, especially in shared work, where one unchecked unfair output can affect a team. Put it where users will see it: a shared guide or tool description. A visible, shared checklist becomes a habit protecting everyone, because each user's checking catches errors others missed, and a team reporting gaps together carries more weight.

Finally, user fairness checking is not distrust of AI; it is using AI as a helper, not an oracle. Sorting and ranking tools save enormous time, even when imperfectly fair, provided users check what matters, report gaps, and demand corrections. That partnership, tool for speed, human for fairness judgement, is the mature model for using AI tools responsibly, and it is far more sustainable than either blind trust, which allows unfairness to persist, or total distrust, which wastes genuine benefits. Check for fairness, in short, not because tools are useless, but because they are useful enough to be worth checking, for everyone they serve.

Section 41: Responsible Sharing of AI Tools

Sharing a tool, whether with a friend, a team, or publicly, extends its fairness responsibilities, because new users bring new inputs, new needs, and new vulnerabilities that the original builder may never have considered. A tool that was fair for its builder's own notes may be unfair for a wider audience, if that audience writes differently, needs different topics, or relies on the tool for higher-stakes decisions. Responsible sharing therefore starts with honesty about scope and limits, as in Section 19, and continues with support for new users to report problems, so that fairness gaps discovered after sharing can be fixed, rather than ignored because the tool has already been released.

Before sharing, re-evaluate on inputs resembling the new audience's, or state clearly it has not been tested for them, so new users can test cautiously. If sharing a formal-note summariser with informal writers, say so, and suggest they test it on their own notes first. That warning, given before sharing, prevents disappointment and harm, and it invites the new audience to contribute test cases, which improves the tool for everyone. Sharing without a scope warning, by contrast, asks new users to discover limits through failures, which is unfair, because those failures could have been predicted and disclosed in advance.

Also share evaluation and fairness materials alongside the tool, as in Section 34: test approach, breakdowns, biggest gap, and transparency note, in plain language, not just code. Users receiving only code cannot judge fit, and may assume general fairness from confident sharing. Users who receive code plus evaluation can judge fit, can check the tool on their own inputs, and can decide whether to use it, to use it with extra checking, or to wait for a version that covers them. Evaluation sharing is therefore part of responsible sharing, not an optional extra for advanced users.

Support shared tools after sharing, by keeping a feedback channel open, by responding to fairness reports, and by updating the tool and its transparency note when gaps are found. A shared tool that is abandoned, with no owner answering reports, will drift into unfairness as its world changes, as in Section 35, while its users continue to rely on outdated fairness claims. Support need not be elaborate: a simple channel, a regular review date, and a willingness to restrict scope or pause a feature when a serious gap is found, are enough to show that sharing was responsible, not merely a release-and-forget act. Users trust shared tools whose owners remain accountable, as in Section 23, far more than tools whose owners disappear after launch.

Finally, when sharing publicly, consider licensing and use guidance that discourages harmful uses, without overclaiming legal control you do not have. A short use note, such as "built for personal notes; not tested for high-stakes decisions; check outputs before sharing," guides users toward safe uses, even if it cannot prevent all misuse. That guidance, combined with scope honesty, evaluation sharing, and ongoing support, makes sharing a responsible act, one that spreads benefit while limiting harm, rather than a careless act that spreads a tool's fairness gaps to new audiences who had no part in its design and no warning about its limits.

Section 42: Bias in Evaluation Itself

Evaluation itself can be biased, because the people who design tests, write gold answers, and judge outputs bring their own backgrounds, assumptions, and blind spots, as Chapters 21 and 23 have warned in several places. If evaluators share a background, their shared norms may become the evaluation standard, so that tools serving other norms score poorly, not because they are worse, but because the evaluation measures conformity to one group's norms rather than task success for all groups. Bias in evaluation is especially dangerous, because it masquerades as objectivity: a biased score looks like a neutral measurement, while actually encoding the evaluators' perspective as if it were universal truth.

One source of evaluation bias is test set composition. If test cases are written mostly by one group, in one style, on familiar topics, then tools optimised for those cases will score well, while tools that serve other styles or topics will score poorly, even if they are equally good for their intended users. That is why diverse test sets, as in Sections 14 and 15, matter for evaluation fairness, not just for tool fairness: an evaluation that only tests one kind of input cannot fairly judge a tool meant for many kinds. Builders should therefore audit their test sets for diversity, as they audit their training data, and report test set composition alongside scores, so readers can judge whether the evaluation itself was fair.

Another source is gold answer bias, as in Section 5. If gold answers reflect one group's judgements about correctness or relevance, tools matching other groups' judgements will be marked wrong, even when defensible. Judge disagreement is a signal of bias or ambiguity, to investigate, not average away. Where judges from different backgrounds disagree systematically, the category or task may need clearer definitions, multiple acceptable gold answers, or a decision not to automate that judgement at all, because no single gold answer fairly represents all perspectives.

A third source is metric bias. Metrics that reward one style, such as formal language, or one kind of output, such as long summaries, will favour tools that produce that style, even when other styles are equally good for users. Word-overlap metrics, for example, reward copying gold wording, which favours tools whose style matches the gold authors' style, rather than tools that paraphrase well in a different style. Builders should therefore choose metrics that measure task success, such as facts covered or user task completion, rather than stylistic conformity, and should report multiple metrics, so that a tool is not judged unfairly by a single metric that encodes one group's preferences.

Finally, counter evaluation bias by diversifying evaluators, test cases, and metrics, and by reporting evaluation methods transparently, so that readers can judge the evaluation's fairness for themselves. Include judges with different backgrounds, test cases in different styles, and metrics that capture different aspects of success, and state the evaluation's limits, including groups or styles not covered. An evaluation reported with its methods, its test composition, and its limits is a fairer evaluation, because its biases, if any, are visible and can be corrected, while an evaluation reported as a bare score, without methods, hides its biases behind a number that looks objective but may not be.

Section 43: When Groups Are Small: Uncertainty

Small groups pose a special fairness challenge, because their test samples are small, their scores are uncertain, and their needs are easily overlooked in favour of larger groups whose scores are steadier and whose improvements move averages more. A builder who reports a small group's score as a precise percentage, based on five or ten examples, misleads readers, because that percentage will swing dramatically with one or two new examples, and because it implies a level of knowledge about that group that the data does not support. Honest fairness work for small groups therefore starts with honest uncertainty: reporting sample sizes, labelling small-sample scores as uncertain, and avoiding confident claims that small data cannot support.

Uncertainty does not mean ignoring small groups. On the contrary, small groups often need extra attention, because their small numbers make them vulnerable to being averaged away, and because their inputs may be systematically different in ways that larger groups' data does not capture. A tool that performs poorly for a small group, even on a small sample, deserves investigation, not dismissal as noise, especially if the poor performance follows a plausible pattern, such as a feature that disadvantages that group's style, or a data gap that explains the failure. Investigation, by reading the small group's failures, as in Chapter 21, can reveal a real cause even when statistical certainty is impossible, because qualitative patterns, such as a repeated kind of error, are informative even in small samples.

To handle small groups responsibly, combine quantitative and qualitative evidence. Report the small score, with size and uncertainty note, alongside a reading of failures: what errors occurred, whether they follow a pattern, and whether that pattern matches a known cause. That combination gives readers a fuller picture than either numbers alone, which are fragile, or stories alone, which may be anecdotal. It also guides action: if failures follow a pattern, fix the pattern, even if the score is uncertain, because pattern-based fixes help regardless of exact percentages, while waiting for a large sample before acting would leave a small group harmed in the meantime.

Also prioritise collecting more data for small groups, with consent, so future evaluations are more certain. Test sets can deliberately oversample small groups, to measure their performance reliably, even if that does not reflect their share of users. Oversampling for testing is a fairness practice, because it gives small groups measurement attention proportionate to their need for protection, rather than proportionate to their size, which would leave them perpetually under-measured and under-served.

Finally, when reporting small-group results publicly, protect privacy, as in Section 18, by aggregating where necessary, by avoiding details that could identify individuals in a small group, and by stating limits clearly. A small group is easier to re-identify from detailed breakdowns, because its members are fewer and more distinctive, so detailed reporting that would be safe for a large group may be unsafe for a small one. Balancing measurement, action, and privacy for small groups requires care, but it is worth it, because small groups are often the most vulnerable to unfair tools, and a builder who measures them honestly, acts on patterns, and protects their privacy is practising fairness where it matters most, not just where it is easiest to measure.

Section 44: Fairness in Multi-Step Tools

Multi-step tools, such as agents that search, summarise, and act, face fairness risks at each step, and those risks can compound across steps, as in Chapter 22's discussion of hallucination in chains. A search step that ranks one group's relevant documents lower, a summary step that drops facts important to another group, and an action step that applies a threshold unfairly, can together produce a final outcome that is far more unfair than any single step's bias alone, because biases accumulate rather than cancel out. Evaluating multi-step fairness therefore requires checking each step, as in Section 16 of Chapter 22, with fairness breakdowns per step, not just a final outcome score that hides where unfairness entered.

Start by defining fairness for each step separately. For search, relevant documents for each group should rank similarly. For summary, key facts for diverse readers should be kept. For action, thresholds should not systematically disadvantage one group. Write those criteria down before testing, so each step is judged against its own standard. Step-level criteria make fairness failures localisable: when the final outcome is unfair, you can trace it to the step where it entered, and fix that step, rather than guessing at the whole chain.

Next, test steps in isolation, with fixed inputs, so an early failure does not hide later steps' fairness. For example, give the summary step the same documents for all groups, and check whether its summaries are equally fair, regardless of retrieval. Then test the chain end to end, with realistic inputs, and compare step-level and end-to-end fairness: if steps are fair in isolation but the chain is unfair, the problem is in how steps connect, such as lost context, unclear handoffs, or accumulated small biases that grow across steps. That comparison, between step and chain fairness, is the key diagnostic for multi-step tools, and it is worth doing even for small local agents with only three or four steps.

Also check fairness at step boundaries, where one step's output becomes the next input. A summary introducing biased framing, or a search passing only top-ranked documents, can constrain later steps unfairly, even if later steps are fair in isolation, because they never see missing information. Boundary checks, which compare a step's output with its input source for fairness-relevant losses, such as dropped facts for certain groups, or skewed rankings, catch these boundary unfairnesses before they propagate. For local tools, boundary checks can be simple: compare fact lists before and after summarising, by group-relevant fact type, and flag systematic drops.

Finally, design multi-step tools with fairness gates: if a step's fairness check fails, or uncertainty is high for a group, pause, ask a human, or fall back to a safer step, rather than proceeding to an action based on unfair intermediates. Fairness gates, like safety gates, prevent a small early bias from becoming a large final harm, by requiring stronger evidence or human judgement when fairness is at risk. A multi-step tool with step-level fairness checks, boundary checks, and fairness gates is far fairer than one judged only by its final outcome, because its fairness does not depend on luck across a whole chain, but is protected at each link.

Section 45: Responsible AI and the Law in Plain Words

This section gives a plain-words overview of law and responsible AI, not legal advice; builders facing a real legal question should seek qualified advice for their jurisdiction. In general, laws that already apply to human decisions, such as laws against discrimination, laws protecting privacy, and laws requiring care in safety-critical work, also apply, in principle, to decisions made with AI tools, even though the tool is a machine. A builder cannot avoid responsibility for an unfair or harmful outcome by saying "the AI did it," because the law, like the accountability principle in Section 23, looks to the people who designed, deployed, and used the tool, not to the tool itself as if it were a person.

Discrimination law, in many places, prohibits unfair treatment because of protected characteristics, such as race, gender, disability, or age, in employment, housing, and services, and can apply to AI tools systematically disadvantaging a protected group, even unintentionally. That is why fairness breakdowns matter legally as well as ethically. Builders working in high-stakes areas should therefore be especially careful about fairness testing, documentation, and human review, and should seek legal guidance early, rather than after a complaint, because early guidance can shape design in ways that late guidance cannot easily retrofit.

Privacy law governs how personal data is collected, stored, shared, and used, and applies to AI data practices, including consent, minimisation, retention, and security. Tools collecting personal data without a lawful basis, keeping it too long, sharing it broadly, or storing it insecurely, may violate privacy law, even if intended only to improve the tool. Local builders, who often work with their own files and small datasets, face lower privacy risk than large-scale collectors, but they should still follow the practices in Chapter 24: collect only what is needed, with consent, store it securely, delete it when no longer needed, and document those practices, because those practices align with both ethical responsibility and common legal expectations.

Safety and consumer laws also matter, especially for tools giving advice users act on. A tool providing misleading safety information, or marketed as accurate when known to hallucinate or be unfair, may create legal risk, in addition to ethical harm. Honest marketing, as in Chapter 21, accurate transparency notes, as in Section 19, and safety gates, as in Chapter 22, therefore serve legal as well as ethical purposes, by ensuring users are not misled about a tool's reliability, and by showing that reasonable care was taken in its design and testing. Documentation of that care, in evaluation logs and fairness reports, is valuable evidence of responsible practice, whatever the legal context.

Finally, law varies by place and changes over time, and is only one part of responsible AI. A tool complying with current law in one place may still be unfair in ways ethics requires addressing, and an ethical tool may still need legal review for a new jurisdiction. Responsible builders therefore treat law as a floor, not a ceiling: they meet legal requirements, seek advice when unsure, and then go further, toward fairness, safety, and accountability, as this chapter teaches, because ethical responsibility often demands more than minimal legal compliance, and because users trust builders who aim higher than the minimum the law requires.

Section 46: Building a Fairer Local Tool

Building a fairer local tool brings this chapter's methods together into a practical workflow you can follow for any small tool, from a note summariser to a message sorter. Start by defining the task, the audience, and the fairness-relevant dimensions, such as style, length, topic, and language scope, before you write any code, so that fairness is designed in from the start, rather than audited in as an afterthought. That early definition, written in plain language in your project folder, guides data collection, test set building, and evaluation, because it names who the tool is for, what kinds of inputs it must handle fairly, and what kinds fall outside its scope, with reasons.

Next, build data and test sets with diversity in mind, as in Sections 14 to 16, using your own files plus, with permission, a few varied examples from others, or synthetic examples that mimic missing patterns without copying private data. Write gold answers with clear definitions and borderline examples, as in Section 5, and have at least one other person check a sample, where possible, to catch definition bias early. Build the tool simply, using only the standard library, with features and thresholds chosen for task relevance, not for style conformity, as in Section 3, and with human review for borderline and high-stakes cases, as in Section 24, built into the workflow from the first version, not added after a failure.

Then evaluate fairness alongside accuracy, from the first test run, using the audit approach from Section 26: report accuracy and error types by input type and, where appropriate, by group, name the largest gap and worst group, and investigate causes, whether data, features, definitions, or thresholds, before accepting any trade-off, as in Section 36. Fix the biggest cause first, re-test on held-out examples, and report before-and-after breakdowns honestly, including gaps that remain, as in Section 37. That evaluation loop, fairness measured, causes fixed, re-tested, reported, is the same honest loop as Chapters 21 and 22, applied to fairness, and it is what makes a tool fairer over time, rather than merely fair-sounding at launch.

Finally, share the fairer tool responsibly, as in Section 41, with its scope, its transparency note, its evaluation and fairness breakdowns, and a feedback channel, so new users can judge fit, test cautiously, and report gaps. Keep monitoring fairness over time, as in Section 35, with scheduled re-evaluations and updated transparency notes, so drift does not quietly undo early fairness work. A fairer local tool, built this way, will not be perfect, and its builder should not claim perfection, but it will be demonstrably fairer than a tool built without these steps, because its fairness was designed, measured, fixed, and reported, with evidence, rather than assumed. That demonstrable fairness is the practical goal of this chapter, and it is within reach of every local builder who follows the workflow consistently.

Section 47: Listening to Affected People

Listening to affected people means seeking out, hearing, and acting on the experiences of those who use a tool, are judged by it, or are excluded by it, especially those who bear its failures most heavily. Listening is not the same as collecting feedback forms that no one reads, or as asking users whether they "like" a tool, which tells you little about fairness. Real listening asks specific questions about fairness: which outputs felt unfair, which cases were mislabelled or buried, which summaries dropped facts that mattered to you, and what would make the tool fairer for you? Those questions, asked with genuine openness to uncomfortable answers, reveal fairness problems that metrics alone miss, because they capture how unfairness feels and what it costs, not just how often it occurs.

Listening well requires safe conditions for honesty. People harmed by a tool may be reluctant to speak, fearing dismissal or exposure. Builders should explain why they ask, how feedback will be used, whether it stays private, and what changes will follow, and should thank contributors and report back on what was done, as in Section 33. A feedback process that never reports back teaches contributors that listening was performative, and they will stop contributing, while a process that reports back, even when it cannot fix everything, teaches them that listening was real, and encourages future honesty.

Listening also requires acting, not just hearing. When affected people report a fairness gap, investigate it, as in Section 38, fix its cause where possible, or explain, honestly, why it cannot yet be fixed and what would need to change. Acting on a few reported gaps well is more valuable than collecting many reports and acting on none, because action builds trust, while inaction, despite listening, destroys it faster than never asking at all. Prioritise reported gaps by harm, as in Chapter 21, so that the most harmful reported problems are addressed first, even if they affect fewer users than less harmful, more common problems, because harm, not just frequency, should guide fairness priorities.

Do not treat one person's experience as representative of a whole group, or dismiss a report because it comes from one person. Individual reports are signals pointing to patterns worth investigating, not verdicts alone. Investigate a report by testing similar cases, by checking breakdowns, and by asking others with similar experiences, where possible, to see whether the pattern holds. That investigation, combining individual experience with systematic testing, is the strongest form of fairness evidence, because it joins lived experience, which reveals harm, with measurement, which reveals scope, and together they guide fixes that are both humane and effective.

Finally, make listening routine, not one-time. Schedule regular check-ins with users, especially from groups that previously performed poorly, and update fairness work based on what you hear, alongside what you measure. A tool whose builders listen regularly will stay fairer longer, because its fairness work will be guided by real, current experiences, not by outdated assumptions from its launch. Listening, in this routine, humble form, is not a soft skill separate from technical fairness work; it is technical fairness work, because it supplies the information, about harms and priorities, that no metric can supply alone, and because it keeps builders accountable to the people their tools actually affect.

Section 48: Fairness Reports That Are Honest

A fairness report is a short document that tells readers how fairly a tool performs, for whom, on what evidence, and with what limits, as part of the broader evaluation reporting in Chapter 21. An honest fairness report does not claim perfect fairness, does not hide the worst group, and does not present a single fairness number as if it settled the question. Instead, it presents breakdowns, methods, gaps, causes, fixes, and remaining work, in plain language, so that readers can judge fairness for themselves, can replicate the measurements where possible, and can decide whether the tool fits their needs, with its fairness limits in mind. Honesty in fairness reporting is what makes fairness claims trustworthy, because readers know that a builder who reports gaps honestly is more likely to fix them than a builder who hides them.

An honest report starts with scope and methods. State what tool was tested, for what task and audience, on how many examples, of what kinds, with what gold answer rules, and with what breakdown dimensions, such as input type, topic, or, where consented, group. State sample sizes for each breakdown, and label small samples as uncertain, as in Section 43, so readers do not over-interpret fragile scores. Methods transparency lets readers judge whether the evaluation was fair, as in Section 42, and it lets other builders replicate or improve the evaluation, which is how fairness measurement itself improves over time, through shared, checkable methods rather than private, unverifiable claims.

Next, report results fully, including worst results. Give accuracy and error breakdowns by dimension, name the largest gap and worst group, and describe the most harmful error pattern, with anonymised examples where needed, as in Section 34. Do not average gaps away, omit a poor dimension, or present an improvement without before-and-after numbers. A report that includes worst results is stronger, not weaker, than one that omits them, because it shows the builder has actually looked for unfairness, rather than merely asserting its absence.

Then explain causes, actions, and trade-offs, as in Section 37. State what caused each major gap, what evidence supports it, what fix was tried, what trade-offs were accepted, and what remains, with a timeline where possible. That explanation turns a report from a snapshot into a plan, which is what readers, especially affected users, need most: not just a verdict on current fairness, but a credible account of how fairness will improve, and how they can contribute test cases or feedback to that improvement. A report without a plan leaves readers with a problem and no path forward; a report with a plan invites collaboration toward a fairer tool.

Finally, update the report regularly, with dates, and keep old versions accessible, so progress, regressions, and drift are visible over time. A report never updated becomes misleading, however honest it once was, because fairness claims must keep pace with change. Regular updating, with old versions preserved, shows that fairness is monitored, not merely declared, and it builds the long-term trust that makes users willing to rely on a tool, report its failures, and help improve it. Honest fairness reporting, in this full, updated form, is the public face of responsible building, and it is worth the time it takes to write well.

Section 49: Responsibility as a Habit

Responsibility, like honest evaluation and hallucination checking, is a habit, practised every time you build, share, or update a tool, not a badge earned once at launch. The habit has a few core parts, repeated so often that they become automatic: ask who is affected, before building; test fairly, with breakdowns, before sharing; state limits honestly, when sharing; listen to affected people, after sharing; and correct, fix, and report, when failures are found. Those parts, taken together, do not guarantee perfect fairness or safety, but they greatly reduce the chance that a tool will harm people unfairly, and they ensure that when harm does occur, it will be found, understood, and addressed, rather than hidden or ignored.

Make the habit easy to keep, by building it into your workflow, as in Section 46. Keep your fairness checklist, as in Section 39, your audit script, as in Section 26, and your transparency template, as in Section 19, in your project folder, so that fairness checking takes minutes, not a fresh effort each time. Schedule fairness reviews alongside accuracy and safety reviews, so they happen by calendar, not only when a complaint forces them. Celebrate fairness improvements, even small ones, such as narrowing a gap by ten points, because small, verified improvements compound into much fairer tools over time, while waiting for a perfect fairness fix often means no fix at all, and a gap that persists unaddressed while builders chase perfection.

Also make the habit social, by sharing it with collaborators, users, and readers, as in Sections 41 and 47. Tell users what you checked for fairness, what you found, and what you are fixing, and invite them to report gaps and to test on their own inputs. Share your checklists and audit scripts, so other builders can use them, and learn from your fairness work, as you learn from theirs. A habit practised by a community is robust, because each person's checking catches gaps others missed, and because community expectation makes skipping fairness checks feel unusual, rather than normal. A habit practised alone is fragile, because it depends on one person's discipline on a busy day, when fairness work is easiest to skip and most needed.

Finally, remember why responsibility as a habit matters, beyond any single tool. AI tools increasingly shape who gets seen, heard, sorted, and served, and builders who practise responsibility consistently help create a world where those decisions are made fairly, transparently, and accountably, rather than opaquely and unfairly. That contribution, made one breakdown, one fix, one honest report at a time, is the lasting value of this chapter's work, and it is a habit worth keeping, even when it slows a release, even when it reveals uncomfortable gaps, and even when no one is watching, because the people affected by your tools deserve fairness whether or not they can see the work that produced it. Responsibility, as a habit, is simply building as if affected people matter, because they do, and acting accordingly, every time.

Section 50: Responsible AI: What Good Looks Like

Good responsible AI, in practice, looks less like a perfect tool and more like a trustworthy process, visible in how a tool is built, tested, shared, and maintained. A good tool has a clear task and audience, stated in plain language, with scope limits that match its testing. It has diverse, consented data and test sets, with gaps named, not hidden. It has evaluation and fairness breakdowns, reported honestly, including worst results and small-sample uncertainty. It has sources, uncertainty signals, and human review for high-stakes cases, so its outputs can be checked and corrected. And it has an owner, a feedback channel, and a history of corrections, showing that problems lead to fixes, not to silence. Those features, together, are what good looks like, and they are achievable for local builders, as this book has shown, without large teams or budgets.

Good responsible AI also looks like good relationships with users. Users know what a tool does poorly, how to report problems, and see reports lead to changes, or honest explanations why a change cannot yet be made. Affected people, including those who do not use the tool directly, are considered in its design, are listened to when they report harm, and are protected by privacy practices that minimise their exposure. Builders who maintain those relationships build trust that survives individual failures, because users know failures will be heard and addressed, while builders who neglect relationships lose trust at the first serious failure, even if their tool was technically impressive, because users have no reason to believe the next failure will be handled any better.

Good responsible AI also looks like restraint. A good builder knows when not to build, when to narrow scope, when to add human review, and when to pause a feature, as in Sections 24 and 31, and they act on that knowledge, even when it costs time, features, or excitement. Restraint is not a lack of ambition; it is ambition directed at trustworthy tools, rather than at impressive demos, because a restrained tool that serves its stated scope fairly is more valuable than an unrestrained tool that serves a broad scope unfairly. Users, over time, learn to value restraint, because they learn that restrained tools can be relied on within their stated limits, while unrestrained tools cannot be relied on at all, because their limits were never stated or honoured.

Finally, good responsible AI looks like ongoing improvement, not a finished state. Fairness gaps narrow, then new gaps appear as users and inputs change, and good builders keep measuring, listening, fixing, and reporting, as in Section 35, without claiming final victory. That ongoing work, visible in updated reports, narrowed gaps, and corrected errors, is the real evidence of responsibility, far more convincing than a single claim of fairness at launch. If you finish this chapter with a workflow for building fairly, a checklist for checking fairly, a habit of listening and reporting honestly, and a willingness to restrain scope when fairness cannot yet be demonstrated, then you have learned what good responsible AI looks like, and you are ready to practise it, one tool at a time, for the people who will use, and be affected by, what you build.
