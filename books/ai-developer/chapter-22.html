Chapter 22: Hallucination and How to Reduce It

Section 1: What Hallucination Means

Hallucination, in AI, means that a system produces something that sounds true but is not supported by evidence. It is not a technical fault in the sense of a crash or a typo. It is a fluent, confident statement that is invented, distorted, or simply wrong, presented as if it were fact. A hallucination might be a date that never happened, a quote nobody said, a book that does not exist, or a detail added to a summary that was never in the original text. The danger is not that the system makes mistakes; all systems do. The danger is that hallucinations look exactly like correct answers, so readers trust them without checking.

The word hallucination can be misleading, because it suggests the machine is seeing things or dreaming. It is not. A language model does not see or dream; it predicts likely words, based on patterns it learned. When it lacks the right fact, it may still predict words that fit the pattern beautifully, producing a plausible answer from weak evidence. Understanding that mechanism, which this chapter explains in plain words, helps you avoid two opposite mistakes: treating hallucinations as deliberate lies, and treating them as harmless quirks. They are neither. They are predictable failures of a prediction system, and they need practical defences.

Hallucinations matter most when the reader cannot easily check the answer. A wrong capital city is easy to spot, because most readers know it or can check it in seconds. A wrong date in a historical summary, a wrong dose in a health explanation, or a wrong clause in a legal summary is much harder to spot, because the reader may not know the topic well, and may be reading precisely because they need to learn it. That is why hallucination is a trust problem, not just an accuracy problem: it strikes exactly where the reader is most dependent on the tool, and least able to catch its errors.

It also matters that hallucinations are often mixed with truth. A summary might get nine facts right and invent the tenth. A list might contain four real items and one invented item. A biography might be accurate until a single invented award appears halfway through. Mixed outputs are harder to judge than wholly wrong outputs, because the correct parts earn trust that the invented part then exploits. When you evaluate, as in Chapter 21, you must therefore check fact by fact, not impression by impression, because a good impression can be built from mostly true material plus one dangerous invention.

Finally, hallucination is not a problem you solve once and forget. It is a risk you manage, with habits, checks, and design choices that reduce how often it happens and how much harm it causes when it does. Those habits include grounding answers in sources you control, asking for uncertainty to be shown, checking claims before sharing, and designing tools that prefer saying "I do not know" over guessing. This chapter teaches those habits, with small local examples you can run on your own machine, so you can practise spotting and reducing hallucinations before you build tools others will rely on.

Section 2: Why Language Models Invent Things

Language models work by predicting likely next words, given the words that came before. They are trained on huge amounts of text, and they learn patterns: which words tend to follow which, which phrases sound natural, and how different kinds of writing are structured. When you ask a question, the model does not look up a fact in a table and report it. It generates a sequence of words that fits the patterns it learned, and that sequence often contains the right fact, because the right fact is also the most likely continuation in many cases. But when the right fact is rare, unclear, or absent from its training patterns, the most likely continuation may still sound perfect while being factually wrong.

Think of it like completing a familiar story from memory. If you know the story well, you will complete it correctly. If you only half remember it, you may still complete it smoothly, filling gaps with plausible details that feel right but are not in the original. Language models do something similar at scale: they fill gaps with plausible continuations, because their job, as designed, is to produce likely text, not to verify truth. That design is powerful for writing, brainstorming, and drafting, where plausibility is useful, but it is risky for factual answers, where truth matters more than fluency.

Another reason models invent things is that they are trained to be helpful and to answer, even when they should not. A model that says "I do not know" to every hard question looks unhelpful, so training and instructions often push it toward giving an answer, any answer, rather than admitting ignorance. That pressure, combined with prediction, produces confident guesses on questions where a careful human would pause, check, or refuse. Builders can counteract this pressure in their own tools, as later sections show, by rewarding honest uncertainty and by requiring sources before factual claims are made.

Models also invent because they generalise from patterns that are mostly true but not always true. If most biographies in training data mention an award, a model may add an award to a biography where none exists, because the pattern "biography includes award" is strong. If most lists of a certain kind have five items, a model may invent a fifth item to complete the pattern, even when only four real items exist. These pattern completions feel natural because they follow familiar shapes, and readers, who also expect those shapes, may not notice the extra item. Pattern completion is a strength for style, and a weakness for facts.

Understanding these causes points to the right defences, which are not about scolding the model, but about changing the task. If you give the model, or your local tool, the facts it needs in its input, and ask it to use only those facts, you reduce the need to guess. If you ask it to show its source for each claim, you make guessing visible. If you check its outputs against sources before sharing, you catch guesses before they reach readers. Hallucination cannot be eliminated by wishing it away, but it can be greatly reduced by designing tasks where guessing is unnecessary, visible, and checked.

Section 3: Confident Wording, Weak Evidence

One of the most misleading features of hallucinations is their confident wording. Invented facts are rarely presented hesitantly. They are presented in the same smooth, certain style as true facts, with phrases like "it is well known that," "the study proves," or simply a bare statement with no qualifier at all. Readers naturally use confidence as a signal of reliability: when someone speaks confidently, we tend to believe them. That habit, which works reasonably well with careful humans, fails with language models, whose confidence reflects how likely the wording is, not how strong the evidence is. Learning to separate wording confidence from evidence strength is a core skill for readers and builders alike.

Strong wording needs strong evidence. Words like "always," "never," "proves," and "certainly" claim a high level of certainty, and they should only be used when the evidence genuinely supports them. Weaker wording, like "suggests," "may," "sometimes," or "one study found," claims less, and is appropriate when evidence is limited, mixed, or indirect. When you read an AI output, check whether its wording matches its evidence: does it cite a source, does it name the study, does it give numbers that can be checked? If the wording is strong but the evidence is missing, vague, or second-hand, treat the claim as uncertain, however confidently it is phrased.

Builders can help by designing tools that match wording to evidence. A local summariser, for example, can be instructed, in its own rules, to use cautious wording when a fact appears only once in the source, or when the source itself is uncertain. It can be required to say "the note says" rather than stating a fact directly, when it is reporting rather than verifying. These small wording rules do not make the tool smarter, but they make its uncertainty visible, which helps readers decide what to check. A tool that says "the note suggests" is giving the reader a fair warning that a tool saying "the note proves" is not.

Readers can also train themselves to notice confidence tricks. Watch for absolute words, for precise numbers attached to vague claims, and for long, detailed descriptions of things that should be uncertain. Precision is not proof: an invented date like "14 March 2019" looks more trustworthy than a vague "in 2019," but it is not more true merely because it is precise. In fact, false precision is a common hallucination pattern, because models generate specific details to complete a pattern, and readers mistake specificity for accuracy. When a claim is surprisingly specific, ask where that specificity came from, and whether a source supports it.

Finally, when you write or share AI outputs, match your own wording to your evidence too. Do not upgrade "suggests" to "proves" when you summarise, do not drop qualifiers that were in the original, and do not present a guess as a fact because it sounds better that way. Honest wording is a habit that protects readers, and it costs nothing except a little care. In a world full of confident-sounding text, a builder who writes cautiously, and whose tools do the same, stands out as trustworthy, precisely because they refuse to sound more certain than they are.

Section 4: Dates, Numbers and Names: Where Errors Hide

Hallucinations are not evenly spread across all kinds of facts. They cluster around dates, numbers, and names, because those details are both important and easy to invent plausibly. A date can be shifted by a year, a number can be rounded or swapped, and a name can be replaced by a similar-sounding one, without breaking the sentence's grammar or flow. Readers often skim over such details, assuming they are correct because the surrounding text is correct, which is exactly why these small inventions cause outsized harm: a wrong date can mislead a timeline, a wrong number can mislead a budget, and a wrong name can mislead an attribution.

Dates are especially vulnerable because they appear in many formats, and because relative dates like "next Monday" or "last year" depend on context. A tool that converts a relative date to an absolute date without knowing the reference point may invent a plausible but wrong date. A tool that summarises a long document may keep the year but change the month, or keep the event but attach it to the wrong year. When you check outputs, therefore, check every date separately, against the source, even if the rest of the summary looks perfect. Dates deserve their own check, because they are both easy to get wrong and easy to verify, if you bother to look.

Numbers are vulnerable for a similar reason: they look precise, so readers trust them, but they are often generated as part of a pattern rather than copied from a source. A percentage might be invented to complete a sentence like "sales rose by X percent," a count might be rounded up to a neat number, or a measurement might be converted incorrectly. When you check numbers, do not just check that they look plausible; check that they match the source exactly, including units, and that any calculation based on them is shown, not just asserted. If a summary says "a third of respondents," check whether the source actually says that, or whether the tool estimated it from a different figure.

Names, including people, places, books, and organisations, are vulnerable because similar names are easily confused. A model may attribute a quote to the wrong author, name the wrong city, or invent a book title that sounds like a real one. These errors are hard to spot unless you know the topic, which is why checking names against a trusted source, or against your own notes, matters so much. For local tools, a simple defence is to require that names in an output appear in the input source, and to flag any name that does not, for human review before sharing.

The practical lesson is to check the small details first, not last. When you review an output, scan for dates, numbers, and names, and verify each one against its source, before you judge style or completeness. That habit catches the most common and most harmful hallucinations quickly, and it trains you to read AI outputs as a checker, not just as a reader. A summary that passes the date, number, and name checks is not guaranteed to be perfect, but it has passed the checks where hallucinations most often hide, which is a strong start.

Section 5: Checking Facts Against a Source

The single most effective defence against hallucination is also the simplest: check each factual claim against a source you trust, before you believe or share it. A source might be the original document being summarised, your own notes, a textbook, or an official record. The key is that the source exists independently of the AI output, so that checking is a comparison, not a repetition of the same guess. If you ask the same tool to check its own output, without a new source, you may simply get the same hallucination repeated with confidence, which feels like verification but is not.

Checking works best claim by claim. Break an output into its individual factual claims: who did what, when, where, and with what result. For each claim, find the corresponding passage in the source, and ask whether the source actually supports it, in the same strength and with the same details. A claim that is partly supported, such as a correct event with a wrong date, should be marked as partly wrong, not as correct, because the wrong detail matters. This claim-by-claim method is slower than reading for overall impression, but it is the only method that reliably catches mixed outputs, where most claims are true and one is invented.

For local tools, you can automate part of this checking with simple Python. A toy checker can extract dates, numbers, and capitalised names from an output, and check whether each one appears in the source text. That checker will not catch every hallucination, especially paraphrased claims, but it will catch the common cases where a detail was invented outright. More importantly, building such a checker teaches you what checking can and cannot do automatically, and why human judgement remains essential for the final verdict. Automation helps, but it does not replace careful reading.

Also check the source itself. A source can be outdated, mistaken, or misquoted, and an output that faithfully copies a wrong source is still wrong for the reader, even if it is not a hallucination in the strict sense. Ask whether the source is trustworthy for this claim, whether it is current, and whether it actually says what the output claims it says, rather than something nearby. If the source is weak, say so, and do not upgrade its claim when you share it. Faithful copying of a weak source, presented as strong fact, is a different failure, but it harms readers in the same way.

Finally, make checking a visible part of your workflow, not a private afterthought. When you share a summary or an answer, note what you checked, and what remains unchecked, so readers know how much to trust it. A note like "dates and names checked against the original; interpretation is my own" is honest and helpful. It invites readers to check further where it matters to them, and it models the habit this chapter teaches: trust is earned claim by claim, against sources, not granted to a whole output because it reads smoothly.

output = "The meeting was on 2024-05-01 in Lagos."
print("Dates match:", "2024-05-01" in source and "2024-05-01" in output)

Section 6: Retrieval as an Antidote

Retrieval, introduced in Chapter 17, is one of the strongest antidotes to hallucination, because it changes the task from "guess a fact from patterns" to "find a fact in a source, then report it." When a tool first searches your own notes or documents for relevant passages, and then bases its answer only on those passages, it has far less need to invent. The facts are in front of it, in its input, rather than buried in vague patterns. Retrieval does not eliminate hallucination, because a tool can still misread, misquote, or overgeneralise a retrieved passage, but it reduces the most common cause: answering without evidence at hand.

For local tools, retrieval can be very simple. A search over your own text files, using word overlap, can find the note most relevant to a question. Your tool can then quote or summarise only that note, and name it as its source, so the reader can check. That simple pipeline, search then summarise, is far safer than asking a tool to answer from memory alone, especially for questions about your own work, your own notes, or topics where you have a trusted document. The retrieved passage acts as an anchor: if the answer drifts away from it, the drift is visible, because the reader can compare answer and source side by side.

Retrieval also helps when the answer is "not found." If no relevant passage exists in your collection, the honest answer is to say so, rather than inventing one. A retrieval tool that reports "no matching note found" is doing its job well, even though it did not answer the question, because it refused to guess. Builders should design for this case explicitly: if the search score is low, or if no passage contains the needed fact, the tool should say it cannot find the answer in your files, and suggest where the user might look, rather than producing a fluent guess. That refusal is a feature, not a failure, and users will trust a tool more when it refuses honestly than when it guesses smoothly.

Be careful, however, not to over-trust retrieval. A retrieved passage can be irrelevant, outdated, or misunderstood, and a tool that copies it blindly may still mislead. Always check that the retrieved passage actually answers the question, not merely mentions the topic, as in Chapter 21's retrieval evaluation. And check that the summary of the passage preserves its meaning, including its qualifiers and limits. Retrieval plus careful summarising, plus claim-by-claim checking, is a strong combination. Retrieval alone, without those checks, is better than guessing, but it is not yet a guarantee of truth.

Finally, keep your retrieval collection trustworthy. A search over unreliable documents will retrieve unreliable facts, however well the search works. Curate the notes and documents your tool searches over: remove outdated versions where you can, label uncertain notes as uncertain, and keep trusted sources separate from drafts and guesses. A small, curated collection, searched honestly, will produce fewer hallucinations than a large, messy collection searched cleverly, because the quality of the answer cannot exceed the quality of the source it is grounded in, however good the tool itself may be.

query = "meeting"
matches = [k for k, v in notes.items() if query in v.lower()]

Section 7: Saying I Do Not Know

Saying "I do not know" is one of the most valuable behaviours an AI tool can have, and one of the hardest to build, because users, builders, and training all push toward always answering. Yet a tool that admits ignorance when evidence is missing protects users far more than a tool that guesses. A guess, presented as fact, can mislead a decision, waste time, or cause harm. An honest "I do not know, and here is what would help me find out" invites the user to provide more information, check a source, or make a decision with eyes open. That invitation is genuine help, even though it is not an answer.

Building this behaviour starts with recognising the situations where ignorance is the right response. If no source contains the fact, if sources disagree and the tool cannot resolve the disagreement, if the question is ambiguous and different interpretations lead to different answers, or if the question asks for a prediction that cannot be known, then "I do not know" or "I cannot tell from the information available" is the honest answer. Write these situations down as rules for your local tools, and test them explicitly, with examples where the correct output is a refusal, not an answer. A tool that passes refusal tests is safer than one that only passes answer tests.

Wording matters for refusals too. A blunt "I do not know" can feel unhelpful, especially if the user expected an answer. A better refusal explains what is missing, and what could help: "I do not know, because none of your notes mention that date. If you add a note about it, I can answer next time." Or: "I cannot tell from this summary alone, because the original does not state the result. You would need to check the full report." Such refusals are helpful, because they guide the user toward a solution, rather than merely blocking them. They also model honest uncertainty, which users learn to recognise and value.

Users also need to learn that refusal is not failure. If you share a tool that sometimes refuses, explain why, in plain language, before users encounter their first refusal. Say that the tool prefers to say it does not know rather than guess, and that a refusal means the evidence was missing, not that the tool is broken. Users who understand this will trust the tool's answers more, because they know that when it does answer, it had evidence. Users who expect an answer every time will be frustrated by refusals, and may pressure the builder to remove them, which would make the tool less safe, not more helpful.

Finally, evaluate refusals as part of evaluation, not as an afterthought. Count how often your tool refuses, on which kinds of questions, and whether those refusals were correct: did it refuse when it should have answered, or answer when it should have refused? Both errors matter. Over-refusal wastes user time and makes a tool feel useless. Under-refusal, which is guessing, misleads users. The right balance depends on stakes: for high-stakes questions, err toward refusal; for low-stakes brainstorming, err toward offering ideas clearly labelled as ideas, not facts. Naming that balance, and testing it, is part of building a trustworthy tool.

def answer(question, found):
    if not found:
        return "I do not know: no matching note found."
    return found

Section 8: Citations and Showing Your Work

A citation is a pointer to the source of a claim: which document, which note, or which passage it came from. Showing citations, or at least naming sources, is a powerful defence against hallucination, because it makes checking easy. A reader who sees a claim with a source can open that source and verify it in seconds. A reader who sees a claim with no source must either trust blindly or search from scratch, which most readers will not do. Citations therefore turn a private guess into a checkable claim, and checkable claims are safer claims, even when they are sometimes wrong, because their errors can be found.

For local tools, citations can be simple. A summariser can list the notes it used, by filename, at the end of its summary. A search tool can show the matching passage, not just its conclusion, so the reader can see the evidence. A question-answering tool over your own files can quote the relevant sentence, with the filename beside it, rather than paraphrasing it into a bare fact. These simple practices do not require complex technology, and they do not require external services. They require only the discipline to keep the source attached to the claim, through every step of processing, rather than dropping it for the sake of a cleaner-looking output.

Citations must be honest, however. A citation that points to a source which does not actually support the claim is worse than no citation, because it borrows the source's credibility for a claim the source never made. When you build or check a tool, verify citations as carefully as claims: open the cited source, find the passage, and ask whether it supports the claim in the same strength and detail. If a tool cites a whole document for a specific fact, ask whether that fact is actually in the document, or whether the citation is vague decoration. Vague citations, like "according to sources," without naming any source, are not citations at all; they are appeals to authority without evidence.

Also be careful about citation style for different audiences. For your own notes, a filename and a date may be enough. For shared work, a fuller reference, with title and date, helps readers find the source later. For summaries of long documents, citing the section or page, where possible, saves readers from searching the whole document. The principle is the same in every case: give the reader enough information to find and check the source without undue effort. If checking takes too long, readers will skip it, and the citation's protective value is lost.

Finally, when you share AI outputs, keep their citations attached, and do not present cited claims as if you verified them yourself unless you actually did. If a tool gave you a claim with a citation, and you have not checked it, say so, or check it before sharing. Passing on an unchecked citation as if it were your own verification transfers false confidence to your readers. Honest citation practice, from tool to builder to reader, is a chain: each link must actually check, or honestly say it has not, for the chain to protect anyone at all.

Section 9: Summaries That Invent Details

Summaries are a common place for hallucinations, because summarising involves choosing, compressing, and rewording, and each of those steps can introduce a new detail that was never in the original. A summary might add a cause that the original never stated, turn a suggestion into a decision, or fill a gap in the original with a plausible detail that makes the summary read more smoothly. These inventions are often small, a word here, a date there, but they change meaning, and readers who only read the summary will learn the invented version, believing it to be the original's content.

One common pattern is elaboration: the summary adds background that sounds helpful but was not in the original. For example, an original note might say "meeting moved," and a summary might say "meeting moved because of rain," inventing a cause. The addition feels helpful, because it explains, but it is not in the source, and the reader has no way to tell, from the summary alone, which parts are original and which are added. A safe summary rule is: do not add causes, motives, or background unless they are in the source, or clearly labelled as the summariser's guess. If a cause is not stated, the honest summary says the meeting moved, without explaining why.

Another pattern is sharpening: turning vague original wording into specific summary wording. An original that says "several people attended" might become "ten people attended" in a summary, inventing a number. An original that says "around March" might become "on 15 March," inventing precision. Sharpening feels like improvement, because specific sounds better than vague, but it is invention, not summarising. A safe rule is to preserve the original's level of vagueness: if the original says "several," the summary should say "several," not a number. Precision should come from the source, never from the summariser's desire for a neater sentence.

A third pattern is merging: combining details from different parts of the original into one statement that implies a connection the original never made. For example, combining "the team met on Monday" and "the budget was approved" into "the team approved the budget on Monday" invents a link between two separate facts. Merging is tempting because it makes summaries shorter and smoother, but it changes meaning. When you summarise, keep separate facts separate, unless the original explicitly connects them, and be especially careful with dates, places, and people, where a merged sentence can silently attach the wrong detail to the wrong event.

The defence against all three patterns is the same: check the summary against the original, fact by fact, as in Section 5, and flag any detail in the summary that cannot be found in the original. For local tools, a simple check, as in Section 5, can flag invented dates, numbers, and names automatically, while human reading catches invented causes and connections. A summary that passes that check, with no added details, may still be incomplete, but it is at least faithful, and faithfulness is the foundation on which a useful summary must be built.

Section 10: Code and Instructions That Look Right

Hallucination is not limited to facts about history or people. It also appears in code, instructions, and explanations of how things work, where a plausible-looking answer can be wrong in ways that are hard to spot until you try to run it or follow it. A generated code snippet might use a function that does not exist, call a function with the wrong arguments, or solve a slightly different problem from the one asked, all while looking clean and professional. A set of instructions might skip a crucial step, reorder steps in a way that breaks the task, or invent a setting that does not exist. These are hallucinations too, because they present invented details as if they were correct, in a form readers trust.

The defence for code is the same as for facts, but with a stronger tool available: running it. Never trust a code snippet merely because it looks right. Run it on your own machine, with your own test inputs, and check that it produces the expected output. If it fails, read the error message, which often points directly to the invented function or wrong argument. If it runs but gives the wrong answer, test it on simple cases where you know the correct answer, and compare. Running code is the equivalent of checking a fact against a source: it turns a plausible claim into a tested one, and it catches inventions that reading alone might miss.

For instructions, the defence is to follow them, step by step, on a safe, local example, before sharing them or relying on them for an important task. If an instruction says to change a setting, check that the setting exists, with that name, in the place described. If it says to run a command, check what the command does, and run it in a safe environment first, where mistakes cannot harm important files. Instructions that cannot be followed, or that produce a different result from the one promised, should be treated as failed, however well written they are. Smooth instructions, like smooth facts, are not evidence of correctness.

Builders should also design code and instruction tools to show their work. A tool that generates code should name the language version, list any assumptions, and suggest tests the user can run. A tool that generates instructions should list prerequisites, and warn about steps that cannot be undone. These practices do not guarantee correctness, but they help users check, which is the real goal. A code snippet with a suggested test is far safer than a snippet presented as perfect, because the test invites the user to verify, rather than to trust blindly.

Finally, when you share code or instructions produced with AI help, say so, and say what you tested. A note like "tested on Python 3 with these two examples" tells readers exactly how much checking was done, and what remains to be checked. Sharing untested code as if it were proven is a form of hallucination by proxy: you pass on an invention with your own credibility attached. Testing, even briefly, and reporting the test honestly, breaks that chain, and protects both you and your readers from plausible-looking errors.

Section 11: Testing for Hallucination Locally

You do not need a large laboratory to test for hallucination. You can test on your own machine, with your own files, using small, deliberate test cases designed to tempt guessing. The idea is to create situations where the correct answer is known, or where the correct answer is "I do not know," and then see whether your tool guesses anyway. Those temptation tests reveal hallucination far more clearly than ordinary tests, where the tool may happen to be right for the wrong reasons, and where a lucky guess looks like knowledge.

Start with unknown-fact tests. Write questions about your own notes where the answer is not in the notes, such as asking about a meeting that never happened, or a date that was never recorded. The correct output is a refusal, or a statement that the information is not found. If your tool instead invents a meeting, a date, or a detail, you have found a hallucination, in a safe, local setting, before any real user was misled. Record the input, the output, and why it was wrong, so you can fix the underlying cause, such as a missing "not found" check, rather than merely patching that one example.

Next, try detail-change tests. Take a note you know well, change one detail in a copy, such as a date or a name, and ask your tool to summarise the changed copy. The summary should reflect the changed detail, not the original detail it may have memorised or assumed. If the summary keeps the old detail, or invents a third detail, you have found a failure to ground the answer in the given input. Detail-change tests are powerful because they distinguish tools that read their input from tools that guess from patterns, which is exactly the distinction hallucination tests need to make.

Also try source-absence tests. Give your tool a task with no source at all, such as summarising an empty file, or answering from a note that contains only a title. The correct output is to say there is nothing to summarise, or that the information is missing, not to invent a plausible summary from the title alone. Tools that invent summaries for empty inputs are particularly dangerous, because their outputs look complete while being entirely fabricated. Testing empty and minimal inputs is therefore a high-value, low-effort check that every local builder should run.

Finally, keep a hallucination test file, separate from your ordinary test set, containing these temptation cases, with the correct output for each, whether that is a fact, a refusal, or a flag for human review. Run it after every change to your tool, as in Chapter 21, and record the results. A tool that passes its hallucination tests consistently, on fresh temptation cases as well as old ones, is genuinely safer than one that merely scores well on ordinary cases. Temptation testing is the local builder's version of a safety check, and it costs little more than the time to write a few tricky questions.

Section 12: Spotting Invented Quotes and References

Invented quotes and references are among the most convincing hallucinations, because they borrow the authority of a real-sounding source. A quote attributed to a famous person, a book title with a plausible author, or a study with a realistic-sounding journal name can all be entirely invented, yet readers often accept them without checking, because checking a quote or a reference takes effort, and because the quote itself may sound wise or fitting. Builders and readers must therefore treat quotes and references with special suspicion, and check them with special care, before repeating them.

The first check is existence: does the quoted person, book, or study actually exist, and did they actually say or write what is claimed? For a book, check whether a real book with that title and author exists, in a catalogue or a library record you trust. For a study, check whether the journal, year, and authors match a real publication. For a quote, check whether a reliable source attributes those exact words to that person, rather than a similar sentiment in different words. If you cannot find the quote or reference in a trusted source, do not repeat it as fact, however fitting it sounds. A fitting quote that cannot be verified is not evidence; it is decoration, and it should be labelled as unverified or removed.

The second check is wording. Even when a real quote exists, it may be misquoted, shortened in a way that changes its meaning, or taken out of context so that it appears to support a claim it never supported. Compare the quoted words with the original, if you can find it, and ask whether the meaning is preserved, including any qualifiers or conditions that were in the original. A quote that drops a "not," a "sometimes," or a crucial condition has been distorted, even if most words match. Misquotation is a subtler hallucination than outright invention, but it misleads readers in the same way, by borrowing authority for a claim the authority never made.

For local tools, a simple rule helps: do not let a tool invent quotes or references at all. If a summary needs a quote, it should copy it exactly from the source document, with its location noted, rather than generating a paraphrase presented as a direct quote. If a tool cannot find a real quote in its source, it should say so, rather than inventing one to make the output look more authoritative. Builders should test this rule explicitly, by asking their tool for quotes from documents that contain none, and confirming that it refuses or reports absence, rather than fabricating a plausible quotation.

Finally, when you share quotes or references you received from an AI tool, check them yourself before sharing, and keep the check visible if you cannot fully verify. A note like "quote unverified" is honest and protects your readers, while sharing an unverified quote as fact passes the hallucination onward with your credibility attached. In a world where invented references circulate quickly, a builder who checks quotes, and who refuses to share unverified ones, is doing a small but real service to everyone who reads their work.

Section 13: When Fluency Fools Readers

Fluency is how smooth, natural, and easy to read a piece of text is. It is a valuable quality in writing, but it is a poor guide to truth, because language models are specifically good at producing fluent text, even when the facts are wrong. A fluent paragraph, with varied sentences, appropriate transitions, and confident tone, feels true to readers, because in human writing, fluency often correlates with knowledge: a person who writes smoothly about a topic usually knows it well. With AI outputs, that correlation breaks down, because fluency comes from pattern prediction, not from knowledge, and a model can be fluent about a topic it knows nothing about.

This fluency trap affects even careful readers. Studies and everyday experience both show that readers rate fluent answers as more accurate than disfluent ones, even when the fluent answer is wrong and the disfluent answer is right. The trap is stronger when the reader is tired, rushed, or unfamiliar with the topic, which is exactly when they are most likely to rely on a tool. Builders must therefore design tools and habits that break the fluency-truth link, by making checking easy and by separating style from substance in evaluation. A summary should be judged first on its facts, as in Chapter 21, and only then on its fluency, not the other way round.

One practical defence is to read AI outputs in a deliberately unfluent way: slowly, fact by fact, with a pen or a checklist, rather than smoothly from start to finish. Smooth reading is for enjoyment; checking reading is for verification, and the two feel different. When you check, pause at each claim, ask "says who, based on what?" and look for the source. That pause breaks the spell of fluency, and it reveals inventions that smooth reading glides over. Teach yourself, and your users, to switch into checking mode whenever an output will be acted on, shared, or used for a decision, rather than merely read for interest.

Another defence is to design outputs that are less fluently deceptive. A tool can present facts as a list, with sources beside each one, rather than as a smooth paragraph that blends facts and guesses together. A list makes each claim visible and checkable, while a paragraph hides weak claims among strong ones. A tool can also use plain, cautious wording for uncertain claims, as in Section 3, rather than smooth, confident wording for everything. These design choices may make outputs slightly less beautiful, but they make them far more trustworthy, which is the right trade for factual tools.

Finally, remember that fluency is not the enemy; it is a tool that must be paired with verification. Fluent explanations help learners understand, fluent summaries save time, and fluent drafts give writers a strong starting point. The problem is only when fluency is mistaken for truth. By judging facts first, checking claim by claim, and designing outputs that show their sources and uncertainty, you can enjoy fluency's benefits without falling for its trap. That balance, fluency plus checking, is the mature way to use language tools, for builders and readers alike.

Section 14: Reducing Hallucination With Clear Prompts

For tools that accept instructions, the wording of those instructions, often called a prompt, can greatly affect how much a tool hallucinates. A vague instruction like "tell me about this topic" invites guessing, because it gives the tool no source, no limits, and no guidance about uncertainty. A clear instruction, by contrast, tells the tool what source to use, what to do when the source is missing, and how to express uncertainty, which reduces the need and the opportunity to invent. Prompt design, for local toy tools, is therefore a practical hallucination defence, not just a matter of style.

A clear instruction for a local summariser might say: "Summarise only the note below. Do not add facts, causes, or numbers that are not in the note. If a fact is missing, say it is missing. Use the note's own wording for dates and names." Each part of that instruction targets a known hallucination pattern: adding facts, inventing causes, sharpening numbers, and guessing missing details. By naming those patterns explicitly, the instruction makes them less likely, because the tool's task is now defined as faithful reporting, not as creative completion. Test the instruction on temptation cases, as in Section 11, to see whether it actually reduces inventions, rather than assuming it will.

Another useful instruction pattern is to ask for structure that exposes guessing. For example, ask a tool to list facts first, with their source lines, before writing a summary, or to list what is known and what is unknown separately. That structure forces the tool to separate evidence from invention, at least in its output, which makes checking easier for the reader. If the "unknown" list is empty on a question where evidence is clearly missing, that itself is a warning sign. Structured outputs do not guarantee truth, but they make the boundary between known and guessed visible, which is a major improvement over a smooth paragraph that hides it.

Be careful, however, not to over-promise what prompt wording can do. A clear instruction reduces hallucination; it does not eliminate it. A tool may still misread a source, overgeneralise, or invent despite instructions, especially on hard or ambiguous inputs. That is why prompt design must be paired with checking, retrieval, and evaluation, as in earlier sections, rather than treated as a magic fix. A builder who relies only on prompt wording, without testing or checking, is still vulnerable, because instructions are guidance, not guarantees, and they can be misunderstood or overridden by strong patterns in the tool's training.

Finally, keep your instructions short, specific, and testable. Long, complicated instructions are harder to follow and harder to test, and they may introduce new ambiguities. Write the shortest instruction that clearly states the source, the limits, and the uncertainty behaviour you need, then test it on a variety of cases, including temptation cases, and refine it based on failures. That iterative loop, instruction, test, refine, is the same honest loop as evaluation itself, applied to the instructions that guide your tool. It is how clear prompts become reliable prompts, over time.

Section 15: Grounding Answers in Your Own Notes

Grounding means tying an answer to specific, checkable material, rather than letting it float free as a general claim. For local tools, the most practical grounding material is your own notes and documents, because you wrote them, you know their limits, and you can check them easily. A grounded answer says, in effect: "based on this note, written on this date, the answer is this, and here is the passage." An ungrounded answer says: "the answer is this," with no passage, no date, and no way to check. Grounding does not make an answer automatically true, but it makes it checkable, which is the next best thing, and often the most practical goal.

To ground a local tool, start with a small, curated set of notes, as in Section 6. Each note should have a clear title, a date, and content you trust for the purpose at hand. When the tool answers a question, it should first find the most relevant note, then base its answer on that note's content, quoting or closely paraphrasing it, and naming the note as its source. If no note is relevant, it should say so, rather than answering from general patterns. That workflow, find, quote, name, is simple to implement with standard library Python, using word overlap for finding, and it is far safer than answering from memory alone.

Grounding also requires honesty about what a note does and does not say. A note that says "meeting planned for Monday" does not prove the meeting happened on Monday; it proves it was planned. A grounded answer should preserve that distinction, saying "the note says the meeting was planned for Monday," rather than upgrading it to "the meeting was on Monday." That preservation of wording, especially for plans, suggestions, and possibilities, prevents a common grounding failure: accurately quoting a source while silently upgrading its certainty. When you check grounded answers, check not only the facts, but also the certainty words, against the source.

Another grounding practice is to keep the source visible in the output, not hidden in a footnote that readers skip. Show the relevant passage, or at least a short quote, alongside the answer, so that checking takes one glance rather than a search. Readers who can see the passage will spot misreadings quickly, while readers who only see a conclusion must trust blindly. Visibility is therefore part of grounding, not decoration: a grounded answer whose source is invisible is only slightly better than an ungrounded one, because its checkability exists in theory but not in practice.

Finally, ground your own sharing in the same way. When you pass on an answer from your tool, keep its source attached, and note any limits, such as "based on my notes up to last month." That habit protects your readers, and it also protects you, because it makes clear what you actually verified and what you merely received. Grounding, practised consistently from tool to builder to reader, creates a chain of checkability that greatly reduces the harm hallucinations can cause, even though it cannot eliminate hallucinations entirely.

Section 16: Verifying Step by Step

Complex answers are built from steps, and hallucinations can enter at any step, then propagate to the final answer. A multi-step tool might retrieve the wrong document, misread a date, calculate incorrectly, and then present a confident final answer based on that chain of errors. Verifying only the final answer may catch the error, but it will not tell you where it entered, and it may miss errors that happen to cancel out or that look plausible in the final form. Verifying step by step, checking each intermediate result against its source or against simple rules, catches errors earlier, when they are easier to fix and less likely to mislead.

Start by making steps visible. A tool that shows its steps, such as which document it retrieved, which facts it extracted, and what calculation it performed, gives checkers something to verify at each stage. A tool that hides its steps behind a single smooth answer gives checkers only the final claim, which is much harder to audit. For local tools, showing steps can be as simple as printing intermediate results, with labels, before printing the final answer. That transparency costs little, and it transforms debugging from guesswork into inspection, because you can see exactly where a chain went wrong.

Next, check each step with a rule appropriate to that step. Retrieval steps are checked by relevance: did the retrieved passage actually contain the needed fact? Extraction steps are checked by comparison: does the extracted date, number, or name match the source exactly? Calculation steps are checked by recomputation: does the arithmetic, done by hand or by a simple script, give the same result? Action steps are checked by safety: is the action correct, safe, and reversible if wrong? Each step has its own check, and applying the right check at each step is far more effective than applying one generic check to the whole chain.

Also watch for error accumulation. Small errors in early steps, such as a slightly wrong number, can grow into large errors later, especially if later steps multiply, extrapolate, or generalise from the early result. A step-by-step check that tolerates small errors may therefore miss a chain that ends badly, because each step looked acceptable in isolation. To guard against accumulation, check the final answer against independent evidence where possible, not just against the chain that produced it, and flag chains where uncertainty grows at each step, even if no single step is clearly wrong. Uncertainty that accumulates should be reported, not hidden.

Finally, when you find a step error, fix the step, not just the final answer. Patching a final answer without fixing the step that produced it leaves the error in place for the next task, where it may produce a different wrong final answer. Fixing the step, and adding a test for that step, as in Chapter 21, prevents recurrence across many tasks. Step-by-step verification is therefore not just a checking method; it is a building method, one that produces tools whose failures are localised, understandable, and fixable, rather than mysterious and recurring.

for step in steps:
    print("Check:", step)

Section 17: Hallucination in Lists and Tables

Lists and tables feel authoritative, because they look organised, complete, and precise. That appearance makes their hallucinations especially dangerous, because readers tend to trust a tidy table more than a paragraph, and assume a list is complete when it may miss items, or that every item is real when one may be invented. A list of "top five" items with one invented entry, or a table with one wrong cell, can mislead a reader who checks the overall shape but not each entry, which is how most people read lists and tables: quickly, for the gist, not cell by cell.

One common list hallucination is completion: inventing items to reach a round number, or to match an expected pattern. If a question asks for five examples, a tool may produce five even when only three real examples exist, inventing two plausible ones to fill the list. If a table has a column for a detail that is unknown for some rows, a tool may fill those cells with plausible guesses rather than leaving them blank or marking them unknown. The defence is to prefer incomplete honesty over complete invention: a list of three real items, labelled as three, is far better than a list of five with two inventions, and a table cell marked "unknown" is far better than a guessed value presented as fact.

Another pattern is ordering and attribution error. A list may contain real items, but in the wrong order, with the wrong ranking, or attributed to the wrong person or place. A table may swap two rows, or attach one row's value to another row. These errors are hard to spot because each item alone looks real; only the combination is wrong. Checking lists and tables therefore requires checking each row, each cell, and each ordering claim, against its source, not just checking that the items look familiar. For important lists, check the count: does the source contain that many items, or did the tool invent extras?

Builders can reduce list and table hallucinations by design. Require the tool to list its source for each row, or to mark unknown cells explicitly, rather than filling them. Limit lists to items actually found in the source, and state the count honestly, even if it is not a round number. For rankings, require the tool to state the criterion for ranking, and to show the values it ranked by, so readers can verify the order themselves. These design rules make lists and tables checkable, which is their main defence, because a checkable list invites the cell-by-cell reading that catches inventions.

Readers, too, should adopt a list-checking habit: for any list or table that will be acted on, check at least a sample of entries, especially the least familiar ones, against a trusted source, and check the total count. Invented entries are often the least familiar ones, because they are not real, so a reader's unfamiliarity is itself a signal to check, not a reason to trust. A list that survives a sample check is more trustworthy; a list that fails on one entry should be checked fully, because one invented entry suggests the list was generated by pattern completion, not by careful retrieval, and other entries may also be invented.

Section 18: The Danger of Plausible Advice

Plausible advice is advice that sounds reasonable, follows familiar patterns, and fits the situation described, but is wrong, incomplete, or unsafe for the specific person or situation. AI tools often produce plausible advice, because they generate text that fits the pattern of helpful advice, without knowing the reader's full circumstances, constraints, or risks. A plausible health tip, financial suggestion, or safety instruction can therefore mislead a reader who follows it in good faith, believing that because it sounds like expert advice, it must be expert advice. That gap between sounding expert and being expert is where hallucination causes real harm.

The danger is greater because advice is action-guiding: readers do things based on it, rather than merely learning from it. A wrong fact in a summary may misinform, but a wrong step in advice may cause a reader to take a harmful action, miss a crucial safety step, or waste resources on an ineffective approach. Advice hallucinations therefore deserve stricter checking than general facts, including checking against trusted, specific sources, and, for high-stakes topics, against a qualified human. This book does not teach tools to give high-stakes professional advice, and builders should be especially cautious about tools that appear to do so, even when their outputs sound confident and helpful.

One defence is to keep advice general, cautious, and clearly labelled as general information, not personal advice. A tool can explain how a topic generally works, what factors people usually consider, and where to find qualified help, without prescribing a specific action for a specific reader. That distinction matters: general information helps readers learn and decide for themselves, while specific prescriptions, based on incomplete information about the reader, risk being wrong for that reader in ways the tool cannot foresee. Builders should design tools to stay on the general, educational side of that line, unless they have a clear, safe, and tested reason to cross it, with human review.

Another defence is to include warnings and checks in advice outputs. A tool that gives steps should also list what to check before starting, what to stop and reconsider if something looks wrong, and when to seek human help. Those warnings do not make the advice automatically safe, but they remind readers to exercise judgement, rather than following steps blindly. Readers, in turn, should treat AI advice as a starting point for their own checking, not as a final instruction, especially when the stakes are high, the situation is unusual, or the advice conflicts with what they already know from a trusted source.

Finally, when you share advice produced with AI help, label it as such, and state what you checked. Do not present AI-generated advice as if it were your own expert judgement, unless you have genuinely verified it and are qualified to stand behind it. Passing on plausible advice with your own credibility, without checking, transfers the hallucination risk to your readers, who trust you more than they trust a machine. Honest labelling, cautious wording, and a clear invitation to check, are the minimum for sharing advice safely in a world full of plausible-sounding text.

Section 19: Hallucination Versus Mistake Versus Lie

Hallucination, mistake, and lie are three different things, and confusing them leads to wrong responses. A mistake is a general term for getting something wrong, which can happen for many reasons: a typo, a misreading, a calculation error, or a misunderstanding. A hallucination is a specific kind of mistake, where a system generates fluent, plausible content that is not supported by evidence, because it predicted likely words rather than verifying facts. A lie, by contrast, implies intent to deceive: a speaker who knows the truth and deliberately states something else. Language models, as prediction systems, do not know truth in the human sense, and they do not intend to deceive, so calling their errors lies is misleading, even when the errors are serious.

Why does this distinction matter? Because each kind of error calls for a different fix. A typo is fixed by proofreading. A calculation error is fixed by recomputing. A hallucination is fixed by grounding, checking, and uncertainty design, as this chapter teaches, because its cause is prediction without evidence. A lie, if it were one, would be fixed by changing motives or incentives, which does not apply to a prediction system. Treating hallucinations as lies leads builders to blame the tool's character, rather than fixing its task design, which wastes time and misses the practical defences that actually work.

The distinction also matters for how we talk about AI outputs to users. Saying "the tool lied" suggests deliberate deception, which can make users distrust the tool entirely, or, paradoxically, make them treat its errors as rare and personal, rather than as systematic risks to manage. Saying "the tool hallucinated, because it guessed from patterns without a source" is more accurate, and it points users toward the right response: check against a source, ask for citations, and treat confident wording with caution. Accurate language about errors helps users calibrate trust correctly, neither over-trusting nor over-distrusting, but checking appropriately.

For human builders, the distinction matters too. When you share an AI output without checking it, and it contains a hallucination, you have not lied, because you did not intend to deceive, but you have still misled your readers, because you passed on false information with your credibility. The remedy is not to feel guilty about intent, but to take responsibility for checking: verify before sharing, label what you have not checked, and correct errors promptly when they are found. Responsibility for sharing is about care and checking, not about whether you personally invented the error.

Finally, keep the distinction in mind when you evaluate tools. Do not score a tool on whether it "lies," which is not a measurable property of a prediction system. Score it on measurable properties: how often it hallucinates, on which kinds of claims, with what confidence wording, and whether its defences, such as retrieval, citations, and refusals, reduce the rate. Those measurements, as in Chapter 21, give you actionable information, while moral language about lying gives you only a feeling. Clear, measurable language about errors is itself a hallucination defence, because it keeps builders focused on fixes that work.

Section 20: Teaching Users to Check

Builders cannot check every output for every user, forever. The lasting defence against hallucination is therefore to teach users to check, so that checking becomes a normal part of using AI tools, not an extra burden imposed only by experts. Teaching users to check starts with honesty about the tool's limits, in plain language, at the point of use: what the tool does well, where it is likely to hallucinate, and what kinds of claims deserve extra checking. A user who knows that dates and names are the most likely inventions, as in Section 4, will check those first, which catches the most harmful errors quickly.

Teach checking as a short, memorable routine, not as a long lecture. A routine like "source, date, number" reminds users to ask, for any important claim: what is the source, is the date right, is the number right? Or a routine like "one claim, one check" reminds users to verify the single most important claim before acting, even if they cannot check everything. Short routines are more likely to be used than comprehensive checklists, especially under time pressure, and a routine that is actually used beats a perfect checklist that is ignored. Give users one routine, practise it with examples, and repeat it in the tool's own output, such as a footer that says "please check dates and names before sharing."

Show users examples of hallucinations, with the invented parts highlighted, so they can recognise the pattern. An example where a summary invents a cause, or a list invents a fifth item, teaches more than a warning that "AI can make mistakes," because it shows what mistakes actually look like. Use your own local examples, from your own notes, so the lesson feels concrete rather than abstract. When users have seen a hallucination, with its smooth wording and plausible detail, they are far less likely to be fooled by the next one, because they know that smoothness is not evidence.

Also teach users when checking matters most. Not every output needs the same level of checking: a brainstorming list for personal use needs less checking than a summary that will be shared, or an instruction that will be followed for an important task. Help users calibrate effort to stakes: check the key claim before acting, check everything before sharing, and check safety-critical claims with a qualified human or a trusted source, not just with another AI output. That calibration prevents both under-checking, which lets hallucinations through, and over-checking, which wastes time and leads users to abandon checking altogether.

Finally, model checking yourself. When you share AI outputs, show your checks: note what you verified, what remains unchecked, and what you corrected. Users learn more from seeing a builder check, than from being told to check. A builder who shares a corrected output, with a note like "the tool originally gave the wrong date; I checked it against the original and fixed it," teaches users that checking is normal, expected, and valued, not a sign of distrust or failure. That culture of checking, built one shared example at a time, is the most durable hallucination defence of all.

Section 21: Building a Fact Checker Toy

A fact checker toy is a small Python program that compares an output with a source, and flags claims that do not appear in the source. It will not catch every hallucination, but building one teaches you exactly what automatic checking can do, and where human judgement must take over. It also gives you a practical tool you can run on your own summaries, before sharing them, which is far more useful than a theoretical understanding of checking alone. Build it with only the standard library, on your own files, in a few dozen lines.

Start with the simplest version: extract dates and numbers from both source and output, using simple patterns, and report any date or number in the output that is not in the source. Dates might be written as four-digit years, or as day-month-year strings, depending on your notes. Numbers might be integers or decimals. Your checker does not need to understand what the dates or numbers mean; it only needs to notice that an output detail has no counterpart in the source, which is a strong signal of invention, or at least of a detail that deserves human review. Test it on a summary you know contains an invented date, and confirm that it flags that date.

Next, add name checking. Extract capitalised words, or words from a list of names you know appear in your notes, and flag output names that do not appear in the source. This check will produce some false alarms, for example when a summary uses a pronoun instead of a name, or when a name is spelled slightly differently, but false alarms are acceptable in a checker, because its job is to draw attention to possible problems, not to give a final verdict. A checker that flags ten possible problems, of which two are real hallucinations, is still useful, if the user reviews the flags rather than blindly accepting or rejecting them.

Then add a simple claim list. Break the output into sentences, and for each sentence, check whether its key words overlap with the source. Sentences with very low overlap may be invented, or may be paraphrases that use different words for the same meaning, so they need human reading, not automatic rejection. Print each sentence with its overlap score, so the user can read the lowest-scoring sentences first, which is an efficient way to focus human checking where it is most needed. That combination, automatic flagging plus human reading of flagged items, is the practical model for most real checking workflows.

Finally, keep the toy honest about its limits. Document, in a comment at the top of the file, what it checks, dates, numbers, names, and overlap, and what it does not check, such as paraphrased claims, invented causes, or misordered events. A checker that honestly states its limits will be used correctly, as a helper for human checking. A checker that claims to detect all hallucinations will be over-trusted, and its misses will be mistaken for proof of correctness. Building a modest, honest checker is therefore not just a coding exercise; it is an exercise in the same honesty this chapter teaches throughout.

import re
source = "Meeting on 2024-05-01 with Ada."
output = "Meeting on 2024-05-02 with Ada."
dates_out = re.findall(r"\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}", output)
print("Output dates not in source:", [d for d in dates_out if d not in source])

Section 22: Comparing Claim to Source in Python

Comparing a claim to a source in Python starts with representing both as text you can search, split, and compare. The source is usually a longer text, such as a note or a document, while the claim is usually a sentence or a short statement. The simplest comparison is containment: does the claim's key detail, such as a date or a name, appear in the source? A more flexible comparison is word overlap: what fraction of the claim's important words appear in the source? Neither method understands meaning, but both give a useful first signal, especially when combined with human reading of low-scoring claims, as in Section 21.

To implement word overlap, first normalise both texts: convert to lower case, remove punctuation, and split into words. Then remove very common words, such as "the," "and," and "is," which appear everywhere and tell you nothing about whether a specific claim is supported. What remains are content words, which carry the claim's meaning. Compute the fraction of the claim's content words that appear in the source. A high fraction suggests the claim is grounded, though it does not prove it, because words can appear in different combinations with different meanings. A low fraction suggests the claim may be invented, or heavily paraphrased, and deserves human review.

Be careful about negation and order, which word overlap ignores. A claim that says "the meeting was not on Monday" shares most words with a source that says "the meeting was on Monday," yet means the opposite. A claim that swaps two names, or two dates, may also score highly on overlap while being wrong. That is why overlap is a flagging tool, not a verdict tool: it tells you where to look, not what to conclude. When you read a flagged claim, check negation words like "not," "never," and "no," check which name goes with which action, and check which date goes with which event, because those are the details overlap misses.

You can also compare claims at the sentence level. Split the output into sentences, and for each sentence, find the source sentence with the highest overlap. Print the pair side by side, so a human can judge whether the output sentence faithfully reflects the source sentence, or whether it adds, drops, or changes an important detail. That side-by-side presentation is often more useful than a single score, because it lets the human see the comparison directly, rather than trusting a number. For local tools, a simple script that prints these pairs is a powerful checking aid, and it runs in seconds on your own files.

Finally, test your comparison script on known cases: a faithful summary, a summary with an invented date, a summary with a negated claim, and a summary that paraphrases correctly with different words. Record how the script scores each case, and note where it succeeds and where it fails. That testing, as in Chapter 21, teaches you the script's limits, and it prevents you from over-trusting it. A comparison tool whose limits are known and documented is a trustworthy helper. A comparison tool whose limits are unknown is a new source of false confidence, which is exactly what hallucination defences are meant to prevent.

def overlap(claim, source):
    claim_words = set(claim.lower().replace(".", "").split())
    source_words = set(source.lower().replace(".", "").split())
    return len(claim_words & source_words) / max(1, len(claim_words))

Section 23: Handling Uncertain Dates and Numbers

Uncertain dates and numbers are a major source of hallucination, because tools often convert uncertainty into false precision. An original note might say "around March," "about fifty people," or "next week," and a summary might report "15 March," "fifty people," or a specific date, as if the uncertainty had been resolved. That conversion feels like improvement, because specific sounds more useful than vague, but it is invention, and it misleads readers who act on the specific value, believing it to be exact. Handling uncertainty honestly means preserving it, not resolving it by guessing.

The first rule is to preserve the original's wording for uncertain values. If the source says "around March," the output should say "around March," not a specific day. If the source says "about fifty," the output should say "about fifty," not "fifty." If the source says "next Monday" without a reference date, the output should either keep "next Monday" with a note that the reference date is unclear, or say that the exact date cannot be determined from the source. Preserving uncertainty may feel less helpful in the short term, but it is far more helpful in the long term, because it prevents decisions based on false precision, which are worse than decisions made with acknowledged uncertainty.

The second rule is to distinguish estimates from measurements. An estimate, such as "about half," is a judgement, with a range of plausible values. A measurement, such as "48 out of 96," is a count, with a specific value. When you summarise, do not present an estimate as a measurement, and do not present a measurement as an estimate, because each implies a different level of confidence. If a source gives a range, such as "between forty and sixty," preserve the range, rather than picking its midpoint as if it were the value. Ranges are honest; midpoints presented as exact values are inventions disguised as summaries.

For local tools, you can add simple uncertainty handling. When your tool encounters words like "about," "around," "roughly," "approximately," or "maybe" in a source, it can keep those words attached to the value in its output, rather than dropping them for brevity. When it encounters a relative date, it can keep it relative, or flag it for human conversion, rather than converting it automatically without a reference date. These small rules are easy to implement with word lists, and they prevent a whole class of hallucinations that arise from dropping qualifiers during summarisation.

Finally, when you share outputs containing uncertain values, make the uncertainty visible to readers, not hidden in a footnote. A summary that says "around March (exact date not stated in the note)" gives readers the information they need to decide how much to rely on it. A summary that says "15 March" gives them false confidence. Honest uncertainty handling is therefore not just a technical detail; it is a way of respecting readers, by giving them the same level of knowledge the source actually provides, neither more nor less, so they can make decisions with eyes open.

Section 24: Avoiding Hallucination in Summaries

Summaries hallucinate most when the summariser is asked to do too much at once: to shorten, to clarify, to complete, and to beautify, all in one step. Each of those tasks, on its own, is reasonable, but together they create pressure to invent, because completing and beautifying often require adding details the source does not contain. A safer approach is to separate the tasks: first extract key facts, then shorten, then check, with each step's output visible and checkable. That separation, like step-by-step verification in Section 16, reduces the opportunity for invention to hide inside a single smooth paragraph.

Start summarising by listing facts, not by writing prose. Read the source, write down its key facts as a short list, each with its source location, and check the list against the source before you write any summary sentences. That fact list becomes your ground truth for the summary: every sentence in the summary must correspond to a fact on the list, and any sentence that does not must be removed or labelled as an addition. Fact-first summarising is slower than writing directly, but it is far safer, and for important summaries, it is the only method that reliably prevents invented details, because it makes invention visible as a sentence without a corresponding fact.

Next, write the summary from the fact list, not from memory of the source. Use the list's wording for dates, numbers, and names, and preserve qualifiers like "about" and "suggests," as in Section 23. Keep the summary short, but do not shorten by merging facts that the source kept separate, as in Section 9, because merging invents connections. If two facts are separate in the source, keep them separate in the summary, even if that makes the summary slightly longer. Length saved by merging is not worth meaning lost by merging, especially when readers will act on the summary without reading the original.

Then check the summary against the source, fact by fact, using the methods of Sections 5 and 21. Flag any detail in the summary that is not on the fact list, and either find it in the source, with its location, or remove it. Check dates, numbers, and names first, as in Section 4, because they are the most common inventions. Check causes and connections second, because they are the most subtle inventions. That checking step, done by you or by a simple script plus human reading, is what turns a draft summary into a trustworthy summary, and it should never be skipped for summaries that will be shared.

Finally, label the summary honestly. Note its source, its date, and any limits, such as "based on the note alone; the full report was not summarised." That labelling helps readers judge how much to trust the summary, and it protects you if the source itself was incomplete or outdated. A summary labelled with its source and limits is a useful, honest tool. A summary presented as a complete, authoritative account, without source or limits, invites over-trust, even if it happens to be accurate, because readers cannot tell, from the summary alone, how it was made or what it left out.

Section 25: Questions That Invite Guessing

Some questions invite guessing more than others, because they assume facts that are not true, ask for information that does not exist, or demand a specific answer where only a range or a refusal is honest. A question like "what was the fifth reason?" assumes there were five reasons, when there may have been three. A question like "when did the meeting that never happened take place?" assumes a meeting that did not occur. A tool that answers such questions directly, without questioning their assumptions, will hallucinate, because it must invent to satisfy a false premise. Recognising loaded and false-premise questions is therefore a key hallucination defence, for builders and readers alike.

The first defence is to check the question's assumptions before answering. Does the question assume a fact, a count, an event, or a cause that the source does not support? If so, the honest answer is to question the assumption, not to invent an answer that fits it. For example, if asked for the fifth reason when only three are documented, the honest answer is "only three reasons are documented; there is no fifth in the source." If asked about a meeting that never happened, the honest answer is "no such meeting is recorded." Those answers may feel less helpful than a fluent invention, but they correct a false premise rather than building on it.

Builders can design tools to handle false-premise questions explicitly. A retrieval tool that finds only three reasons should report three, with a note that no fourth or fifth was found, rather than inventing extras to reach the requested number. A tool that finds no record of an event should say so, rather than describing the event as if it happened. Testing tools with false-premise questions, as part of temptation testing in Section 11, reveals whether they question assumptions or merely comply with them. Tools that comply blindly are dangerous, because users often ask loaded questions unknowingly, and will trust a fluent answer as confirmation.

Readers, too, should learn to spot loaded questions in their own asking. Before you ask a tool a question, ask yourself: am I assuming something I have not verified? Am I asking for a specific number, date, or reason that may not exist? If so, phrase the question more openly: instead of "what was the fifth reason?" ask "how many reasons are documented, and what are they?" Open questions invite honest answers, while loaded questions invite inventions, because they narrow the acceptable answer to a form that may not match reality. Rephrasing a question openly is a simple, powerful habit that reduces hallucination at its source: the question itself.

Finally, when you receive an answer to a loaded question, check whether the answer questioned the premise or merely complied with it. An answer that begins "actually, only three reasons are documented" has done valuable work, by correcting you. An answer that smoothly lists five reasons, when only three exist, has misled you, however helpful it felt. Learning to value premise-correcting answers, and to distrust smooth compliance with false premises, is a hallmark of a careful reader, and it is a skill worth practising every time you use an AI tool for factual questions.

Section 26: Short Answers, Fewer Inventions

Short answers hallucinate less, on average, than long answers, for a simple reason: every additional sentence is another opportunity to invent a detail, add a cause, or sharpen a number. A one-sentence answer that states a single fact, with its source, has only one claim to check, and one place where invention can hide. A ten-paragraph answer has dozens of claims, many of which readers will not check, because checking a long answer takes time and effort that most readers do not have. Length therefore increases hallucination risk, not just by adding more text, but by overwhelming the checking capacity of readers.

That does not mean short answers are always better. A short answer can be incomplete, misleading by omission, or too vague to act on. A question that genuinely requires explanation, steps, or context cannot be answered well in one sentence, and forcing brevity may produce an answer that is technically true but practically useless. The principle is not "always be short," but "be as short as the task allows, without omitting facts the reader needs." For factual questions, that usually means a direct answer, plus its source, plus any crucial qualifier, and nothing more. Extra elaboration, background, and examples should be added only when the reader needs them, and each addition should be checked as carefully as the main answer.

Builders can use length as a hallucination control. A local tool can be instructed to answer in a limited number of sentences, or to list facts rather than writing prose, when the task is factual. That constraint reduces the surface area for invention, and it makes checking easier, because a short, listed answer can be verified claim by claim in seconds. For tasks where elaboration is needed, such as teaching a concept, the tool can separate facts from explanation, labelling which parts are factual claims with sources, and which parts are illustrative examples or general guidance. That separation helps readers check the factual parts strictly, while enjoying the explanatory parts more loosely.

Readers can also use length as a warning signal. When an answer is much longer than the question requires, ask why: is the extra length adding genuine value, or is it padding that may contain inventions? Long answers that repeat the same point in different words, add unnecessary background, or list many examples where one would do, deserve extra checking, because padding is where hallucinations often hide, unnoticed among correct material. A reader who checks long answers by sampling, reading a few claims carefully rather than skimming all of them, will catch more inventions than a reader who skims the whole answer for its gist.

Finally, when you write or share AI outputs, prefer the shortest honest version that serves the reader's purpose, and say when you have shortened. A short, checked answer is more trustworthy than a long, unchecked one, even if the long one looks more impressive. In a world where AI tools can produce unlimited fluent text at no cost, brevity combined with checking is a mark of care, not laziness. It shows that the builder valued the reader's time and trust enough to give them only what was needed, and only what could be verified, rather than flooding them with plausible text they cannot realistically check.

Section 27: Long Answers and Drift

Long answers suffer from drift: as an answer gets longer, it gradually moves away from the question, the source, or the facts, even if it starts correctly. Early sentences may be grounded and accurate, but later sentences, generated to continue the pattern, may introduce new details, shift emphasis, or draw conclusions the source does not support. Drift is subtle, because each step away from the source is small, and the overall text still reads smoothly, but by the end, the answer may claim something quite different from what it started with, or from what the source actually says.

Drift happens for the same reason as other hallucinations: prediction. Each new sentence is predicted from the sentences before it, not re-checked against the original source. As the chain grows longer, the influence of the original source weakens, and the influence of the generated text itself strengthens, so the answer begins to follow its own internal logic rather than the source's facts. That is why long, free-form answers are riskier than short, structured ones: they give prediction more steps in which to drift, with fewer opportunities for the source to correct the course. Breaking a long answer into sections, each tied to a source passage, reduces drift, because each section is re-anchored to its source.

Another form is topic drift, where the answer shifts to a related but different topic, because related topics share vocabulary. A question about one event may drift into a discussion of a similar event, with dates and names from the second event mixed into the first. A summary of one document may drift into general knowledge about its topic, adding facts that are true in general but not in that document. Topic drift is hard to spot, because the drifted material is often true in itself, just not true of the specific subject asked about. Checking against the specific source, rather than against general knowledge, is the defence, because general truth is not the same as source truth.

Builders can reduce drift by structuring long outputs. Instead of one long paragraph, use headings, lists, or sections, each with its own source or fact list, as in Section 24. Require the tool to return to the source at each section, rather than continuing from its own previous text alone. Limit the length of each section, and check each section separately, before combining them into a final answer. These structural choices do not eliminate drift, but they compartmentalise it: an error in one section does not automatically infect the next, because the next section starts fresh from its source, rather than from the previous section's drifted text.

Readers, too, should check long answers section by section, rather than judging them as a whole. Read the first section against its source, then the second, and so on, and watch for the point where the answer starts to add details, shift topics, or draw conclusions not supported by the source. That point, where drift begins, is often more informative than the final answer, because it shows where the tool stopped reporting and started inventing. A long answer that stays grounded section by section is trustworthy; a long answer that drifts, however smoothly, is not, and it should be shortened, corrected, or discarded accordingly.

Section 28: Repeating and Snowballing Errors

Errors in AI outputs can snowball: a small invented detail, once introduced, can be repeated, elaborated, and built upon, until it becomes a central part of an answer that started mostly correct. For example, an invented date in an early sentence may be used later to calculate a duration, to order events, or to attribute a cause, so that one hallucination generates several more, each looking more solid because it follows logically from the first. Snowballing is dangerous because it makes a single error look like multiple independent facts, when they are really one error repeated in different forms, and because correcting the final answer without correcting the first error leaves the snowball intact for the next task.

Snowballing also happens across outputs, not just within one. If a hallucinated fact is copied into a summary, then that summary is used as a source for a later summary, the invented fact becomes part of the new source, and later tools will treat it as true, because it appears in a document they trust. That chain, from invention to copy to trusted source, is how hallucinations spread and harden into apparent facts, especially in collections of notes or documents that are reused over time. Builders must therefore be careful about what they save as source material: a summary containing an unchecked hallucination should not be filed as a trusted note, or it will poison future retrieval and summarisation.

The defence against snowballing is to catch errors early, at their first appearance, before they are repeated or built upon. That means checking outputs before they are saved, shared, or used as inputs to another step, as in Sections 5 and 16. It also means correcting the original error, not just its later consequences: if a date is wrong, fix the date in the source output, and check all later statements that used that date, because they may also be wrong, even if they look logically consistent. A correction that only fixes the final conclusion, while leaving the wrong premise in place, will fail the next time that premise is used.

Another defence is to keep sources separate from outputs. Your trusted notes, which tools search over, should be kept in a different folder from generated summaries and drafts, so that generated material does not accidentally become retrieval material. If a generated summary proves accurate after checking, you may choose to save it as a note, but that choice should be deliberate, with a note saying it was checked, rather than automatic. That separation prevents the most common snowball: a plausible generated summary, saved carelessly, becoming the source for the next generation, which then treats its inventions as facts.

Finally, when you discover a snowballed error, correct it transparently, wherever it has spread, as far as you can. Tell readers who received the wrong version, correct the saved copies, and note the correction, so that future readers do not encounter the old, wrong version without warning. Transparent correction is not embarrassing; it is how trust is maintained when errors inevitably occur. A builder who corrects snowballed errors openly, and who prevents them by checking early and separating sources from outputs, will produce tools and collections that stay trustworthy over time, rather than gradually filling with confident, repeated inventions.

Section 29: When the Source Itself Is Wrong

Hallucination defences usually assume the source is correct, and the tool's job is to report it faithfully. But sources can be wrong, outdated, or misleading, and a tool that faithfully copies a wrong source will still mislead readers, even though it did not hallucinate in the strict sense. That distinction matters for builders, because fixing a faithful-but-wrong output requires fixing or flagging the source, not just improving the tool's copying. A tool that copies perfectly is not automatically a trustworthy tool, if its sources are untrustworthy, and evaluation must therefore check sources as well as outputs.

Sources go wrong in several ways. They may be outdated, describing a situation that has since changed. They may be mistaken, containing a typo, a wrong date, or a confused name. They may be biased, presenting one side of a story as if it were the whole story. They may be drafts, guesses, or notes to self, never intended as authoritative records, but later treated as such because they were saved alongside trusted notes. Each kind of source error calls for a different response: updating, correcting, balancing, or labelling the source as a draft, rather than treating it as fact. Builders should therefore curate their source collections, as in Section 6, removing or labelling outdated and draft material, so tools do not present it as current truth.

When a tool's output is checked against its source and matches, but the source itself is suspect, the honest response is to flag the source, not to praise the tool. A summary that faithfully reports a wrong date from a mistaken note is still wrong for the reader, and sharing it without a warning passes the source's error onward with new credibility. Instead, note the uncertainty: "the note says this date, but it may be outdated; please check the current record." That note preserves faithfulness to the source, while warning readers not to over-trust it. Faithfulness and truth are related, but they are not the same, and honest builders distinguish them explicitly.

Readers, too, should ask about sources, not just about outputs. When you receive a grounded answer, ask: is the source trustworthy for this claim? Is it current? Is it the right kind of source for this kind of claim, such as an official record for a date, rather than a casual note? If the source is weak, treat the answer as weak, however faithfully it was copied, and seek a stronger source before acting. That habit, checking the source's quality as well as the output's fidelity, is the second layer of defence against misleading information, after the first layer of checking output against source.

Finally, when you discover a source error, correct the source, not just the output, where you can, and note the correction, so future tools and readers do not repeat the error. If you cannot correct the source, because it belongs to someone else or is a historical document, label it as containing a known error, rather than silently using it as if it were correct. Source hygiene, correcting, updating, and labelling sources, is unglamorous work, but it is essential for any tool that grounds its answers in documents, because the quality of grounded answers cannot exceed the quality of their grounds.

Section 30: Confidence Scores and Warnings

Confidence scores are numbers or labels that say how sure a tool is about its answer, such as "high confidence," "medium," or "eighty percent." Warnings are messages that alert users to specific risks, such as "dates not verified" or "source is outdated." Both are useful hallucination defences, but only if they are honest and well-designed. A confidence score that is usually high, even when answers are wrong, teaches users to ignore it, creating a false sense of safety. A warning that appears on every output, regardless of risk, teaches users to ignore warnings, which defeats their purpose. Designing honest scores and warnings is therefore a careful task, not a decorative one.

For local tools, confidence can be based on simple, checkable signals, not a mysterious internal feeling. A retrieval tool can report how closely the retrieved passage matched the question, using word overlap, as in Section 22. A summariser can report how many of its output dates and names were found in the source, as in Section 21. A tool can report whether its source was recent, whether it found multiple supporting passages, or whether it had to guess for any part of the answer. Those signals are transparent: users can understand what they mean, and builders can test whether they actually predict correctness, by comparing scores with accuracy on a test set, as in Chapter 21's calibration discussion.

Warnings should be specific and rare enough to be noticed. Instead of a generic "AI can make mistakes" on every output, which users quickly learn to ignore, use specific warnings where they matter: "this summary contains a date that was not found in the source; please check it," or "no source was found for this claim." Specific warnings point users to exactly what to check, which makes checking efficient and effective. Generic warnings, by contrast, tell users to check everything, which is impractical, so users check nothing. Specificity is therefore not just better writing; it is better safety design, because it directs limited checking effort to the highest-risk claims.

Also test whether users actually notice and use scores and warnings. Show a tool's output to a friend, ask what they would check before acting, and see whether the score or warning changed their behaviour. If users ignore a warning, it may need to be clearer, placed earlier, or worded more directly. If users over-trust a high confidence score, the score may need to be recalibrated, or replaced with a more cautious label. User testing, as in Chapter 21, is the way to learn whether a safety feature actually works in practice, rather than merely looking good in design.

Finally, never use confidence scores or warnings as a substitute for fixing hallucinations. A tool that hallucinates frequently, but warns about it, is still a poor tool, and its warnings will soon be ignored if they appear too often. Scores and warnings are supplements to grounding, checking, and good design, not replacements for them. The goal is a tool that hallucinates rarely, and that honestly signals its remaining uncertainty, so users can check the few cases that matter. A tool that achieves both, rare errors plus honest signals, is genuinely trustworthy, and its scores and warnings will be believed, because they are usually reassuring and occasionally, usefully, alarming.

Section 31: A Hallucination Test Set

A hallucination test set is a collection of cases designed specifically to tempt guessing, as introduced in Section 11, organised so you can run it regularly and track whether your tool is getting safer. Unlike an ordinary test set, which measures how often a tool is right on typical tasks, a hallucination test set measures how often it invents when it should refuse, flag, or stay faithful. Building such a set is one of the most valuable safety investments a local builder can make, because it turns hallucination from a vague worry into a measurable, trackable property of your tool.

Build the set in categories, so you can see which kinds of temptation your tool handles well and which it fails. Useful categories include: questions with no answer in the source, questions with a false premise, summaries of empty or minimal inputs, requests for a specific number of items when fewer exist, requests for quotes from documents containing none, and detail-change cases where a source detail was altered. For each case, write the input, the source, and the correct output, which is often a refusal, a partial list, or a faithful summary, rather than a single fact. That correct output is your gold answer for safety, and it should be written before you run the tool, as with any gold answer.

Include both easy and hard temptations. Easy temptations, such as asking about a completely unrelated topic, should be refused by any reasonable tool, and failing them signals a serious problem. Hard temptations, such as a false premise buried in a long, plausible question, or a detail change in a long document, will challenge even careful tools, and failing them is informative rather than damning, because it shows where to improve next. A test set with only easy cases will flatter your tool; a set with only hard cases will discourage you. A mix, labelled by difficulty, gives a realistic picture of safety, and lets you track progress on hard cases over time without losing sight of easy ones.

Run the hallucination test set separately from your ordinary evaluation, and report its results separately, as a safety score, not blended into an overall accuracy score. For example, report "passed 18 of 20 hallucination tests; failures were inventing a fifth item and inventing a quote." That separate reporting, as in Chapter 21's safety evaluation, prevents a high ordinary score from hiding safety failures, and it makes safety progress visible: when you fix a temptation failure, the safety score rises, even if the ordinary score stays the same. Safety progress deserves its own celebration, because it protects users directly.

Finally, grow the hallucination test set whenever you discover a new failure in real use. Each real hallucination, once found, should become a test case, so it cannot recur unnoticed. That habit, turning failures into tests, is the core of regression testing for safety: it ensures that fixes stay fixed, and that new changes do not reintroduce old inventions. Over time, your hallucination test set becomes a record of everything your tool has learned not to invent, which is a valuable asset, and a clear demonstration that safety was tested, not merely asserted.

Section 32: Reading Outputs Like a Detective

Reading like a detective means reading AI outputs with suspicion, curiosity, and a checklist, rather than reading for enjoyment or gist. A detective does not accept a story because it sounds coherent; they check alibis, timelines, and details, looking for inconsistencies that reveal invention. A careful reader of AI outputs does the same: they check dates against timelines, names against sources, numbers against calculations, and causes against evidence, looking for the small inconsistency that reveals a hallucination hiding among true facts. That mindset, more than any tool, is the ultimate defence, because it applies to every output, however it was produced.

Start detective reading by listing the output's claims, as in Section 5, before you judge whether it feels right. Write each claim on its own line, in your own words if needed, so that blended sentences become separate, checkable statements. A paragraph that felt convincing as a whole often looks weaker as a list, because its claims, separated, reveal gaps: a cause with no evidence, a date with no source, a number with no calculation. Listing claims is therefore a powerful first step, and it takes only a few minutes for a short output, while saving far more time than it costs if it catches a harmful invention before you act on it.

Next, check the claims that are easiest to verify and most consequential if wrong, as in Section 4: dates, numbers, names, and any claim that will guide an action or a decision. For each, find its source, or note that no source was given, and mark it as verified, unverified, or contradicted. A claim marked unverified is not necessarily false, but it should not be acted on as if it were true, until it is checked. A claim marked contradicted should be discarded, and should prompt a full re-check of the output, because one contradiction suggests the output was generated by pattern completion rather than careful reporting, and other claims may also be invented.

Also look for internal inconsistencies within the output itself. Does it give two different dates for the same event? Does it name two different people as doing the same thing? Does it claim a result in one sentence and a different result later? Internal inconsistency is a strong signal of hallucination, because a faithful report of a single source rarely contradicts itself, while a generated text, produced sentence by sentence without re-checking, often does. Spotting an internal contradiction is therefore a quick win: it tells you, without any external source, that at least part of the output cannot be trusted, and that the whole output deserves careful checking.

Finally, practise detective reading on outputs you know are correct, as well as on those you suspect, so you learn what a well-supported output looks like, not just what a hallucinated one looks like. A well-supported output has sources, qualifiers, and consistent details, and its claims can be traced back to evidence. A hallucinated output, by contrast, often has smooth prose, confident wording, and vague or missing sources. Learning to recognise both patterns, by practising on real examples, will make detective reading faster and more reliable over time, until it becomes a habit you apply automatically to any output that matters.

Section 33: Fixing Invented Facts Without Hiding Them

When you find an invented fact in an output, the temptation is to quietly fix it and move on, as if it never happened. That quiet fix may correct the immediate output, but it hides valuable information: that the tool hallucinated, on what kind of claim, and why. Fixing without recording means the same hallucination is likely to recur, because its cause was never investigated. Honest fixing therefore has two parts: correcting the output, and recording the error, its cause, and the fix, so that both the tool and its readers can learn from it.

Start by documenting the error, in plain language, before you fix it: what was claimed, what the source actually says, and what kind of hallucination it was, such as invented date, invented cause, or invented item in a list. That documentation, kept in your evaluation log, as in Chapter 21, turns a single error into a test case for the future, as in Section 31. Add the case to your hallucination test set, with the correct output, so that future versions of your tool are tested against it. An error that becomes a test case is an error that is unlikely to recur unnoticed, because it will be caught by the next evaluation run.

Next, fix the underlying cause, not just the single output. If a summariser invented a cause, the cause may be a missing instruction, such as "do not add causes not in the source," or a missing check, such as flagging added words. If a list tool invented a fifth item, the cause may be a missing rule, such as "list only items found in the source." Fix the rule, the instruction, or the check, and then re-test on the original case and on fresh cases of the same kind, to confirm the fix generalises. A fix that only corrects one output, without changing the tool, is not a fix; it is a patch that will peel off the next time a similar input appears.

Then, if the wrong output was already shared, correct it where it was shared, with a clear note that it was corrected and why. Readers who acted on the wrong version deserve to know, and readers who encounter the corrected version deserve to know that an earlier version was wrong, so they do not trust a cached or copied old version. Correction notes need not be long or apologetic; a simple "corrected: the date was originally given as X; the source says Y" is enough. That transparency builds trust, because it shows that errors are found and fixed, rather than hidden, and it models the same honesty you ask of your tool.

Finally, review your fixes periodically, looking for patterns. If many fixes involve invented dates, your date handling needs redesign, not another patch. If many involve invented causes, your summarising instructions need rewriting. Pattern review, as in Chapter 21's error analysis, turns individual fixes into systematic improvements, which is how a tool becomes steadily safer over time. Fixing invented facts honestly, with documentation, cause-level fixes, transparent corrections, and pattern review, is slower than quietly editing one output, but it is the only approach that actually reduces hallucination over time, rather than merely hiding it for a day.

Section 34: Hallucination in Multi-Step Tools

Multi-step tools, such as agents that search, summarise, and act, face a special hallucination risk: an invention in an early step can propagate through later steps, as in Section 28, becoming harder to spot at each stage. A search step might retrieve an irrelevant document, a summary step might invent a detail from that document, and an action step might then act on the invented detail, producing a real-world effect based on a chain of errors. Evaluating and defending multi-step tools therefore requires checking each step, as in Section 16, with hallucination checks at each stage, not just a final check on the outcome.

The first defence is to ground each step in its own source. A search step should report which documents it found, and why they were judged relevant. A summary step should report which facts it extracted, and from which passages. An action step should report which facts it based its action on, and should refuse to act if those facts are uncertain, missing, or contradicted by their sources. That grounding creates a trail from source to action, which a checker can follow backwards to find where an invention entered.

The second defence is to check for invention at step boundaries. When one step passes its output to the next, compare that output with its input source, using the fact-checking methods of Sections 21 and 22, before allowing the next step to proceed. If a summary step introduces a date not in its retrieved passage, flag it and stop the chain, rather than passing the invented date to an action step that will treat it as fact. Step-boundary checks act as gates: they prevent inventions from travelling further, where they become harder to detect and more consequential. For local tools, these gates can be simple scripts that check dates, names, and numbers at each boundary, plus human review for important chains.

The third defence is to limit what action steps can do based on uncertain information. An action step should require high-confidence, well-sourced facts before it takes an irreversible action, such as deleting a file, sending a message, or changing a record. If the facts are uncertain, the action step should ask the user, or refuse, rather than proceeding on a guess. That design, sometimes called a safety gate, is covered more fully in Chapters 28 to 30, but its hallucination relevance is clear: it prevents a guessed fact from causing a real effect, by requiring stronger evidence for stronger actions. The more consequential the action, the stronger the grounding required, which is a simple, defensible rule.

Finally, test multi-step tools with hallucination temptation cases at each step, not just end to end. Give the search step a question with no answer, and confirm it reports none. Give the summary step a passage with a changed detail, and confirm it reflects the change. Give the action step uncertain facts, and confirm it refuses or asks. Step-level temptation testing, combined with end-to-end testing, gives a complete picture of where a multi-step tool hallucinates, and where its defences hold. A multi-step tool that passes step-level tests is far safer than one that only passes end-to-end tests on easy cases, because its safety does not depend on luck across a whole chain.

for s in steps:
    print(s)

Section 35: Local Toy: Claim Extractor

A claim extractor is a small program that takes a paragraph and splits it into individual claims, one per line, so that each claim can be checked separately, as in Section 32. Building one, even a simple version, teaches you how to turn smooth prose into checkable pieces, which is the first step in any serious hallucination check. It also gives you a practical helper: paste an output into the extractor, get its claims as a list, and check each one against its source, without having to disentangle blended sentences by hand. The toy will be imperfect, but its imperfections are instructive, because they show what automatic claim extraction can and cannot do.

The simplest extractor splits text into sentences, using full stops as separators, and treats each sentence as a claim. That works well for short, simple outputs, where each sentence does state one main fact. It fails for complex sentences that contain several facts, such as "Ada met Ben on Monday in Lagos and they approved the budget," which contains who, when, where, and what, all in one sentence. A simple extractor will treat that as one claim, which is too coarse for careful checking, because one part could be true while another is invented. Noting that limitation is part of the lesson: sentence splitting is a start, not a complete solution, and complex sentences need human splitting into separate claims before they can be checked properly.

A slightly better toy can also split on conjunctions like "and," and can flag sentences containing multiple dates, names, or numbers, as likely multi-claim sentences that need human splitting. It can print each sentence with a count of its dates, names, and numbers, so the user can see which sentences are complex and deserve extra attention. That flagging, rather than perfect splitting, is the toy's real value: it directs human checking to the sentences where invention is most likely to hide, because complexity gives invention more places to hide. A tool that says "this sentence has three claims; check each" is helping, even if it cannot split them itself.

Test your extractor on three kinds of text: a faithful summary, a summary with an invented detail, and a complex paragraph with several facts per sentence. Observe where it helps and where it fails, and write those observations in a comment at the top of the file, as in Section 21. That documentation prevents over-trust: users will know sentence splitting is a helper, not a verdict. Documented limits are what separate a useful toy from a misleading one, in hallucination work as in evaluation work generally.

Finally, use the extractor as part of your checking routine, not as a replacement for it. Paste an output, get its claims, check each claim against its source, and mark each as verified, unverified, or contradicted, as in Section 32. That routine, extractor plus human checking, is faster than checking by reading alone, because it makes claims visible, and it is more reliable than automatic checking alone, because it keeps human judgement in charge of the final verdict. Building and using such a toy, on your own files, is a concrete step toward the habit this chapter teaches: never trust a smooth paragraph; always check its claims, one by one.

claims = [s.strip() for s in text.split(".") if s.strip()]
for claim in claims:
    print("Claim:", claim)

Section 36: What to Do When You Catch an Error

Catching a hallucination is only the first step; what you do next determines whether catching it helps anyone. The immediate step is to stop the error from spreading: do not share the output, do not save it as a trusted note, and do not use it as input to another tool, until it is corrected. That pause prevents snowballing, as in Section 28. If already shared, correct it where it was shared, with a clear note, so readers learn it was wrong.

Next, understand the error before you fix it. Ask what kind of hallucination it was: invented date, invented cause, invented item, misattributed quote, or something else? Ask where it entered: was the source missing, was the instruction unclear, was the tool asked a false-premise question, or did a long answer drift? That diagnosis tells you whether the fix should be a clearer instruction, a better source, or a new check. Fixing without diagnosing, as in Section 33, feels faster, but it leaves the cause in place, ready to produce the same error on the next similar input.

Then fix the cause and test the fix, as in Chapter 21's re-evaluation loop. Change the instruction, add the check, or improve the source, and then run both the original failing case and fresh cases of the same kind, to confirm the fix generalises. If the fix helps the original case but fails fresh cases, it was too narrow, and it needs broadening. If the fix helps hallucination cases but harms ordinary cases, for example by making the tool refuse too often, it needs balancing, with a trade-off decision recorded. Testing fixes, rather than assuming they work, is what turns catching an error into genuine improvement, rather than into a cycle of patching and re-breaking.

Also consider whether the error reveals a limit that should be communicated to users. If your tool hallucinates on a certain kind of question, such as questions about events not in your notes, tell users about that limit, in plain language, so they know to check those answers extra carefully, or to avoid asking those questions until the tool improves. Communicating limits is not admitting defeat; it is giving users the information they need to use the tool safely, which is more valuable than a tool that appears perfect but fails silently on certain inputs. Users who know a limit can work around it; users who do not know it will be misled by it.

Finally, treat catching errors as success, not failure. A builder who catches many hallucinations during testing is doing evaluation well, not building badly, because those errors were always there, waiting to be found, and finding them before release is far better than having users find them after. Celebrate catches, log them, turn them into tests, and share the lessons, as in Section 31. That culture, where catching an error is praised and fixing its cause is expected, produces tools that get safer over time, because errors are treated as information, not embarrassment. The worst response to a caught error is to hide it; the best is to learn from it openly, fix its cause, and tell users what changed.

Section 37: Hallucination and Trust

Trust in an AI tool is built slowly, through repeated, checkable accuracy, and it can be destroyed quickly, by a single confident hallucination that misleads a user on an important decision. That asymmetry, slow to build, fast to lose, is why hallucination matters so much for builders who want their tools to be used and relied upon. Users do not expect perfection, but they do expect honesty: they expect a tool to say when it does not know, to show its sources, and to correct its errors openly, as this chapter has taught. A tool that does those things, even with occasional errors, will be trusted more than a tool that claims perfection and then hallucinates confidently, because its trust is grounded in verifiable behaviour, not in promises.

Trust also depends on stakes. A user may trust a tool for brainstorming, where hallucinations are low-stakes, while distrusting it for factual summaries. That selective trust is rational, and builders should encourage it, by labelling tools and outputs according to their reliability for different tasks, rather than claiming general trustworthiness. A tool described as "good for drafting ideas; check all facts before sharing" sets honest expectations, and users who follow that guidance will have good experiences. A tool described simply as "accurate" invites blind trust across all tasks, which will break on the first high-stakes hallucination, damaging trust even for low-stakes uses where the tool actually performs well.

Rebuilding trust requires more than fixing the error. It requires explaining what went wrong, what was fixed, and what will be checked in future. It requires showing, through subsequent evaluations, that the fix worked and that similar errors are now caught by tests. And it requires time, during which the tool performs reliably on the tasks users care about, without new serious hallucinations. There is no shortcut to rebuilding trust: apologies without fixes, or fixes without transparency, will not restore it, because users have learned, from experience, that the tool can mislead them, and only sustained, checkable reliability can teach them otherwise.

Builders should also think about trust at community level. When many tools hallucinate confidently, users begin to distrust AI outputs generally, harming even careful builders. Conversely, when builders consistently evaluate honestly, show sources, admit uncertainty, and correct errors openly, they contribute to a culture where AI outputs are treated as checkable drafts rather than as oracles, which benefits everyone. Your individual honesty therefore has a collective effect, beyond your own tool's reputation, by shaping what users expect from AI tools in general.

Finally, measure trust, not just accuracy, where you can. Ask users whether they check outputs, whether they feel confident acting on them, and whether they would recommend the tool to others for factual tasks. Those answers, combined with accuracy and hallucination rates, give a fuller picture of whether your tool is genuinely trusted, or merely used because no alternative exists. A tool that is used but not trusted will be abandoned when an alternative appears; a tool that is trusted, because it is honest about its limits and reliable within them, will be kept, recommended, and improved with user feedback. That lasting trust is the real reward for the hard work of reducing hallucination.

Section 38: Designing Tools That Show Uncertainty

Designing a tool that shows uncertainty means making its doubts visible, in a way users can understand and act on, rather than hiding them behind confident prose. Uncertainty can be shown in many simple ways: by using cautious wording, as in Section 3; by listing what is known and what is unknown separately; by showing sources, as in Section 8; by marking unchecked claims; or by using a simple confidence label, as in Section 30, based on checkable signals like retrieval match. The best design depends on your tool and your users, but the principle is universal: uncertainty that is visible can be managed, while uncertainty that is hidden will mislead.

Start by deciding what uncertainty matters. For a summariser, it matters whether a fact was in the source, whether a date was exact, and whether any part was guessed. For a search tool, it matters whether a retrieved document was highly relevant or only loosely related. For a question-answering tool, it matters whether an answer was found in a trusted note, inferred from several notes, or not found at all. List those uncertainty types for your tool, and design a way to show each one, in plain language, without technical jargon. A label like "from your note, exact date not stated" is more useful than "0.73," because users know what to do: check elsewhere.

Place uncertainty where users will see it before they act, not buried at the end of a long output. If a claim is uncertain, mark it in place, beside the claim, so the reader encounters the warning at the moment they read the claim. If a whole answer is uncertain, say so at the top, before the answer, so readers adjust their trust before they read, rather than after they have already believed. Placement is part of design: a warning that appears after the reader has already acted is too late, however well worded it is. Think about the reader's journey, from question to answer to action, and put uncertainty signals at the decision points along that journey.

Test your uncertainty design with real users, asking whether they noticed the signals and changed their behaviour. If users did not notice a signal, make it more prominent. If they noticed but misunderstood it, reword it. If they noticed and understood but ignored it, ask why: was checking too hard, was the signal too frequent, or did they feel they had no alternative? User testing reveals whether uncertainty design works in practice, which is the only test that matters, because a design that looks good on paper but is ignored in use provides no safety at all.

Finally, keep uncertainty design simple and consistent. Use the same wording, the same labels, and the same placement across your tool, so users learn what each signal means and can recognise it quickly. Changing signals from one output to the next confuses users and erodes trust in the signals themselves. Consistency, simplicity, and placement at decision points, combined with honest calibration, as in Section 30, make uncertainty design a genuine hallucination defence, rather than a decorative disclaimer. A tool that shows its uncertainty clearly, consistently, and early, respects its users, and earns their trust by refusing to pretend to know more than it does.

Section 39: Common Myths About Hallucination

Several myths about hallucination mislead builders, and correcting them helps design better defences. The first myth is that hallucinations will disappear as models get bigger or better. Bigger models may hallucinate less on some tasks, but prediction without verification will always risk invention, especially on rare, ambiguous, or missing facts, because no model can know everything, and no training set can cover every question. The solution is therefore not to wait for a perfect model, but to design tools with grounding, checking, and uncertainty, as this chapter teaches, regardless of model size. A bigger model without checks is still a guessing machine, just a more fluent one.

The second myth is that hallucinations are rare, or only happen on strange questions. In reality, they happen on ordinary questions too, especially dates, numbers, names, and lists. Builders who believe hallucinations are rare will not test for them, will not design defences, and will be surprised when users find them on everyday tasks. Builders who assume hallucinations are common enough to matter will test, design, and check, and will therefore produce safer tools, even if their underlying model is the same.

The third myth is that a tool that sounds confident must be right, or that a tool that sounds hesitant must be wrong. Confidence wording, as in Section 3, reflects likelihood of phrasing, not strength of evidence, and it can be manipulated by instruction wording or by training to be helpful. A hesitant answer may be carefully correct, with qualifiers that reflect genuine uncertainty, while a confident answer may be a fluent invention. Judging by confidence alone is therefore unreliable, and readers must be taught to judge by sources, checks, and evidence, not by tone. Builders should also avoid designing tools that always sound confident, because that design exploits readers' trust in tone, rather than earning trust through evidence.

The fourth myth is that checking with another AI output is sufficient verification. Asking a second tool, or the same tool again, to check an answer may repeat the same hallucination, especially if both tools share similar patterns, or if the checker is given the original answer as a hint. Real checking requires an independent source, as in Section 5, or a human judgement against that source, not merely another prediction. A second prediction is not evidence; it is another guess, however confidently phrased. Builders who rely on AI-to-AI checking without independent sources are building a circle of guesses that feels like verification but is not.

The fifth myth is that hallucination is only a problem for factual tools, not for creative ones. Creative tools also hallucinate, in the sense that they present invented facts as if they were real, when they are used for factual purposes, such as drafting a biography, a report, or a summary. The boundary between creative and factual use is set by the reader's purpose, not by the tool's intention, so a tool designed for creativity can still mislead when its output is used as fact. Builders should therefore design even creative tools to label factual claims, show sources where relevant, and warn when an output should not be treated as factual, because users will use creative outputs factually, whether the builder intended it or not.

Section 40: Hallucination in Your Own Writing

Hallucination is not only a machine problem; human writers also invent details, especially when summarising from memory, paraphrasing, or writing under time pressure. A human summariser might add a cause that was not in the original, sharpen a vague number into a specific one, or misattribute a quote, for the same reasons a model does: to make the text smoother, more complete, or more convincing. Recognising that human writing shares these patterns helps builders in two ways: it makes them more sympathetic to the difficulty of faithful summarising, and it makes them more careful in their own writing, because they know that checking is needed for human outputs too, not just for machine ones.

The habits that prevent machine hallucination also prevent human hallucination: work from the source, not memory; list facts before prose; preserve qualifiers; and check the draft fact by fact. Those habits feel slow at first, but they become faster with practice, and they produce writing that readers can trust, because it can be verified. A writer who works from a fact list, checks dates and names, and labels guesses as guesses, is practising the same discipline this chapter teaches for tools, applied to their own craft.

Human writers also face a temptation that machines do not: the temptation to improve a story, to make it neater, more dramatic, or more fitting to a point they want to make. That temptation can lead to sharpening, merging, and elaboration, as in Section 9, even when the writer knows, in the back of their mind, that the detail was not in the source. The defence is to separate drafting from checking: write freely first, if that helps creativity, then check strictly against the source, removing or labelling any detail that cannot be found there. That two-stage process, free draft then strict check, gives writers both creativity and accuracy, without sacrificing either to the other.

When you share your own writing that was based on sources, show your sources, as in Section 8, and note any parts that are your interpretation, guess, or opinion, rather than sourced fact. That labelling helps readers distinguish what you verified from what you think, which is essential for trust, especially when your writing will be used as a source by others. A writer who labels interpretations clearly is not weakening their argument; they are strengthening it, because readers can see which parts are solid and which are open to debate, and can engage with the argument honestly, rather than being misled by a blend of fact and opinion presented as pure fact.

Finally, apply the same correction habits to your own writing as to machine outputs: when you discover an error, correct it transparently, as in Section 33, and learn from it by adding it to your personal checklist of common mistakes. Over time, that checklist, whether it is "check dates, check names, do not add causes," becomes a personal hallucination defence, as reliable as any tool's check. Human and machine hallucination share causes and cures, because both arise from the pressure to produce smooth, complete text from incomplete memory or evidence. The cure, in both cases, is the same: ground in sources, check claim by claim, and be honest about uncertainty.

Section 41: Checking Before Sharing

Sharing is the moment when a hallucination stops being a private draft and starts affecting other people. Before you share any AI-assisted output, whether it is a summary, a list, an explanation, or a set of instructions, check it as if someone else will act on it, because they may. That pre-sharing check does not need to be exhaustive for every casual note, but it must be thorough for anything that will be shared widely, used for a decision, or filed as a record. The effort of checking should scale with the reach and stakes of sharing, not with how much time you happen to have.

A practical pre-sharing check has four steps. First, list the output's factual claims, as in Section 32, and mark the most important ones: those that affect decisions, dates, numbers, names, or safety. Second, verify those important claims against their sources, using the methods of Section 5, and correct or remove any that fail. Third, check for invented details in the less important claims by sampling, reading a few carefully, especially the least familiar ones, as in Section 17. Fourth, label what you checked and what remains unchecked, in a short note attached to the shared output, so readers know how much verification was done. Those four steps, done consistently, prevent most sharing-stage hallucinations from reaching readers.

Also check the framing of what you share. Does the title, introduction, or summary promise more certainty than the checked facts support? Does it present a guess as a conclusion, or an interpretation as a fact? Framing errors are hallucinations too, in the sense that they mislead readers about the strength of evidence, even when individual facts are correct. Adjust framing to match evidence: soften strong claims that rest on weak evidence, restore qualifiers that were dropped, and say plainly when a conclusion is your interpretation rather than a sourced fact. Honest framing protects readers who skim, because skimmers judge by framing more than by details.

Be especially careful when sharing outputs that have passed through several hands or tools. A summary of a summary, or a list copied from a generated list, may contain hallucinations from an earlier stage that are now hidden, because the intermediate source is no longer visible. Whenever possible, share from the original source, or re-check against the original before sharing a derived version. If you cannot access the original, say so, and label the shared version as derived and unchecked against the original. That label prevents a chain of copying from turning an early invention into an apparent fact, as in Section 28.

Finally, make pre-sharing checking a habit, not a hurdle you skip when busy. Keep a short checklist beside your sharing workflow, with the four steps above, and run through it quickly for every shared output. Over time, the checklist becomes automatic, and checking becomes part of writing, not an extra task. A builder who checks before sharing, consistently, will share fewer errors, correct the ones that slip through transparently, and build a reputation for reliability that no amount of fluent, unchecked output can match.

Section 42: Hallucination and Safety

Hallucination becomes a safety issue when an invented fact leads a reader to take a harmful action, miss a safety step, or trust a dangerous instruction. A wrong date in a trivia summary is embarrassing; a wrong step in a safety procedure, a wrong warning in a health explanation, or a wrong detail in an emergency guide can cause real harm. Safety-critical outputs therefore need stricter hallucination defences than ordinary outputs: stronger grounding, more thorough checking, explicit warnings, and, where stakes are high, human review before sharing or acting. The higher the stakes, the stronger the defences must be, which is a simple rule that should guide every safety-related design decision.

One key defence is to keep safety-related tools narrow and grounded. A tool giving general safety information and pointing to official guidance is safer than one giving specific instructions for a specific person, based on incomplete information. Narrow tools, with clear limits, are easier to test, easier to check, and less likely to hallucinate in harmful ways, because their scope is limited to material they can ground well. Builders should resist pressure to make safety tools broader than they can safely support, even when users ask for broader answers, because a narrow, honest tool helps more than a broad, hallucinating one.

Another defence is to include safety checks at every step, as in Sections 28 and 34, with a fail-safe default: when in doubt, refuse, warn, or ask a human, rather than guessing. A safety tool that says "I cannot verify this step; please check the official guide before proceeding" is doing its job well, even though it did not complete the task, because it prevented a potentially harmful guess from being acted on. Fail-safe defaults feel frustrating to users who want a quick answer, but they are essential for safety, and users who understand why the default exists will appreciate it, especially if the tool explains what to check and where to find reliable guidance.

Also test safety tools with hallucination temptation cases that are safety-specific, such as asking for a procedure step that does not exist, or for a warning that was never given in the source. Those tests reveal whether the tool invents safety details to complete a pattern, which is one of the most dangerous hallucination patterns, because invented safety details look like real precautions. A tool that passes ordinary hallucination tests but fails safety-specific ones is not yet safe, and it should not be used for safety tasks until those failures are fixed and re-tested with fresh cases.

Finally, report safety-related hallucination risks honestly, as in Chapter 21, with a separate safety evaluation, and do not release a safety tool for general use while known safety hallucinations remain unfixed. Restrict it to a safer, narrower use, or keep it as a draft for human-reviewed use only, until its safety tests pass consistently. That restraint may feel like slow progress, but it is the responsible pace for safety work, where a single hallucination can outweigh many correct answers. Safety, in hallucination work as in all AI work, is not a feature to add later; it is a constraint that shapes the tool from the start.

Section 43: Building a Safer Summariser

A safer summariser is not a more complicated summariser; it is a summariser designed, from the start, to stay faithful to its source, to show its work, and to refuse when it cannot. Building one, as a local toy, brings together many defences from this chapter into a single, practical tool. Start with a clear rule set, written in plain language and kept with the code: summarise only the given note, do not add facts, causes, or numbers, preserve qualifiers, keep dates and names exactly as written, and say when information is missing. Those rules, stated explicitly, are the summariser's specification, and every later test should check it against them.

Next, implement the summariser in simple steps, each checkable. First, extract key facts from the note, as a list, rather than writing prose directly, as in Section 24. Second, write a short summary from that list only, using the list's wording for dates, numbers, and names. Third, run a checker, as in Section 21, that flags any date, number, or name in the summary that is not in the note, and any cause word, such as "because," that introduces a cause not on the fact list. Fourth, if any flag is raised, either correct the summary or mark it for human review, rather than outputting it silently. That pipeline, extract, write, check, flag, is simple to build with the standard library, and it embodies the chapter's defences in code.

Then test the summariser with the hallucination test set from Section 31, including temptation cases: empty notes, notes with vague dates, notes with false-premise questions, and notes where a detail was changed. Record which tests pass and which fail, and fix the biggest failure group first, as in Chapter 21. A safer summariser is not one that passes every test on the first try; it is one whose failures are known, logged, and progressively fixed, with tests that prevent regressions. That testing discipline, more than any single clever feature, is what makes a summariser safer over time.

Also design the summariser's output to show uncertainty and sources, as in Sections 8 and 38. End each summary with its source filename, and with a note about anything that was missing or uncertain in the source, such as "exact date not stated in the note." That output format helps readers check, and it reminds the builder, every time they see an output, whether the summariser stayed within its rules. An output format that hides sources and uncertainty may look cleaner, but it is less safe, because it removes the very information readers need to judge trust.

Finally, share the safer summariser with its evaluation, as in Chapter 21, including its test set, its scores, its biggest remaining failure, and its limits. That sharing invites others to test it on their own notes, to add failure cases, and to suggest improvements, which is how a local toy becomes a community-trusted tool. A summariser shared with honest evaluation, even if it is small and imperfect, is more valuable than a larger, untested one, because its users know what to trust, what to check, and what to avoid. Building safely, testing honestly, and sharing transparently are the three habits that make a summariser genuinely safer, not just safer-sounding.

Section 44: Measuring Hallucination Rate

Measuring hallucination rate means counting how often a tool invents, out of how many opportunities it had to invent, on a defined test set. That rate, with its test set size and categories, turns a vague worry into a number trackable over time. To measure it, you need a test set where the correct output is known, including cases where the correct output is a refusal or a faithful summary, and a clear rule for what counts as a hallucination: any factual claim in the output that is not supported by the source, or that contradicts it, counts, whether it is a date, a name, a cause, or an invented item.

Counting hallucinations claim by claim, as in Section 5, is more informative than counting outputs as simply right or wrong, because one output may contain several hallucinations, or one hallucination among many correct claims. For each output, count its total claims, count how many are hallucinated, and compute the rate as hallucinated claims divided by total claims, for that output and across the test set. Also report the rate by category, such as dates, numbers, names, causes, and items, because different categories may need different fixes. A tool with a low overall rate but a high rate on dates, for example, needs date-specific work, which an overall rate alone would hide.

Be careful about what counts as an opportunity. A tool that refuses many questions will have fewer claims, and therefore fewer chances to hallucinate, which can make its rate look artificially low if refusals are not counted separately. Report refusals alongside the rate: how many questions were refused, and whether those refusals were correct, as in Section 7. A tool that refuses correctly is safer than one that answers with hallucinations, because refusal prevents harm. The full picture therefore includes both the hallucination rate on answered questions, and the refusal rate on unanswerable ones.

Also report the test set and its limits with the rate, as in Chapter 21. A rate of five percent on twenty easy cases means something very different from five percent on two hundred hard, temptation-rich cases. State the test size, the categories, the difficulty mix, and whether the test set was fresh or reused for tuning, because those details determine how much the rate predicts real performance. A rate reported without those details is a slogan, not a measurement, however precise it looks, and it should not be used to compare tools or to claim safety.

Finally, use the rate to track improvement, not to chase a perfect zero. A rate that falls from twenty percent to ten percent after a grounding fix is real progress, even though ten percent is still too high for high-stakes use. A rate that stays flat despite fixes suggests the fixes are not addressing the main cause, and that a different approach is needed. Tracking the rate, by category, over time, with fresh test cases added as new failures are found, gives builders a honest, actionable picture of safety, and it is far more useful than a single, uncontextualised claim that a tool "rarely hallucinates."

print("Hallucination rate:", hallucinated / total)

Section 45: When to Refuse to Answer

Refusal is not a failure; it is a safety behaviour, and knowing when to refuse is a core skill for builders and tools alike. A tool should refuse, or partially refuse, when it lacks the evidence to answer honestly, when the question is based on a false premise it cannot correct with available sources, when the answer would require guessing about high-stakes facts, or when answering would expose private information or cause foreseeable harm. In each of those cases, a refusal, with an explanation of what is missing and what could help, protects the user more than a fluent guess, because it prevents a wrong answer from being acted on as if it were true.

Designing refusal well starts with clear rules, written down and tested, as in Section 7. For a local tool, rules might include: refuse when no relevant source is found; refuse when sources disagree and the disagreement cannot be resolved from available material; refuse when the question asks for a specific fact that the source does not state; and refuse, or give only general information, when the question concerns a high-stakes personal situation that requires qualified human judgement. Test each rule with examples, including borderline cases where refusal and answering are both defensible, and record how the tool behaved, so that refusal behaviour can be improved over time, like any other behaviour.

Wording refusals well matters, because a blunt refusal can frustrate users and push them to seek answers from less careful sources, which may hallucinate without warning. A good refusal explains what is missing, suggests a next step, and offers safe help. For example: "I cannot find that date in your notes. If you add a note with the date, I can answer, or you could check the original meeting record." That refusal is helpful, because it guides the user toward a solution, and it models honesty, which users learn to value. A refusal that merely says "I cannot answer" without explanation feels like a dead end, and users will rightly be frustrated by it, even though it is safer than a guess.

Also consider partial refusal: answering the parts you can ground, and refusing the parts you cannot, rather than refusing everything or guessing everything. A question with three parts, where two are grounded and one is not, can be answered in part, with the ungrounded part clearly marked as unknown. Partial answers respect the user's need for help, while protecting them from guesses on the parts that matter. They also teach users that answers can be mixed, with some parts solid and others uncertain, which is a more accurate picture of most real questions than a simple answer-or-refuse binary.

Finally, evaluate refusal behaviour, as in Section 7, by counting correct refusals, incorrect refusals, and missed refusals, where the tool answered when it should have refused. That evaluation, reported alongside accuracy and hallucination rate, gives a complete picture of safety: a tool that refuses correctly, answers accurately when it can, and rarely guesses, is a tool users can trust, because its boundaries are clear and honest. Refusal, practised well, is not the opposite of helpfulness; it is a form of helpfulness, one that prioritises truth over fluency, and user safety over the appearance of competence.

    return found if found else "I do not know: no source found."

Section 46: Hallucination in Names and Places

Names and places deserve a section of their own, because they are both highly specific and easily confused, and because errors in them carry special consequences. A wrong person name can misattribute work, blame, or praise. A wrong place name can misdirect a reader, confuse a timeline, or imply a connection that does not exist. Language models confuse names and places readily, because similar names share patterns, because many places share names, and because a plausible-sounding name is easy to generate when the real one is rare or unknown. Readers, meanwhile, often lack the knowledge to spot a wrong name, especially for unfamiliar people or places, which makes these hallucinations both common and hard to catch.

The most common pattern is substitution: replacing the correct name with a similar one. A model might swap two people with similar names, name a well-known city instead of a smaller town with the same name in another region, or attribute a work to a famous author instead of its real, lesser-known author. Substitution feels plausible because the substituted name fits the context: it is the right kind of name, in the right kind of sentence, and readers who know the general topic but not the specific detail may accept it without question. The defence is to check every name and place against its source, exactly as written, not merely for plausibility, because plausibility is precisely what substitution exploits.

Another pattern is inventing a full name where only a partial name exists. A source mentioning "Ada" might become "Ada Lovelace" in a summary, or a town might gain a country never stated. That addition may be true generally, but if not in the source, readers cannot verify it, and it may be wrong here, where two people share a first name. A safe rule is to report names and places exactly as the source gives them, and to label any expansion as your own addition, with its own source, rather than silently merging it into the sourced claim.

For local tools, name checking can be partly automated, as in Section 21: flag output names that do not appear in the source. That check will flag legitimate paraphrases and pronoun replacements too, but those flags are worth reviewing, because they draw attention to exactly the details where substitution hides. Human review of flagged names, against the source, is quick, because names are short and distinctive, and it catches the most consequential substitutions before they reach readers. A name check that takes two minutes can prevent a misattribution that would take far longer to correct after sharing.

Finally, when you share outputs containing names and places, double-check the least familiar ones, because familiarity is a poor guide to correctness. A familiar name feels right even when it is substituted, while an unfamiliar name feels suspicious even when it is correct, so intuition alone will mislead you in both directions. Check unfamiliar names to confirm they are real and correctly spelled, and check familiar names to confirm they are the right familiar name for this specific claim, not merely a famous name that fits the pattern. That two-way check, unfamiliar and familiar alike, is the disciplined habit that keeps names and places honest in any output you share.

source_names = {"Ada", "Lagos"}
output_names = {"Ada", "Abuja"}

Section 47: A Worked Example: Checking a Summary

This section works through a complete checking example, so you can see the chapter's methods applied to a concrete summary. Imagine a short note you wrote: "Team meeting planned for Monday in Lagos. Ada will present the budget. The budget is about fifty thousand, subject to approval." Now imagine a summary produced from that note: "The team met on Monday in Lagos, where Ada presented a budget of fifty thousand, which was approved." That summary reads smoothly and looks accurate, but detective reading, as in Section 32, reveals several hallucinations hiding in its fluency, and working through them teaches the checking habit better than any abstract rule.

First, list the summary's claims, one per line. Claim one: the team met on Monday. Claim two: the meeting was in Lagos. Claim three: Ada presented the budget. Claim four: the budget was fifty thousand. Claim five: the budget was approved. Now compare each claim with the note. Claim two matches: the note says Lagos. Claim three is partly wrong in tense: the note says Ada will present, in the future, while the summary says she presented, in the past, turning a plan into a completed event. Claim four drops a qualifier: the note says about fifty thousand, while the summary says fifty thousand, turning an estimate into an exact figure, as in Section 23. Claim five invents an outcome: the note says subject to approval, while the summary says approved, turning a condition into a completion.

Claim one also needs care: the note says the meeting was planned, not that it happened, so "met" upgrades a plan to a fact, as in claim three. Repeated across claims, that tense shift turns a planned meeting with a proposed budget into a completed meeting with an approved budget. A reader who only reads the summary would believe decisions had been made, when they had only been planned, and might act accordingly, for example by spending money that had not yet been approved. That is why tense, qualifiers, and conditions matter as much as dates and names, even though they are easier to overlook.

Now fix the summary, claim by claim. A faithful version might read: "Team meeting planned for Monday in Lagos. Ada will present the budget, about fifty thousand, subject to approval." That version is shorter, less dramatic, and far more accurate, because it preserves plans as plans, estimates as estimates, and conditions as conditions. It may feel less satisfying than the fluent, invented version, but it is the version a reader can safely act on, because it does not promise decisions that have not been made. Faithfulness, in this example, is not pedantry; it is the difference between a useful summary and a misleading one.

Finally, note what checking taught you: smooth summaries can contain several small hallucinations, tense and qualifiers are common hiding places, and listing claims separately reveals errors smooth reading misses. Add this example to your checking examples, and use it to teach others, because concrete examples teach checking better than warnings alone. Every builder should have a few such worked examples, from their own files, to remind themselves, and their users, why checking claim by claim is worth the time it takes.

Section 48: Hallucination Checklist for Builders

A builder's checklist turns this chapter's defences into a routine you can run before releasing or sharing a tool. Keep it short, on one page, and use it every time, even when you are in a hurry, because hallucination defences skipped under pressure are exactly the ones users need most. The checklist below is a starting point; adapt it to your tool, but keep it short enough to actually use, and update it when you learn a new failure pattern, as in Chapter 21's checklist habit.

First, grounding: does the tool base factual answers on a source it can name, and does it say when no source is found, rather than guessing? Second, instructions: are the tool's rules clear about not adding facts, causes, or numbers, and about preserving qualifiers and uncertainty? Third, output format: does the output show sources, mark uncertain claims, and use cautious wording where evidence is weak? Fourth, checking: have you run a fact checker, as in Section 21, on a sample of outputs, and have you read flagged claims by hand, rather than trusting the checker alone? Fifth, temptation tests: have you run the hallucination test set from Section 31, including false-premise, empty-input, and detail-change cases, and recorded the results separately from ordinary accuracy?

Sixth, categories: have you checked dates, numbers, names, places, quotes, and lists separately, as in Sections 4, 12, and 17, because each hides inventions differently? Seventh, multi-step safety: if the tool has multiple steps, have you checked each step and each boundary, as in Sections 16 and 34, and does an uncertain fact block irreversible actions? Eighth, user guidance: does the tool tell users what to check, especially dates and names, and does it explain its limits in plain language? Ninth, correction: if a hallucination was found, was its cause fixed, was it added to the test set, and was any shared wrong version corrected transparently, as in Section 33?

Use the checklist as a gate, not as paperwork. If any item fails, fix it or restrict the tool's use until it passes, rather than releasing with a known hallucination weakness and hoping users will not notice. Users will notice, on the inputs that matter to them, and the cost of a post-release correction, in trust and in rework, far exceeds the cost of a pre-release fix. A checklist that gates release is therefore not bureaucratic; it is protective, for users and for builders alike, because it ensures that safety work is done before harm occurs, not after.

Finally, review the checklist itself regularly. If an item never catches anything, consider whether it is still needed, or whether it needs to be made more specific. If a failure occurs that the checklist did not catch, add a new item that would have caught it, with a concrete test. A checklist that evolves with your tool's failures becomes steadily more effective, while a checklist that stays fixed becomes steadily less relevant, as tools and failure patterns change. The goal is not a longer list, but a list that reliably catches the hallucinations your tool actually produces, in the forms they actually take.

Section 49: Hallucination Checklist for Readers

Readers need a checklist too, because builders cannot check every output for every reader, and because readers are the last line of defence before a hallucination is acted on or shared. A reader's checklist must be even shorter, because readers are often busy and a long list will be ignored. Three to five questions, memorised and used consistently, will catch more hallucinations than a comprehensive list that is never opened. The questions below are a starting point; adapt them to your needs, but keep them short, memorable, and focused on the highest-risk claims.

First, what is the source? For any important claim, ask where it came from: is a source named, can you open it, and does it actually support the claim? If no source is given, treat the claim as unverified, however confidently it is phrased, and check it elsewhere before acting. Second, are the dates, numbers, and names right? Check those details first, against the source or a trusted reference, because they are where hallucinations most often hide, as in Section 4, and because they are quick to verify. Third, are qualifiers preserved? Ask whether the original said "about," "suggests," or "planned," and whether the output upgraded those words to exact figures, proofs, or completed events, as in Section 47. Upgraded qualifiers are a warning sign that the output may have invented certainty where none existed.

Fourth, does the output make sense as a whole? Check for internal inconsistencies: two dates for one event, or a conclusion that does not follow from the facts. An internally inconsistent output cannot be fully trusted, even if individual claims look plausible, because at least one part must be wrong, and other parts may be wrong too. Fifth, what will you do based on this output? If you will act, share, or decide based on it, check more thoroughly, especially the single claim your action depends on, as in Section 20's "one claim, one check" routine. If you will merely read it for interest, a lighter check may be enough, but never treat a merely interesting output as a verified fact without checking, if you later decide to act on it.

Teach this checklist to others, especially in shared work, where one unchecked sharing can mislead a team. Put it where readers will see it: shared notes, a tool footer, or a short guide. A visible, shared checklist becomes a habit that protects everyone. A culture where checking is normal, expected, and praised, as in Section 20, is the strongest reader-side defence against hallucination, stronger than any single tool or warning.

Finally, remember that reader checking is not distrust of AI in general; it is the appropriate use of AI as a helper, not an oracle. Tools that draft, summarise, and search can save enormous time, even when they sometimes hallucinate, provided readers check the parts that matter before acting. That partnership, tool for speed, human for verification, is the mature model for using AI tools, and it is far more sustainable than either blind trust, which leads to harmful errors, or total distrust, which wastes the tool's genuine benefits. Check, in short, not because tools are useless, but because they are useful enough to be worth checking.

    print(q)

Section 50: Reducing Hallucination as a Habit

Reducing hallucination is not a one-time fix, but a habit, practised every time you build, use, or share an AI tool. The habit has a few core parts, repeated so often that they become automatic: ground answers in sources you can name; preserve qualifiers and uncertainty; check claim by claim, especially dates, numbers, and names; test with temptation cases that invite guessing; and correct errors transparently, fixing causes rather than hiding single outputs. Those parts, taken together, do not eliminate hallucination, but they reduce its frequency, its visibility, and its harm, which is the realistic and valuable goal for any builder.

Make the habit easy to keep, by building it into your workflow, so that checking is the default step rather than an extra chore you must remember under pressure. When the checker, the test set, and the checklist are all within reach, you will use them even on busy days, and your tools will stay safer as a result. Keep your fact checker script beside your tools, so checking takes seconds, not a fresh effort each time. Keep your hallucination test set in your project folder, so running it is one command. Keep your checklists, for builders and readers, where you and your users will see them, not in a file you never open. Habits survive when they are easy; they die when they require heroic effort on every occasion. Design your workflow so that the honest, checking path is also the easy path, and you will follow it far more often than a workflow that makes checking a chore to be skipped when busy.

Also make the habit social, by sharing it with users, collaborators, and readers. Tell users what you checked, what remains unchecked, and what to check themselves. Invite collaborators to add temptation cases when they find failures, and to review your checklists. Share corrected outputs transparently, with notes about what was wrong and what was fixed. A habit practised alone is fragile, because it depends on one person's discipline on a busy day. A habit practised by a community is robust, because each person's checking catches errors others missed, and because the community's expectation of checking makes skipping it feel unusual, rather than normal.

Finally, remember why this habit matters, beyond any single tool or chapter. AI tools are increasingly used to learn, decide, and create, and the information they provide shapes what people believe and do. Builders who reduce hallucination, honestly and consistently, help create an information environment where readers can trust checked outputs, question unchecked ones, and learn effectively, rather than an environment flooded with fluent, confident inventions that erode trust in all information. That contribution, made one checked summary, one grounded answer, and one honest refusal at a time, is the lasting value of this chapter's work, and it is a habit worth keeping for every tool you build from now on.
