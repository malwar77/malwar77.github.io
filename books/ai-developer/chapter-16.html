Section 1: Why One Big Jump Often Fails

This section is about why one big jump often fails. Jumping straight from a hard question to an answer leaves no trail, so when the answer is wrong you cannot tell which part of your thinking failed. You already meet this idea in everyday life. like guessing the total of a long shopping list without adding anything up. The goal here is not speed but clarity. When you can say each part of a job out loud, in order, you can spot mistakes early, explain your thinking to another person, and fix one part without throwing the whole job away. Keep this picture in mind as you read: small pieces, in order, each one checked, leading to an answer you can trust.

A chain is nothing fancy. It is a short list of steps where the answer of one step becomes the starting point of the next. You do this whenever you cook from a recipe: first you gather the ingredients, then you prepare them, then you cook them, and only then do you serve the meal. Nobody tries to do all four at once. In a program the same idea holds. Each step should be small enough that you can test it on its own, and its result should be written down in a clear variable so the next step can use it without guessing.

The hardest part of why one big jump often fails is usually deciding where one step ends and the next begins. A good rule is that a step should do one job and produce one visible result. If you find yourself writing a step that does three different things, split it. Splitting feels slower at first, but it pays off the moment something goes wrong, because you can point to the exact step that failed instead of staring at a whole answer and wondering. Small steps also make your work easier to explain to another person.

Every chain needs a way to check itself as it goes. After each step, ask one simple question: does this result make sense? A shopping total should not be negative. A count of days should not be larger than the days in a month. These quick sense checks catch silly slips before they poison later steps. In code, a sense check can be a printed value or a short test with a known example. You do not need fancy tools. You need the discipline to look at each middle result before you trust the final one.

Before you move on, try this on paper. Take a tiny task from your own day and write it as three steps, labelling what each step produces. If any step still feels fuzzy, split it once more. That habit, small steps with visible results, is why one big jump often fails in action, and it is exactly the skill the next section builds on.

This section is about naming each intermediate result. Giving each middle result a clear name turns a fog of numbers into a story you can read back and check. Order matters in a chain because later steps depend on earlier ones. You cannot work out a discount before you know the price, and you cannot choose the best option before you have listed the options. If you ever feel stuck, list what each step needs and what it produces, then sort them so that everything is ready before it is used. Drawing the steps as a simple numbered list on paper is often enough. Many confusing problems become simple once the order is written down plainly.

Mistakes in a chain are normal, and a good chain expects them. When a middle result looks wrong, you do not throw the whole chain away. You walk backwards, step by step, until you find the first result that looks wrong, and you fix the step that produced it. This is far cheaper than starting again, and it teaches you something about the problem each time. Programs that keep every middle result make this walk backwards easy. Programs that hide their middle results make it painful.

Long chains are not always better than short ones. A chain should be as long as the problem needs and no longer. If a step adds no new information, remove it. If two steps always run together and are easy to understand as one, combine them. The goal is a chain that a beginner could follow with a pencil: each step obvious, each result labelled, each check quick. When a chain grows past about ten steps, consider grouping some steps into a named helper, so the main chain stays readable.

The best way to learn naming each intermediate result is to practise on tiny problems where you already know the answer. Add up a real shopping list by hand, then by chain, and compare. Plan a real journey, step by step, and check each step against a map. Tiny problems teach the habit without the stress of a hard problem, and the habit transfers. Once chaining feels natural on easy problems, you can trust it on harder ones, because the method stays the same even when the problem grows.
