Section 1: Why Testing Comes Before Trust

This section is about why testing comes before trust, one piece of testing a small AI tool you built yourself, on your own local examples. It is worth slowing down here, because this piece turns up again and again in later work, and a clear understanding now will save you confusion when the tool grows. At its simplest, why testing comes before trust is about making your intention clear: what should go in, what should come out, and what the tool should do when something is missing or unclear. The computer will follow your instructions literally, so vague thinking becomes a visible mistake, while clear thinking becomes a program that behaves as you expected. That is why the examples in this chapter stay small. With only a few lines to watch, you can see cause and effect without distraction, and you can trace exactly how each part contributes to the result. To understand why testing comes before trust, start from the problem it solves rather than from the code. Ask yourself what a person would do by hand, slowly, with a pencil and a single page. Then ask which part of that hand work is repetitive, easy to describe, and safe to automate. The parts that are repetitive are good candidates for code. The parts that need judgement, taste, or knowledge of the wider world are parts where the tool should be careful, show its working, or ask the person to decide. A good local tool does not pretend to know more than it does. It does one job, it shows what it did, and it leaves the final decision with the person using it. That honesty is not a weakness. It is what makes a small tool trustworthy enough to use every day. Imagine a shopping list, a garden notebook, or a set of revision notes that only you need to understand. Now apply why testing comes before trust to that picture. First, decide what the input is: a file, a folder, a short piece of text, or a list you typed yourself. Next, decide what the output should look like: a shorter text, a list of matching files, a tidy task list, or a simple yes-or-no answer about whether a test passed. Then work through one example completely by hand, writing down each step before you write any code. If you cannot do the step by hand, you do not yet understand it well enough to automate it, and that is a useful signal. Go back, simplify the step, or split it into two smaller steps until the hand version feels obvious. Only then should you turn the steps into Python. Beginners often stumble on why testing comes before trust for the same few reasons, and naming those reasons in advance makes them easier to avoid. One common problem is building too much at once, which makes it impossible to tell which new part caused a new result. Another is testing only with a perfect example, which hides what happens with an empty file, a very long file, or a file with unusual characters.
