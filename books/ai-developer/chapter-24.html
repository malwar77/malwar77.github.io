Chapter 24: Privacy and Data Safety for AI Builders

Section 1: Privacy in Plain Words

Privacy, in plain words, means that people control who knows what about them, and what is done with that knowledge. It is not about having something to hide; it is about having a say. A person may happily share a phone number with a friend, yet reasonably object to that number being shared with strangers, stored forever, or used to sort and judge them. Privacy is therefore about control, context, and consent: the same information can be fine to share in one context, with one person, for one purpose, and a violation in another context, with other people, for another purpose. Builders who understand privacy as control, rather than as secrecy, design tools that respect that control at every step.

For AI builders, privacy matters because tools collect, store, copy, and use data, often in ways users do not see. A note summariser reads personal notes. A search tool indexes personal files. A classifier learns from personal messages. Each of those actions involves someone's information, even when the tool runs locally and never sends data anywhere, because the tool's builder, tester, or future user may see data the original author never intended to share. Privacy work for local tools is therefore not about preventing internet transmission alone; it is about controlling access, copying, retention, and use, even on one machine, among people who share it, or who may later receive the tool or its test data.

Privacy also matters because data, once shared, is hard to unshare. A note copied into a test set, a summary shared with a team, or a file included in a project folder can be copied again, forwarded, or stored in backups, long after the original author forgot it existed. That persistence means privacy decisions made early, at collection and storage, have long-lasting effects, while privacy decisions made late, after sharing, often come too late to undo exposure. Builders should therefore think about privacy at the start, when data is first collected, not at the end, when it is already widely copied, because early care prevents exposures that late care cannot reverse.

Finally, privacy is linked to trust, fairness, and safety, as in Chapter 23. Users who trust that a builder will handle their data carefully are more willing to use a tool, to contribute test cases, and to report problems, which improves the tool for everyone. Users who discover their data was used without consent, stored insecurely, or shared beyond its purpose will stop trusting that builder, and may stop trusting similar tools altogether, which harms the whole community of builders. Privacy, practised consistently, is therefore not a burden on building; it is a foundation for building tools people will actually use, contribute to, and recommend, because they know their control over their own information will be respected.

Section 2: What Counts as Personal Data

Personal data is any information that relates to an identifiable person, directly or indirectly, whether it names them or not. Obvious examples include names, phone numbers, email addresses, and home addresses. Less obvious examples include a distinctive writing style, a rare combination of details, a photo, a voice recording, a location history, or a set of notes that, taken together, reveal who someone is, what they do, where they go, or what they think. Builders often underestimate what counts as personal, because they focus on names alone, while forgetting that indirect details, combined, can identify a person just as surely as a name, especially in small groups where few people share a particular combination.

Sensitive personal data deserves extra care. It includes information about health, finances, beliefs, political views, and other intimate aspects of life, where exposure can cause embarrassment, discrimination, or harm, even if the information itself is not secret in a strict sense. A health note, a financial record, or a private opinion, shared without consent, can harm its author in ways that a shopping list would not, because it touches areas where people reasonably expect stronger control. Builders should therefore treat sensitive data with higher protection: collect it only when genuinely needed, store it more securely, share it more narrowly, and delete it sooner, than ordinary data, and they should ask, for each sensitive item, whether the task truly requires it, or whether a non-sensitive example could serve the same purpose.

Data that seems anonymous may still be personal, as Chapter 23 warned for anonymisation. A note with names removed, but with distinctive dates, places, and details intact, may still identify its author to anyone who knows them, or who can combine it with other public information. A set of messages with names replaced by codes may still reveal its authors through writing style, topic, or timing. Builders should therefore treat "anonymised" data as potentially personal, unless anonymisation has been tested and shown to prevent re-identification for the intended sharing context, which is a high standard, especially for small, distinctive datasets. When in doubt, treat data as personal, and protect it accordingly, rather than assuming anonymisation has solved the problem.

Finally, personal data includes data about people other than the tool's user. A user's notes may mention friends, family, colleagues, or clients, whose information the user does not own and cannot consent to share on their behalf. A builder who uses a user's notes as test data, without considering the third parties mentioned in them, may expose those third parties without their knowledge or consent. Responsible builders therefore check test data and examples not only for the main author's personal details, but also for details about others mentioned in them, and they remove, replace, or obtain consent for those details before sharing, because privacy protects everyone mentioned in data, not just the person who provided it.

Section 3: Data You Collect Without Realising

Builders often collect more data than they intend, because tools, by default, save logs, copies, temporary files, and metadata that contain personal information, even when the builder never asked for it. A summariser might save the full original note alongside its summary, in a log file, long after the summary was delivered. A search tool might save every query a user typed, revealing what they were looking for, when, and how often. A test script might save full outputs, including private details from test notes, in a results file that is later shared as evidence of performance. Each of these accidental collections creates a privacy risk that the builder never consciously accepted, because they never realised the data was being collected at all.

Metadata is a common hidden collection. A file's metadata can include its author, creation date, location, device, and editing history, even when the file's visible content seems harmless. A photo's metadata can include where and when it was taken, and with what device, which may reveal far more than the image itself. A document's tracked changes or comments can include earlier drafts, deleted text, or private notes that were never intended for sharing. Builders who share files without checking metadata may therefore share personal information they never intended to share, and recipients who check metadata may learn things the sender never meant to reveal. Checking metadata before sharing, and removing it where it is not needed, is a simple, high-value privacy habit.

Temporary and backup copies are another hidden collection. Operating systems, editors, and tools often create temporary files, autosave copies, and backups automatically, to protect against crashes, and those copies may persist after the original is deleted or edited. A builder who deletes a sensitive note, but leaves its autosave copy, its backup copy, and its log copy in place, has not actually deleted it, and anyone with access to those copies can still read it. Deleting data safely, as in Section 10, therefore requires finding and deleting copies, not just the main file, and builders should periodically search their project folders for temporary, backup, and log files that may contain personal data they forgot about.

Logs deserve special attention, as in Section 12, because logs are often written automatically, shared for debugging, and kept indefinitely, without anyone reviewing their contents for personal data. A log that records full inputs, full outputs, or file paths containing personal names can expose a great deal, especially when logs are shared with collaborators or posted publicly for help. Builders should therefore design logs to record as little personal data as possible, such as counts and error types rather than full text, and should review logs before sharing them, removing personal details, as they would review any other shared file. A log is a data collection, even when it feels like a technical by-product, and it should be treated as such, with the same privacy care as any intentional collection.

Section 4: Consent That Means Something

Consent means a person agrees, freely and with understanding, to a specific use of their data, for a specific purpose, and it is the foundation of responsible data collection, as in Chapter 23. Consent that means something has several qualities. It is informed: the person knows what data will be used, how, by whom, for how long, and what risks are involved, in plain language, not in vague or technical terms they cannot reasonably understand. It is specific: it covers a particular use, not a blanket permission for any future use the builder might think of later. It is freely given: the person can refuse without penalty, pressure, or loss of a service they need, and they can withdraw consent later, with their data then deleted or removed from future use, as far as practical.

Consent lacking those qualities is not meaningful, even if someone clicked "agree" in a hurry. A jargon-filled agreement no one reads does not inform. A blanket "any purpose" permission does not specify. Consent under pressure is not freely given. Builders relying on hollow consent may believe they have permission, while providers believed they agreed to something narrower, and that mismatch destroys trust. Meaningful consent requires effort from the builder: clear explanations, specific purposes, genuine choice, and an easy withdrawal path, all in plain language, before data is used, not after.

For local tools, consent is usually direct and personal, making meaningful consent easier than at large scale. Ask a friend directly, explaining what you want to use, for what purpose, who will see it, and how long you will keep it, and accept a "no" gracefully. That direct conversation, recorded briefly, such as a note saying "Ada agreed on 12 May for summariser testing only, delete after June," is more meaningful than a formal form, because it is specific, understood, and revocable, and because it respects the provider as a person, not as a data source to be harvested. Keep that record with the dataset, so future builders know the consent basis, as in Section 32 of Chapter 23.

Also consider third parties mentioned in data, as in Section 2. A note's author cannot consent for them, so builders should remove those details, obtain consent where feasible, or use synthetic replacements preserving the pattern without real details. That step is essential, because third parties have the same right to control their information, even though they never spoke to the builder. Ignoring third-party consent, because it is inconvenient, is a common privacy failure in small projects, where builders focus on the person in front of them and forget the people mentioned in their data.

Finally, revisit consent when purpose, sharing, or retention changes. Consent for testing does not cover publishing cases, sharing with a wider team, or keeping them indefinitely, and expanding use without re-asking exceeds consent received, even if the new use seems harmless. When a purpose changes, ask again, explain the new use, and accept a refusal, by either excluding that data from the new use or by finding an alternative, such as synthetic data, that does not require new consent. That habit, re-asking when purpose changes, keeps consent meaningful over time, rather than letting it decay into a one-time permission that is stretched far beyond what was originally agreed.

Section 5: Minimisation: Collect Less

Minimisation means collecting only the data you actually need, for the task at hand, and no more, and it is one of the simplest and most powerful privacy practices. Every piece of data you collect creates a risk: it must be stored safely, it may be copied, it may be shared by mistake, and it may be exposed in a breach or a leak. Data you never collect creates no such risk, costs nothing to protect, and cannot be misused, because it does not exist in your project. Minimisation therefore reduces privacy risk at its source, by reducing the amount of personal data in play, rather than by adding ever-stronger protections around an ever-larger pile of data that did not need to be collected in the first place.

Practising minimisation starts by asking, for each piece planned, "do I need this for this task, or just in case?" If just in case, do not collect it, or use a non-personal substitute, such as a synthetic example or count, serving the same testing purpose. For example, to test whether a summariser handles dates, you do not need real dates from real people's notes; you can use invented dates in synthetic notes, which test the same skill without exposing anyone's real schedule. To test whether a classifier handles short messages, you do not need real short messages from friends; you can write short synthetic messages yourself, in varied styles, which test the same robustness without using anyone's private writing.

Minimisation also applies to retention and copies. Data kept longer than needed, or copied to more places than needed, creates more exposure opportunities, even if collection was justified. A test set kept for a month then deleted is more minimised than one kept indefinitely "just in case," because indefinite retention turns a temporary need into a permanent risk. Similarly, one secure folder is more minimised than copies across several folders and backups, each needing protection and eventual deletion. Minimisation is therefore about quantity, duration, and copies, not just about initial collection.

Builders sometimes resist minimisation, fearing worse tools, but for local tools it often improves quality too. A small, purposeful test set, from your own files and a few consented examples, is easier to audit for bias, check for hallucinations, and maintain than a large, messy collection whose consent basis is unclear. Minimised data is also easier to share safely and to delete, with less personal material and fewer copies to track. Quality, in local building, comes from relevance and variety, not from sheer volume, and minimisation encourages builders to choose relevant, varied examples deliberately, rather than collecting indiscriminately.

Finally, make minimisation a habit by reviewing data regularly, asking for each dataset, "do I still need this, in this form, for this purpose?" If not, delete it, or replace it with aggregate counts or synthetic examples preserving testing value without personal content. That regular review, like the fairness and accuracy reviews in earlier chapters, keeps data holdings lean, current, and justifiable, rather than allowing them to grow by inertia into a privacy liability. A builder who collects less, keeps less, and copies less will have fewer privacy problems to solve, because they will have created fewer opportunities for privacy to fail in the first place.

Section 6: Storing Data Safely Locally

Storing data safely locally starts with knowing where your data lives. Personal notes, test sets, logs, and backups are often scattered across many folders: a project folder, a downloads folder, a desktop, a shared drive, and temporary folders created by editors and tools. That scattering makes protection hard, because you cannot secure what you cannot find, and it makes deletion hard, because copies hide in places you forgot. The first step in safe local storage is therefore to consolidate personal data into a small number of known folders, with clear names, and to keep a simple inventory, such as a text file listing which folders contain personal data, for what purpose, and until when. That inventory, updated when folders change, turns scattered data into managed data.

Next, control who can access those folders. On a shared computer, or a computer others may borrow, use separate user accounts where possible, so that other users cannot casually open your personal folders. On your own computer, use the operating system's file permissions to restrict sensitive folders, where your system supports it, and avoid placing personal data in shared, public, or synced folders unless you intend it to be shared or synced. Synced folders deserve special care: a folder synced to a cloud service, or to another device, is no longer local-only, even if it feels local, because its contents are copied elsewhere, with that service's protections and risks. Before placing personal data in a synced folder, ask whether syncing is needed, and if not, keep the data in a non-synced folder instead.

Also protect the computer itself, with basic habits that cost little but prevent much harm. Use a screen lock, so that an unattended computer does not expose personal data to passers-by. Keep your operating system and tools updated, because updates often fix security flaws that could expose files. Do not leave personal data open in editors or browsers when you step away, and close files containing sensitive information when you finish with them, rather than leaving them open indefinitely. Those habits may feel like common sense, but they are the most common points of failure in local data safety, because builders focus on sophisticated threats while overlooking the simple risk of an unlocked screen or an open file on a shared machine.

Finally, plan for loss and theft, as well as for unauthorised access. A laptop can be lost, stolen, or damaged, and if its personal data is unprotected, that loss becomes a privacy breach, not just a hardware loss. Back up important, non-sensitive work regularly, as in Section 13, but think carefully before backing up sensitive personal data: a backup is another copy, with its own risks, and it should be protected as carefully as the original, and deleted when no longer needed, as in Section 10. For highly sensitive data, consider whether it needs to be stored at all, as in Section 5, rather than stored and then protected, because the safest storage for data you do not need is no storage. Safe local storage is therefore a combination of consolidation, access control, basic computer hygiene, and minimisation, practised consistently, not a single tool or setting that solves privacy on its own.

Section 7: Passwords and Access for Local Tools

Access control for local tools means deciding who can run a tool, open its data, and change its settings, and then enforcing those decisions with the simplest effective means available. For most local tools in this book, access control will be simple, because the tools run on your own machine, for your own use, and the main access question is whether other people who share or borrow the machine can open your personal folders or run tools that read them. That question deserves a deliberate answer, not a default assumption that "my computer is private," because shared machines, borrowed laptops, and family computers are common, and because a tool that reads personal files will expose those files to anyone who can run it, unless access is controlled.

Start with the computer's own controls. Use a login password or PIN, and separate accounts for different users, so files are separated by the system, not trust alone. Those controls protect all files, not just one tool's data, and are enforced by the operating system, harder to bypass than a tool-level check. If your computer does not support separate accounts, or if you must share an account, be extra careful about where personal data lives, as in Section 6, and avoid leaving sensitive files in shared folders or on the desktop, where any account user can find them.

For tools themselves, avoid building your own password system unless genuinely needed, because home-made checks are easy to get wrong and create false security. Instead, rely on operating-system access control, and design tools to read only needed folders, not an entire home directory, so a tool run by someone else cannot easily reach unrelated files. That principle, least access, meaning a tool accesses only what its task requires, is a powerful privacy and safety practice, and it is easy to implement in local Python tools, by passing explicit folder paths, rather than defaulting to broad searches.

Also be careful about secrets in general, even though this book's tools need no external services or keys. Never write passwords or private details into code, comments, test files, or shared examples, because code is often shared and forgotten, while secrets in it persist and spread. If a tool ever needs a secret for a legitimate local purpose, such as unlocking a personal file, prefer the operating system's secure storage, where available, or prompt for it at run time without saving it, rather than storing it in a file alongside the tool. And never share a tool folder that contains a secret, even if you believe you removed it, without searching the folder for secret-like strings first, because secrets hide in logs, backups, and old versions, as in Section 3.

Finally, review access periodically. Ask who recently used your computer, who accesses shared folders, and whether any tool's access exceeds its task, and tighten where you find excess. Like data review, access review keeps permissions lean and current. A local tool with least access, running under a protected user account, on a locked computer, with no secrets in its code, is a well-controlled tool, and that control is the practical meaning of access safety for local builders, without needing complex or expensive systems.

Section 8: Sharing Data Safely

Sharing data safely means giving others only what they need, for the purpose they agreed to, in a form that minimises exposure, and with clear expectations about further sharing. It starts before sharing, with a check: what does this dataset contain, who is mentioned in it, what consent covers it, and what could go wrong if it spreads beyond its intended recipients? That pre-sharing check, similar to Chapter 22's pre-sharing check for hallucinations, catches personal details, third-party mentions, and sensitive content that should be removed, replaced with synthetic examples, or covered by new consent before sharing, rather than discovered after sharing, when removal is often impossible because recipients have already copied it.

Next, minimise what you share, as in Section 5. Share aggregate results, such as scores and breakdowns, rather than raw personal examples, wherever aggregate results serve the purpose, such as demonstrating performance or fairness. Share synthetic examples, built to show a pattern without real personal details, rather than real examples, wherever a pattern is what matters, such as a spelling variation or a format. Share only the subset of real data that is genuinely needed, rather than an entire folder, when a subset will do, and remove columns, fields, or details that are not needed for the recipient's purpose, even if they seem harmless in isolation, because combined details can identify people, as in Section 2. Minimised sharing reduces risk for both sharer and recipient, because there is simply less personal material to protect, misuse, or leak.

Also control how data is shared, not just what is shared. Prefer sharing through a channel that limits further copying, where possible, such as a direct transfer to a named recipient, rather than a public link that anyone can forward, and state, in plain language, what recipients may and may not do with the data: may they copy it, share it further, keep it indefinitely, or use it for other purposes? Those expectations, agreed in advance, do not technically prevent misuse, but they set a clear standard, they give recipients a reason to handle data carefully, and they give sharers a basis for asking for deletion or correction if expectations are violated. For highly sensitive data, consider whether sharing is needed at all, or whether a recipient can instead run a test on their own data, using your script, without ever receiving your personal examples, which is often the safest sharing of all: share the method, not the data.

Finally, follow up after sharing. Keep a record of what was shared, with whom, when, and for what purpose, so that if consent is withdrawn, if a risk is discovered, or if retention expires, you can ask recipients to delete their copies, as far as practical. That follow-up may not guarantee deletion, because copies may already have spread, but it is far better than forgetting a sharing ever happened, which guarantees that outdated or withdrawn data will persist indefinitely. Responsible sharing is therefore a process, not a single send: check before, minimise during, set expectations at sharing, and follow up after, with records that let you act if circumstances change. A builder who shares that way protects data providers, recipients, and themselves, by making sharing deliberate, limited, and accountable.

Section 9: Anonymisation and Its Limits

Anonymisation means removing or altering personal details from data, so that individuals cannot be identified from it, and it is often proposed as a way to share data safely without consent or risk. In practice, anonymisation is harder than it sounds, and its limits matter greatly for builders, because data that looks anonymous may still identify people, especially in small, distinctive datasets, as in Section 2. Understanding those limits prevents builders from sharing supposedly anonymous data that actually exposes people, while believing they have protected them, which is one of the most common and most harmful privacy mistakes in small projects.

Simple anonymisation, such as removing names and replacing them with codes, is often insufficient, because other details in the data can still identify a person, alone or in combination. A note that mentions a rare job, a specific date, a distinctive place, and a unique event may identify its author to anyone who knows them, even without a name, because few people share that combination. A set of messages with names removed may still reveal authors through writing style, vocabulary, timing, or topics, especially if the set is small or if authors write distinctively. Builders who rely on name removal alone, without considering other identifying details, are therefore likely to under-protect their data, while over-trusting their anonymisation.

Stronger techniques, such as generalising dates to months or places to regions, suppressing rare details, and aggregating results, can reduce identification risk, but also reduce usefulness, because generalised data loses specific patterns testing may need. That trade-off, privacy versus usefulness, must be judged for each sharing purpose: if generalised data still tests the needed pattern, such as date handling in general, then generalisation is a good choice. If only exact details test the pattern, such as a specific date format, then anonymisation may destroy the test's value, and synthetic data, built to mimic the pattern without real details, may be a better choice than weakly anonymised real data, because synthetic data carries no identification risk at all, however specific its pattern.

Testing anonymisation is essential, yet often skipped. Before sharing, try to re-identify it yourself, or ask a trusted colleague to try, using only anonymised data plus public information. If re-identification succeeds, or seems plausible, the anonymisation is insufficient for public sharing, and stronger generalisation, aggregation, or synthetic replacement is needed. That re-identification test, done honestly, is the only reliable way to judge anonymisation, because intuition about what "looks anonymous" is notoriously unreliable, especially for builders who already know the original data and therefore cannot judge what a stranger would infer from the anonymised version alone.

Finally, treat anonymised data as still potentially personal, unless re-identification testing shows otherwise for the specific sharing context, and apply the same care as for personal data: limit sharing, set expectations, keep records, and plan deletion, as in Section 8. Anonymisation, done well, reduces risk, but it rarely eliminates it entirely, and claiming it does, without testing, misleads recipients into handling data carelessly, which increases risk further. Honest anonymisation practice therefore states its method, its limits, and its re-identification test results, rather than merely labelling data "anonymous" and assuming the label alone protects anyone. That honesty is what makes anonymisation a useful privacy tool, rather than a false reassurance.

Section 10: Deleting Data Properly

Deleting data properly means removing it and its copies so future access is prevented, not merely hiding it from view. Many builders believe they have deleted data when they have only moved it to a trash folder, removed its main file while leaving backups, or deleted a database row while leaving logs that contain the same information. Those partial deletions create a false sense of safety, while the data remains accessible to anyone who knows where copies hide, which is why proper deletion requires a systematic approach, covering main files, copies, backups, logs, and shared versions, as far as practical, rather than a single delete action on a single file.

Start deletion with an inventory, as in Section 6: where does this data live, in what forms, and who else has copies? List main, backup, temporary, log, derived, and shared copies, because each must be addressed. Then delete each copy and empty trash folders, where data would otherwise persist, because trash is temporary storage many forget to empty and others can restore. For highly sensitive data, consider whether your system's ordinary delete, which often merely marks space as reusable, is sufficient, or whether a secure deletion method, such as overwriting, is needed, depending on your threat model and your system's tools, and seek guidance for your system if unsure, rather than assuming ordinary delete is always enough.

Next, address shared copies. If data was shared, ask recipients to delete theirs, explaining why, and record responses. Shared deletion cannot be guaranteed, because recipients may have copied further, but asking is far better than forgetting, because it reduces copies in circulation and shows providers their request was taken seriously. If a recipient cannot or will not delete, note that limit honestly to the data provider, rather than claiming full deletion when it has not been achieved, because false deletion claims mislead providers about their privacy in ways that are hard to undo.

Also delete derived data where appropriate. A test set from personal notes, a summary quoting personal details, or a log recording personal inputs may all need deletion when the original is deleted, because they carry the same information in a new form. Builders who delete originals but keep derivatives have not actually deleted the personal data; they have merely moved it to a less obvious place, where it may be forgotten and later shared as if it were safe. Deletion planning should therefore include derivatives, with a note of which derivatives were deleted, which were kept in anonymised or synthetic form, and why, so that future builders can see what was done and what remains.

Finally, verify deletion by searching for distinctive details, such as a rare name or phrase, across project folders after deletion, to confirm no obvious copy remains. That search will not guarantee no hidden copy exists, but it catches forgotten copies in temporary or backup folders, where most failed deletions hide. Record the deletion, with date, scope, and verification result, in your data inventory, so that future questions about that data can be answered honestly, with evidence, rather than with a vague memory that it was "probably deleted." Proper deletion, done systematically and verified, is the final act of responsible data stewardship, and it deserves the same care as collection and storage.

Section 11: Data in Test Sets and Examples

Test sets and examples are a special privacy risk, because they are often copied, shared, and kept long after the tool they tested is forgotten, and because they are written to be realistic, which often means they contain real personal details. A test set built from real notes, messages, or records, without careful checking, can expose the people in those records every time the test set is shared, run, or stored in a new place. Builders should therefore treat test data with the same privacy care as any other personal data, not as a technical artefact exempt from privacy rules simply because it is used for testing rather than for production.

Start test-set privacy by choosing test data deliberately, as in Section 5. Prefer your own files, synthetic examples you write yourself, and consented examples from volunteers, over real data copied from others without clear permission. Synthetic examples are especially valuable for testing, because you can design them to cover exactly the patterns you need, such as dates, names, lists, or edge cases, without exposing anyone's real details, and because you can share synthetic test sets freely, without privacy risk, which makes evaluation and fairness work easier to share and replicate, as in Chapter 21. A test set that is mostly synthetic, with a small number of consented real cases where realness genuinely matters, is both safer and more shareable than a test set built entirely from real personal data.

Next, check every test case before you store or share it, for personal details about the author and about third parties mentioned, as in Section 2. Remove names, contact details, sensitive content, and distinctive combinations that could identify someone, or replace them with invented equivalents that preserve the testing pattern, such as replacing a real date with an invented date in the same format. Keep a note, with the test set, stating its source, its consent basis, and what was removed or replaced, so that future users know what the test set contains and what privacy steps were taken, rather than assuming it is safe to share because it was labelled as a test set. That note is part of responsible test-set documentation, like the transparency notes in Chapter 23.

Finally, store and share test sets with the same controls as other personal data, even after checking, because checking is rarely perfect, and because test sets accumulate copies over time, as in Sections 6 and 13. Store test sets in known, access-controlled folders, not in public or synced folders unless they are fully synthetic and safe to share. Share test sets only with recipients who need them, for a stated purpose, with expectations about further sharing and deletion, as in Section 8. And delete test sets, or replace them with synthetic versions, when their testing purpose ends, as in Section 10, rather than keeping them indefinitely "just in case," because indefinite test-set retention is a common, overlooked privacy liability in otherwise careful projects.

Section 12: Logs That Leak

Logs are records a tool writes about its own operation, such as what it did, when, with what inputs, and what errors occurred, and they leak personal data when they record full inputs, full outputs, file paths, or other details that reveal private information. Logs leak because they are written automatically, often without the builder reviewing their contents, kept indefinitely, and shared casually for debugging, as in Section 3, which means personal data in logs can spread far beyond its original context, without anyone consciously deciding to share it. A builder who would never share a personal note directly may nevertheless share a log containing that note's full text, simply because they did not realise the log recorded it, which is why log design deserves explicit privacy attention.

Design logs to record as little personal data as possible, while still serving their debugging purpose. Instead of logging full inputs and outputs, log counts, types, and error categories, such as "summarised 1 note, 3 key facts, no errors," rather than the note's text itself. Instead of logging full file paths that contain personal names, log relative paths or file categories, such as "note file" rather than a path including a person's name. Instead of logging every query a user typed, log query length, or query category, if that serves the debugging purpose, rather than the query text, which may reveal what the user was seeking. Those minimised logs, as in Section 5, still help debugging, because they show what kinds of operations succeeded or failed, without exposing the personal content of those operations.

Also control log storage and retention. Store logs in the same access-controlled folders as other personal data, not in public or shared folders, and set a retention period, after which logs are deleted automatically or by a scheduled review, rather than kept forever. Old logs are a common leak source, because they accumulate personal data over months, are forgotten, and are then shared or copied as part of a project folder, without anyone reviewing their contents. A simple habit, reviewing and deleting logs monthly, or whenever a project is shared, catches most log leaks before they spread, and it costs far less than dealing with a leak after sharing, when recipients may already have copied the logs further.

Finally, review logs before sharing them, as you would review any shared file. Search a log for personal details, such as names, dates, phone-like numbers, and distinctive phrases, and remove or replace them before sending the log to a collaborator, posting it for help, or including it in a project archive. If a log contains personal data that cannot be removed without destroying its debugging value, consider whether sharing is needed at all, or whether a minimised summary of the log, such as error counts and types, would serve the recipient's purpose without the personal content. Sharing a log summary, rather than a raw log, is often the safest sharing of all, because it gives collaborators what they need to help, without giving them personal data they do not need and should not receive.

Section 13: Backups and Copies

Backups are copies made to protect against loss, and they are essential for important work, but they also multiply privacy risk, because every backup is another copy of personal data, in another place, with its own access controls, retention, and deletion challenges. A builder who carefully protects an original file, but backs it up to a shared drive, a synced folder, or an external device without the same protections, has not actually protected the data; they have merely moved the risk to a less obvious place, where it may be forgotten and later exposed. Backup privacy therefore requires the same care as original storage, applied to every copy, with a clear inventory of where backups live and when they will be deleted.

Start backup planning by deciding what needs backing up, and what does not. Important, non-sensitive work, such as code, synthetic test sets, and documentation, should be backed up regularly, because losing it would waste effort and harm progress. Sensitive personal data, such as real notes, messages, or test cases containing personal details, should be backed up only if genuinely needed, and only with protections matching the original, such as access-controlled storage and a deletion plan, as in Section 6. Data that is not needed, as in Section 5, should not be backed up at all, because backing up unnecessary personal data creates risk without benefit, and because a backup of data you do not need is a liability you created for no reason.

Next, inventory backups. List each location, contents, access, and review date, in the same inventory as main stores. That prevents forgotten backups, where an original is deleted believing data is gone, while a backup persists for years in a synced folder or old device. Review the inventory regularly, and delete backups that are no longer needed, or that contain data whose retention has expired, as in Section 10, so that backup holdings stay lean, current, and justifiable, rather than growing by inertia into a large, unmonitored store of personal data.

Also consider backup security practically. A lost or stolen external backup can expose contents, just as a lost laptop can, so protect external backups as carefully as the original device, keeping them in a secure place. A backup in a synced folder, as in Section 6, is no longer local-only, and its protections depend on the sync service, which the builder may not fully control, so sensitive data should not be placed in synced backup folders unless syncing is genuinely needed and its risks have been considered. Those practical considerations, device security and sync awareness, are more important for most local builders than sophisticated backup technology, because most backup leaks come from simple oversights, not from advanced attacks.

Finally, test restoration, but test it safely. A backup that cannot be restored is useless, so periodically check that important backups can be restored, using non-sensitive test data where possible, rather than restoring sensitive personal data unnecessarily, which would create yet another copy. Restoration testing, done with care, ensures backups serve their purpose, protecting against loss, without creating new privacy risks through unnecessary copying. Backups, managed this way, with selective copying, inventory, secure storage, regular deletion, and safe restoration testing, protect work without multiplying privacy risk beyond what is justified by the work's importance.

Section 14: Data Safety for Vulnerable Users

Vulnerable users face higher risk from data exposure than typical users, because of circumstances such as age, health, or safety situation, and builders should give their data extra protection, even without knowing exactly who they are. A child using a learning tool, or someone in an unsafe situation, may be harmed far more by a leak than a typical user, because leaked information could embarrass, discriminate, or endanger them, in ways hard to undo. Builders cannot always identify vulnerable users, and should not try to, by collecting sensitive status information, but they can design tools with extra care by default, so that vulnerable users are protected even when they are not identified as such.

One extra-care practice is minimisation for all users, as in Section 5, because it protects vulnerable users most, ensuring less sensitive material leaks even if a leak occurs. Another is avoiding data revealing vulnerability, such as health details or location, unless genuinely needed, replacing them with general categories or synthetic examples where possible. A learning tool, for example, does not need a learner's health details to summarise study notes, and a task helper does not need a user's exact location to sort tasks, so collecting those details would create vulnerability risk without task benefit, which is a poor trade that responsible builders should refuse, even if users offer the data freely, without understanding the risk.

Another practice is private use by default. Local tools running on the user's own machine, without sending data elsewhere, are inherently safer for vulnerable users than tools transmitting data externally, because transmission creates exposure points beyond user control. This book's local-first design, using only the standard library and the user's own files, is therefore a vulnerability-protecting design, not just a simplicity choice, and builders should preserve it, by resisting pressure to add external transmission, cloud syncing, or sharing features, unless those features are genuinely needed and their risks have been carefully considered and disclosed to users, especially vulnerable ones who may not understand the change in risk.

Also consider how outputs could expose vulnerability. A summary repeating sensitive details, or a shared example containing a vulnerable person's story, can expose vulnerability even when inputs were handled carefully, because outputs are often shared more widely than inputs. Builders should therefore check outputs for sensitive details before sharing, as in Section 8, and should label outputs that contain sensitive material, so recipients handle them with extra care, rather than forwarding them casually. Output checking is especially important for vulnerable users, because output exposure is often the moment when private information actually reaches people who should not see it.

Finally, when in doubt, err on protection. If unsure whether data is sensitive, treat it as sensitive. If unsure whether sharing is safe, do not share, or share a minimised synthetic version. If unsure whether a user understands a risk, explain it plainly, and offer a safer local alternative. That precautionary approach may feel overly cautious for typical users, but it protects vulnerable users who cannot afford a mistake, and it builds trust with all users, who learn that the builder prioritises their safety over convenience or impressive features. Protecting the most vulnerable, by default, is the standard that makes a tool safe for everyone.

Section 15: When Data Leaves Your Machine

Data leaves your machine whenever sent, synced, shared, or copied elsewhere, and that moment changes its privacy risk fundamentally, because control passes partly to someone else, whose protections and retention you may not know or control. Local tools in this book keep data on your machine, a strong privacy advantage, lost the moment data is synced, emailed, posted publicly, or included in a shared project, even if sharing feels casual or temporary. Builders should therefore treat every departure of data from the machine as a privacy decision, to be made deliberately, with checks and minimisation, rather than as an automatic side effect of a convenient feature.

Common departure points deserve explicit attention. Synced folders copy data to another service and often to other devices automatically, without a per-file decision, so personal data placed there leaves the machine without conscious sharing. Email and messaging attachments copy data to recipients and to service providers, and may be stored, forwarded, or backed up beyond the recipient's control. Public sharing, such as posting an example, a test set, or a project folder online, copies data to an audience that cannot be recalled, even if the original post is later deleted, because others may have already copied it. Each departure point has different risks, but all share the same principle: once data leaves, you cannot fully control it again, so the decision to let it leave must be made carefully, before it leaves, not after.

Before data leaves, apply Section 8 checks: what does it contain, who is mentioned, what consent covers it, what could go wrong if it spreads, and is there a safer alternative, such as sharing a method or aggregate results instead of raw data? If raw data must leave, minimise it, anonymise or synthesise where possible, and set expectations about further sharing, retention, and deletion. Those steps do not make departure risk-free, but reduce it proportionately, rather than sharing raw data casually and hoping recipients handle it carefully, which they may not, even with good intentions.

Also keep a record of departures, stating what left, to whom, when, and why, so that if consent is withdrawn or retention expires, you can ask recipients to delete copies, as far as practical. Records also help audit sharing over time, revealing patterns needing stronger protections, or sharing no longer justified. A builder who cannot list where their data has gone cannot protect it, however careful they were at the moment of sharing, because protection after departure depends on knowing where copies live and who controls them.

Finally, preserve local-first design wherever possible, asking for each proposed departure, "is this genuinely needed, or can the task be done locally?" Often a collaborator can run your script on their own data, and a reviewer can check aggregate results instead of raw cases, without data leaving your machine. Choosing the local alternative, when it serves the purpose, is the safest departure decision of all: no departure, no new risk. Local-first, as in Section 38, is therefore not just a technical preference; it is a privacy strategy, one that keeps control with the data's owner, by default, and departs from that default only deliberately, minimally, and with records.

Section 16: Third-Party Tools and Risk

Third-party tools are programs, services, or libraries made by someone else, which you use as part of your own tool, and they introduce privacy risk because they may collect, store, or transmit data in ways you do not control and may not fully understand. This book's tools avoid that risk by design, using only Python's standard library and the user's own files, with no external services, no accounts, and no data transmission, which means there is no third party to trust with personal data in the first place. That local-first choice is a strong privacy protection, and builders should understand it as such, rather than treating it as a limitation to be overcome by adding third-party services whenever they seem convenient, because each added service is a new party with access to data, or at least to metadata about its use.

When you do consider a third-party tool, for a purpose outside this book's local tools, ask three questions before using it. First, what data does it collect, store, or transmit, including metadata such as file names, timestamps, or usage patterns, not just file contents? Second, who controls that data afterwards, how long is it kept, and can it be deleted on request? Third, what happens if the third party is breached, changes its practices, or shuts down, and who bears the harm then? If those questions cannot be answered clearly, from the tool's documentation or terms, in plain language, then using it for personal data is a risk you cannot honestly assess, and you should either avoid it for personal data, or use it only with minimised, synthetic data, as in Section 5, so that even if the third party mishandles data, no real personal information is exposed.

Also consider indirect risk, where a trusted tool uses another tool behind the scenes, such as a library sending usage statistics, a synced folder, or automatic backups. Those indirect parties may not be obvious, and may collect data without builder or user realising it, as in Section 3. Checking a tool's settings for syncing, telemetry, backups, and sharing, and turning off any such feature that is not needed for the task, is a practical privacy step that costs little but prevents silent data departure, as in Section 15. A tool that is private in its main function, but leaky through a background sync, is not a private tool, however good its main design may be.

Finally, document third-party decisions, as with other data decisions. Note which third-party tools, if any, your project uses, what data each can access, what protections it offers, and why its use was justified despite its risks, or why it was avoided. That documentation lets future builders, reviewers, and users judge whether third-party risk was considered, and it lets you revisit decisions when a third party's practices change, as they often do, without warning. For this book's tools, the documentation is simple and reassuring: no third-party services, standard library only, local files only, which is a privacy statement users can verify by reading the code, rather than by trusting a promise. That verifiability, no hidden transmission to check for, is itself a privacy benefit of local-first design, and it is worth preserving as you build more complex tools in future.

Section 17: Data Breaches: What to Do

A data breach means personal data has been accessed, shared, or exposed without authorisation, whether by an attacker, a mistake such as emailing the wrong recipient, or a lost device with unprotected files. Breaches feel alarming, and they are serious, but a calm, systematic response limits harm far more than panic or concealment, and builders should know the basic steps before a breach occurs, so they can act quickly and honestly when one does. The first step is always to stop further exposure: revoke access, remove a mistakenly shared file or link, or retrieve a lost device, if possible, before investigating further, because continued exposure increases harm, while investigation can proceed after containment.

Next, assess what was exposed, to whom, and what harm could follow. List the data, people mentioned, whether it included sensitive details, and who may now access it, whether one mistaken recipient, a public audience, or unknown. That brief assessment guides later steps, because a breach of aggregate synthetic data requires a very different response from one involving sensitive personal notes. Assessment should be honest about uncertainty too: if you do not know who accessed data, say so, rather than claiming it was only one person, when it may have been more.

Then tell affected people, promptly and plainly, what happened, what data was involved, what you have done to contain it, and what they can do to protect themselves. Notification should not be delayed to protect reputation, because delay leaves affected people exposed without knowledge, while they could have been taking protective steps. Notification language should be clear, specific, and free of jargon or minimising phrases like "minor incident" when the data involved was sensitive, because minimising language destroys trust, while honest, specific notification, even when embarrassing, builds trust by showing that affected people are being treated as partners in protection, not as a public relations problem to be managed.

Also fix the cause, so the breach does not recur. If it was a mistaken email, add a recipient check before sending sensitive files. If a shared folder with broad access, tighten access, as in Section 7. If a lost device, improve device protection, as in Sections 6 and 13. Record cause, containment, notification, and fix, so future builders learn, and affected people can see it was addressed systematically, not merely apologised for. A breach record, kept honestly, is evidence of accountability, as in Chapter 23, not an admission to be hidden.

Finally, learn from breaches in general, not just your own. Read about common breach causes, such as mistaken sharing, weak access, and forgotten backups, and check your own projects for those causes regularly, as in Section 43, before a breach forces you to learn the hard way. Prevention, through minimisation, access control, and careful sharing, as this chapter teaches, is far cheaper, in harm and in effort, than breach response, however well handled, because response can limit harm but cannot fully undo exposure once data has spread. A builder who prevents most breaches, and responds honestly to those that still occur, will protect users far better than a builder who relies on response alone, while collecting and sharing data carelessly in the meantime.

Section 18: Privacy by Design for Local Tools

Privacy by design means building privacy in from the start, through architecture, defaults, and workflows, rather than adding protections afterwards. For local tools, it means: keep data local by default, collect only what is needed, store it in known, access-controlled folders, log as little personal data as possible, and make sharing explicit, minimised, and recorded, not automatic. Those design choices, made early, shape everything that follows, because a tool designed to keep data local will naturally leak less than a tool designed to share by default, even if both later add privacy features, because default behaviour is what users actually experience, most of the time, without thinking about it.

Start privacy by design with defaults. Set default storage to a private, non-synced folder, not shared or public. Set default logging to counts and error types, not full text, as in Section 12. Set default sharing to none, requiring explicit action with a minimisation check before data leaves, as in Section 15. Set default retention short, with deletion after the task, not indefinite, as in Section 10. Defaults matter enormously, because most users never change them. Private defaults protect most users automatically, while leaky defaults expose most users, even if a privacy setting exists somewhere few ever find.

Next, design minimisation into workflow, not just policy. If a task can be done with aggregate counts, compute aggregates locally and discard raw inputs, rather than storing them "just in case." If synthetic examples suffice, use them by default, with real data only as an opt-in, consented exception, as in Section 11. If a tool needs to show an example, design it to show a synthetic example by default, rather than a real user's example, unless that user has consented to sharing. Workflow minimisation, built into how the tool actually works, is more reliable than policy minimisation, which relies on users remembering to minimise manually, because workflow design makes the private path the easy path, and the risky path the one that requires extra, deliberate effort.

Also design transparency and control into the interface. Show users plainly what data the tool reads, where it stores results, how long it keeps them, and how to delete them, in its main output or help text, not buried in a document they will never read. Provide a simple delete instruction, so users can exercise control without technical expertise. A tool that tells users what it does with data, and gives them an easy way to delete it, respects their control, as in Section 1, while a tool that hides its data practices behind technical complexity, even unintentionally, undermines that control, however good its intentions may be.

Finally, test privacy by design, as you test accuracy and fairness. Run your tool on personal-like test data, then search outputs, logs, backups, and shared folders for details that should not be there, and fix leaks before sharing. Ask, as a fresh user, "would I understand what data it uses, and feel in control?" If not, redesign defaults, workflow, or interface until yes. Privacy by design, tested this way, becomes demonstrable, not just claimed, and that demonstrability makes users trust, use, and recommend it.

Section 19: Transparency About Data Use

Transparency about data use means telling people, plainly, what data a tool uses, why, how long it keeps it, who can access it, and what happens on deletion, in language they understand, when that information can influence their decisions. Transparency is essential for consent, as in Section 4, because consent without understanding is hollow, and for trust, as in Section 1, because users who understand practices can judge acceptability, while users who do not must either trust blindly or refuse to use the tool, neither a good outcome. Transparency therefore serves both ethics and practicality: it enables informed choice, and it builds the trust that makes tools usable.

A good transparency note covers several points, plainly, on one page. It states what data the tool reads, and what it does not read, which is often as reassuring. It states why each kind is needed, for what specific task, not vaguely "to improve the tool." It states where data is stored, for how long, who can access it, and whether it leaves the machine, as in Section 15. It states how users can view, correct, or delete their data, with concrete steps, not just a promise that deletion is possible. And it states what happens when consent is withdrawn, or when a project ends, so users know their data will not linger indefinitely after they stop using the tool.

Transparency must also be honest about uncertainty. If practices may change in future versions, say so, and commit to updating the note with a date, rather than presenting it as permanent. If a tool relies on system syncing or backups controlled by someone else, say so, rather than implying your tool controls data it does not. If a transparency note cannot guarantee something, such as full deletion of shared copies, as in Section 10, say that plainly, rather than promising what cannot be delivered, because a broken promise damages trust more than an honest limit, which users can plan around.

Make transparency accessible, not just available. Put the note where users will see it before first use, such as startup message or help text, not in a buried file. Use short sentences and concrete examples, such as "this tool reads only files in the notes folder you choose, and never sends anything anywhere," rather than abstract legal language. Test transparency by asking a new user to read the note and explain, in their own words, what data the tool uses and how to delete it; if they cannot, rewrite it, because transparency is measured by user understanding, not author completeness.

Finally, update transparency notes whenever practices change, and keep old versions with dates, so users see what changed and when. A note never updated, while practices drift, becomes misleading, however honest it once was. Regular updating, with clear change notes, shows that data practices are monitored, not merely declared, and it gives users a reason to keep trusting a tool over time, because they know they will be told when something important changes, rather than discovering it through a leak or a surprise. Transparency, maintained this way, is a living practice, not a one-time document, and it is worth the small, regular effort it requires.

Section 20: Data Safety Checklist for Builders

A data safety checklist turns this chapter's practices into a routine you can run before building, sharing, or updating a tool, as in Chapters 21 to 23. Keep it short, on one page, and use it every time, even when busy, because data safety checks skipped under pressure are exactly the ones that prevent leaks that later prove costly and embarrassing. The checklist below is a starting point; adapt it to your tool, but keep it short enough to actually use, and update it when you learn a new failure pattern, so it evolves with your tool's data risks, rather than becoming a fixed ritual that no longer catches real exposures.

First, purpose and consent: do you know why each dataset is needed, and does consent cover that task, including third parties, as in Sections 2 and 4? Second, minimisation: have you collected only what is needed, using synthetic data where possible, as in Sections 5 and 11? Third, storage: are personal datasets in known, access-controlled, non-synced folders, with an inventory, as in Section 6? Fourth, access: does each tool read only needed folders, under a protected account, with no secrets in code, as in Sections 7 and 33? Fifth, logs and copies: have you checked logs, temporary files, backups, and metadata for personal data, and set retention and deletion for each, as in Sections 3, 12, and 13?

Sixth, sharing: before sharing, have you checked contents, minimised what leaves, set expectations, and recorded what left, to whom, and when, as in Sections 8 and 15? Seventh, anonymisation: if you claim data is anonymous, have you tested re-identification, as in Section 9, and stated method and limits honestly? Eighth, deletion: when retention ends or consent is withdrawn, have you deleted main files, copies, backups, logs, and derivatives, and verified by searching, as in Section 10? Ninth, transparency: have you written a plain-language data note, placed where users will see it, and tested understanding? Tenth, breach readiness: do you know Section 17 steps, and can you act quickly if a leak occurs?

Use the checklist as a gate, not paperwork. If any item fails, fix it or restrict sharing until it passes, rather than releasing with a known weakness and hoping no one notices, because exposures, once shared, cannot be fully recalled. A checklist that gates sharing is therefore protective, for data providers, recipients, and builders alike, because it ensures safety work is done before exposure, not after, when it is often too late to undo harm. That gate may slow a release by an hour, but it can prevent a leak that would cost far more, in trust, in rework, and in harm to people whose data was exposed.

Finally, review the checklist regularly. If an item never catches anything, make it more specific. If a leak occurs it did not catch, add an item that would have caught it, with a concrete test. A checklist that evolves with failures becomes steadily more effective, while a fixed checklist becomes less relevant as tools, data, and risks change. The goal is not a longer list, but a list that reliably catches the data safety failures your projects actually produce, in the forms they actually take, so that data safety checking becomes a habit, not a hurdle.

Section 21: Handling Other People's Notes

Handling other people's notes means working with files written by someone else, whether they were shared with you for testing, for collaboration, or for a task, and it requires extra care, because you are not the author, you may not know what is sensitive in them, and you cannot consent on the author's behalf to new uses. A note shared for one purpose, such as summarising for its author, should not be reused for another purpose, such as testing a tool for others, without new permission, because the original sharing was limited to its original purpose, as in Section 4. Builders who treat borrowed notes as if they owned them, copying, sharing, or keeping them indefinitely, violate the author's control, even if they had good intentions and even if no harm was intended, because control, not intent, is what privacy protects, as in Section 1.

Start by clarifying the terms of borrowing, in plain language, before you use someone else's notes. Ask what you may do with them: may you read them, summarise them, use them as test cases, share summaries, or keep copies, and for how long? Record the answer briefly, with the date, alongside the notes, so future use can be checked against the original permission, rather than relying on memory, which fades and expands over time. If terms were never clarified, because notes were shared casually, ask now, before further use, rather than assuming broad permission from a casual share, because casual sharing usually implies a narrow, immediate purpose, not a general licence for testing, sharing, or retention.

Next, minimise your handling of borrowed notes, as in Section 5. Read only what the task requires, rather than browsing an entire folder of someone else's files out of curiosity, because unnecessary reading is itself a privacy intrusion, even if nothing is shared afterwards. Copy only the notes or excerpts needed, rather than duplicating a whole collection, and delete borrowed copies promptly when the task ends, as in Section 10, rather than keeping them "just in case," because borrowed data kept indefinitely becomes a liability for both borrower and author, especially if the borrower's computer is later shared, lost, or backed up to a synced folder, as in Sections 6 and 13.

Finally, protect borrowed notes more carefully than your own, because their authors trusted you with them, and that trust deserves extra care. Store them in access-controlled folders, do not place them in shared or synced folders without explicit permission, do not quote them in shared examples or reports without permission or thorough anonymisation, as in Section 9, and tell the author if a borrowed note was accidentally shared, exposed, or retained longer than agreed, promptly and plainly, as in Section 17. That extra care, plus prompt honesty about mistakes, builds the trust that makes people willing to share notes for testing and collaboration in future, while careless handling, even once, can destroy that willingness for a long time, for you and for other builders who later ask the same people for help.

Section 22: Photos, Voice and Location in Data

Photos, voice recordings, and location data are especially sensitive kinds of personal data, because they reveal identity, appearance, voice, movements, and surroundings, often in ways their authors do not realise, and because they are hard to anonymise effectively. A photo can reveal faces, places, documents in the background, and metadata about where and when it was taken, as in Section 3, even when the photo's main subject seems harmless. A voice recording can reveal identity through voice itself, as well as through what is said, background sounds, and accents, which are difficult to remove without destroying the recording's usefulness. Location data can reveal home, work, routines, and associations, from a trail of places that, taken together, identifies a person and their habits far more precisely than any single location alone.

For local AI tools in this book, which work with text notes and files, photos, voice, and location will usually be out of scope, and builders should keep them out of scope deliberately, rather than collecting them "just in case," as in Section 5. If a task truly requires such data, for example testing a tool that handles image descriptions, prefer synthetic or publicly consented examples, with no real people, places, or voices, over real personal media, because synthetic media tests the same technical skill without exposing anyone's identity or movements. If real media must be used, obtain explicit, specific consent, as in Section 4, explaining exactly what will be done with faces, voices, or locations, who will see them, and how they will be deleted, and accept that many people will reasonably refuse, which is itself a signal that the task's privacy cost may outweigh its benefit, as in Chapter 23's decision not to build.

Also be careful about media embedded in otherwise text-based data. A note file may contain an attached photo, a document may contain a scanned image with personal details, or a message export may include location tags or voice notes alongside text, which builders may overlook when checking text for personal data, as in Section 11. Checking test data for embedded media, and removing or replacing it with synthetic equivalents before testing or sharing, is therefore part of test-set privacy, not a separate media topic, because a test set that is text-clean but media-leaky still exposes people, through the media it carries unnoticed. A simple habit, listing all files in a test set, including attachments, and checking each for personal media, catches most such leaks before sharing.

Finally, when handling photos, voice, or location, apply the strongest protections in this chapter by default: minimisation, access control, short retention, no sharing without explicit consent, and prompt deletion, as in Sections 5, 6, 8, and 10. Do not rely on blurring, voice changing, or location rounding alone, without testing whether re-identification is still possible, as in Section 9, because those techniques often leave enough detail for identification, especially in small datasets or for distinctive individuals. If you cannot protect such data confidently, do not collect it, and design the task to work without it, because a tool that works without sensitive media is safer, simpler, and more trustworthy than one that works with it but cannot protect it reliably.

Section 23: Data Retention: How Long

Data retention means how long you keep data, for what purpose, and when you delete it, and it is a key privacy decision, because data kept longer than needed creates risk without benefit, while data deleted too soon may lose testing or historical value that was genuinely needed. A responsible retention plan balances those two sides, by setting a specific retention period for each dataset, tied to its purpose, and by deleting or minimising data when that period ends, rather than keeping everything indefinitely by default, which is the most common retention failure in small projects, where deletion is postponed until "later," and later never arrives, while copies accumulate in backups and shared folders.

Start retention planning by assigning a period to each dataset when you collect it, such as "keep test notes until evaluation ends in June, then delete," and record that period with the dataset, as in Section 32 of Chapter 23. That record turns retention from a vague intention into a commitment that can be checked, and it gives data providers a clear expectation about how long their data will be held, which is part of meaningful consent, as in Section 4. If a dataset's purpose changes, or if it becomes useful for a new purpose, do not simply extend retention silently; re-ask consent where needed, as in Section 4, or replace the dataset with a minimised, synthetic version for the new purpose, so that extended retention is justified by a new, consented purpose, not by inertia.

Different kinds of data deserve different retention periods, based on sensitivity and need. Aggregate results, such as scores and breakdowns, which contain no personal details, can often be kept longer, because they carry little privacy risk while preserving evaluation history, as in Chapter 21. Raw personal notes, messages, or test cases, which contain identifiable details, should be kept for the shortest period that serves their purpose, and then deleted, as in Section 10, because their risk grows with time, while their testing value often declines as tools and inputs change. Sensitive data, as in Section 2, deserves the shortest retention of all, with deletion as soon as its specific purpose ends, rather than retention "in case" a future purpose emerges, because future purposes can usually be served by new, consented data, rather than by old, sensitive data kept without a current purpose.

Finally, enforce retention by schedule, not by memory. Set a regular review date, for example monthly or at each project milestone, to check which datasets have reached their retention end, and to delete or minimise them, as in Section 10, including copies and derivatives, as far as practical. That scheduled review, like the fairness and accuracy reviews in earlier chapters, turns retention from a good intention into a routine, and it prevents the slow accumulation of outdated personal data that characterises most privacy failures in long-running projects. A builder who keeps data only as long as its purpose requires, and who can show retention records for each dataset, is practising data safety in a way that data providers can trust, because they know their data will not linger indefinitely, forgotten, in a folder no one remembers to clean.

Section 24: Secure Deletion Versus Trash

Secure deletion and trash deletion differ in an important way: trash deletion hides data from ordinary view, but leaves it recoverable, while secure deletion aims to make recovery impractical, by overwriting or otherwise destroying the data's storage. Most operating systems, when you delete a file normally, do not erase its contents immediately; they mark its storage as available for reuse, and remove it from the folder listing, which means the file's contents may remain on the disk until that storage is reused for something else, and may be recoverable with simple tools in the meantime. Trash or recycle folders add another layer: they keep the file fully intact, in a special folder, until trash is emptied, which many users forget to do, so "deleted" files in trash are not deleted at all, in any meaningful sense, as in Section 10.

Whether secure deletion is needed depends on who might try recovery, and how much harm it would cause. For ordinary, low-sensitivity data on a computer you control, emptying trash and deleting copies may suffice, because recovery is unlikely and sensitivity low. For highly sensitive data, such as health, financial, or safety-related notes, or for data belonging to vulnerable users, as in Section 14, ordinary deletion may not be enough, because recovery by someone who gains access to the device, or to a discarded drive, could cause serious harm, and because the data's sensitivity justifies stronger measures, such as using your system's secure deletion tool, where available, or overwriting the file before deleting it, following guidance for your specific system, rather than assuming one method works everywhere.

Secure deletion also has limits that builders should understand honestly. On modern storage, especially solid-state drives and synced or backed-up storage, overwriting a file may not reliably erase all copies, because the storage system may keep hidden copies, wear-level copies, or synced versions elsewhere, as in Sections 6 and 13. A file deleted securely on one device may therefore persist in a backup, a synced folder, or a system snapshot, beyond the builder's control, which is why deletion planning, as in Section 10, must cover copies and backups, not just the main file's storage. Claiming a file is "securely deleted" when its backup copies remain is misleading, however thoroughly the main copy was overwritten, because the data itself, in another copy, is still accessible, and privacy depends on data, not on any single copy's storage.

For practical local building, the honest approach is layered: delete main files, empty trash, delete known copies, backups, logs, and derivatives, as in Section 10, use secure deletion tools where available and appropriate for sensitive data, and state, honestly, what deletion covers and what it cannot guarantee, such as copies beyond your control or system snapshots you cannot access. That layered, honest deletion is far better than either extreme: claiming perfect deletion that cannot be delivered, or doing nothing because perfect deletion is impossible. Most privacy protection comes from the layered steps that are within your control, applied consistently, not from a single perfect erasure technique, and builders who practise layered deletion, with honest limits, protect data providers far better than builders who rely on a single delete action and assume the job is done.

Section 25: Protecting Local Files in Plain Words

Protecting local files, in plain words, means making it hard for unauthorised people to read, copy, or change files containing personal data, while keeping those files usable for their legitimate owner and purpose. For most local builders, protection does not require advanced cryptography or expensive tools; it requires a combination of simple, consistent practices, as in Sections 6 and 7: store personal files in known, access-controlled folders, use a protected user account and a locked screen, keep software updated, and avoid placing personal files in shared, public, or synced folders unless sharing or syncing is genuinely needed. Those practices, applied consistently, prevent the most common local file exposures, which come from shared access, unlocked screens, and misplaced folders, rather than from sophisticated attacks that most local builders will never face.

One useful concept is file permissions: rules your system uses to decide who can read, write, or execute a file. On many systems, you can set a folder so only your account can read it, a simple, effective protection for shared computers, as in Section 7. Learning how to check and set permissions for your system, using its built-in help or settings, is a worthwhile investment for any builder handling personal data, because permissions are enforced by the system, they apply automatically to every file in a protected folder, and they do not require you to remember a password for each file. Permissions are not perfect: they do not protect against someone who gains full access to your account, or who removes the storage device, but they do protect against casual access by other users, which is the most common local risk.

Another concept is encryption, which scrambles contents so they cannot be read without a key. Full-disk encryption, where available, protects all files on a lost device, by requiring a password to unlock the disk, a strong protection for laptops, as in Section 6. File-level encryption, where individual files or folders are encrypted, protects specific sensitive files even when the disk itself is unlocked, but it requires careful key management, because losing the key means losing the files, and sharing the key carelessly defeats the protection. Builders should use encryption features provided by their operating system, where available, and follow that system's guidance, rather than inventing their own encryption, which is easy to get wrong in ways that provide false security while losing data, as in Section 7's warning about home-made password systems.

Finally, protection also means protecting files from accidental change and loss, not just from unauthorised reading. Keep backups of important, non-sensitive work, as in Section 13, store personal files separately from tool code, so that a tool bug cannot easily overwrite them, and test tools on copies of personal files, rather than on originals, where possible, so that a bug that corrupts a copy does not destroy the original. Those practices, combined with access control and, where appropriate, encryption, form a practical, layered protection for local files: permissions and accounts control who can read, encryption protects lost devices, backups protect against loss, and working on copies protects originals from bugs. Layered protection, honestly described with its limits, is the realistic goal for local builders, not perfect, unbreakable security, which no single practice can deliver on its own.

Section 26: Access Control: Who Can Open What

Access control answers, for each file and tool: who can open, change, and share it further? For local projects, that question is often answered implicitly, by where files live and who uses the computer, rather than explicitly, and implicit answers hide failures, because builders assume a folder is private when it is actually shared, synced, or accessible to other accounts, as in Section 6. Making access explicit, by listing who can access each personal dataset and tool, and by checking that list regularly, turns an assumption into a managed decision, which is far safer, especially as projects grow, collaborators join, and computers are shared or replaced.

Start by inventorying access, as in Section 6. For each personal folder, note who uses the computer, who has an account, who can access shared or synced folders, and who received copies, as in Section 8. That inventory often reveals surprises: a supposedly private folder inside a synced folder, a collaborator retaining access long after their task ended, or a backup accessible to people who never needed the original. Each surprise is an access control failure waiting to happen, and fixing it, by moving the folder, revoking shared access, or deleting an old backup, is a simple, high-value privacy action that costs minutes but prevents exposures that could last for years.

Next, apply least access, as in Section 7: give each person, tool, and process only needed access, for no longer than needed. A collaborator testing a summariser needs test summaries, not your entire notes folder. A sorting tool needs its messages folder, not your entire home directory. Least access limits the damage if an account is misused, a tool has a bug, or a collaborator's computer is compromised, because the exposed scope is narrow, rather than broad, and because each access grant, being specific and time-limited, can be revoked when its task ends, rather than lingering indefinitely as broad, forgotten access.

Also control change and sharing access, not just reading. Someone who can change a file can delete or corrupt it, harming its author even without reading it maliciously. Someone who can share further can pass data to people the author never approved, expanding exposure beyond the original decision, as in Section 8. Access control should therefore distinguish reading, changing, and sharing, and should grant sharing rights sparingly, only to people who need to share for a specific, agreed purpose, with an understanding that further sharing requires new permission from data providers, not just from the current holder. That distinction, often overlooked in simple sharing settings, is essential for privacy, because sharing is the action that most expands risk.

Finally, review access regularly. When a collaborator finishes, revoke access promptly, rather than leaving it "just in case." When a project ends, revoke shared access, delete shared copies where possible, and update the inventory, as in Section 10. When a computer changes hands, remove unneeded accounts and check for files left behind. Regular access review, like data review, keeps permissions lean and current, and it prevents the slow accumulation of broad, outdated access that characterises most long-running privacy failures, where no single access grant was wrong at the time, but their accumulation, over time, became unsafe.

Section 27: Data Safety in Shared Projects

Shared projects, where several people build or test together, create challenges solo projects do not, because data must move between people, access must be coordinated, and decisions about collection, sharing, retention, and deletion must be agreed, not just made by one builder. A shared project without agreed rules will drift into unsafe practices, as each collaborator applies differing assumptions about sharing, keeping, or copying, often without realising, until a leak reveals the mismatch. Agreeing data rules early, in plain language, and writing them down where all collaborators can see them, prevents most shared-project privacy failures, because it replaces differing assumptions with a shared standard.

Start with a short data agreement, covering purpose, consent, minimisation, storage, sharing, retention, and deletion, tailored to the project. It need not be formal; a one-page note stating "only consented test notes, in the shared folder, not synced publicly, deleted at project end, not shared further without re-asking" is enough, provided all collaborators read, agree, and can refer back to it. That agreement should also name a data owner, as in Chapter 23, responsible for answering data questions, handling withdrawal or deletion requests, and updating the agreement when practices change, so safety has a clear owner.

Next, organise shared data carefully. Use a single, access-controlled shared folder, rather than multiple personal folders, emails, and messages with different access and retention, because one shared location is easier to inventory, protect, and delete at project end. Separate shared test data from private data, and use synthetic examples for shared testing wherever possible, so shared folders contain as little real personal data as necessary. That separation, shared synthetic data plus private real data kept locally, is a powerful shared-project pattern, because it lets collaborators test together without exposing each other's personal files to the whole group.

Also agree on project end before starting, because end handling is where shared projects most often fail on privacy. Will shared data be deleted, returned, or kept minimised, and who will delete it, by when, and how verified, as in Section 10? Will collaborators delete their personal copies, and how will that be confirmed? Those end-of-project questions, agreed in advance, prevent the common outcome where a project ends, everyone moves on, and shared personal data lingers indefinitely in forgotten folders, backups, and email attachments, accessible to people who no longer need it and who may have forgotten it exists. An agreed end, with a named person responsible for deletion and verification, turns project closure into a privacy action, not just a stopping point.

Finally, handle mistakes openly, as in Section 17. If a collaborator shares beyond the agreement, or discovers a leak, tell the group promptly, contain exposure, notify providers, fix the cause, and update the agreement, rather than hiding the mistake, because hidden mistakes spread, while openly handled ones are contained and learned from. Shared projects succeed on trust, and trust is built by honest handling of mistakes, not by pretending mistakes never happen. A shared project with clear data rules, organised shared folders, an agreed end, and honest mistake handling will be safer, and more productive, than one where data safety is left to individual assumptions, however good each collaborator's intentions may be.

Section 28: Privacy and Fairness Together

Privacy and fairness are often treated separately, but for AI builders they are deeply connected, because data practices protecting privacy can affect fairness, and fairness practices requiring breakdowns can affect privacy, as in Chapter 23. Understanding that connection helps builders make balanced decisions, rather than optimising one at the expense of the other, which can happen when privacy work is done without considering fairness, or fairness work without considering privacy. A builder who protects privacy by deleting all data about a small group, for example, may make fairness measurement for that group impossible, while a builder who measures fairness by collecting sensitive group data carelessly may violate privacy in the name of fairness, and both outcomes harm the very people the builder intended to protect.

One connection is minimisation and representation. Minimisation reduces privacy risk by collecting less, but if it removes examples from a small group, because they were harder to obtain, it can worsen representation bias, leaving that group even less represented. To balance, minimise by removing unnecessary personal details, not entire groups, and check representation after minimisation, to ensure minimised datasets still cover diversity needed for fair testing. Minimisation that preserves diversity, by generalising details rather than deleting groups, serves both privacy and fairness, while minimisation that deletes diversity serves privacy at fairness's expense, which is a poor trade that should be recognised and avoided.

Another connection is fairness measurement and group data, as in Chapter 23. Measuring fairness by group often requires group information, creating privacy risk, but measuring by input type often reveals the same gaps without personal group data, serving both goals. Prefer input-based measurement first, and use group data only when genuinely needed, with consent and protection, so fairness work does not itself become a privacy risk. That preference, input first, group only with care, is a practical balance that most local builders can apply, without needing to choose between fairness and privacy, because it achieves much of fairness measurement's value with much less privacy cost.

A third connection is transparency. Transparency about data use and fairness limits both build trust, but must be balanced against privacy, because detailed reporting can expose individuals, especially in small groups. Balance means sharing aggregates, methods, and limits openly, while sharing individual cases only anonymised or synthetic, with re-identification testing, as in Section 9. That layered transparency, open about methods and aggregates, careful about individuals, serves both trust and privacy, while maximal transparency about individuals, or maximal secrecy about methods, serves neither well, because one exposes people and the other hides fairness problems.

Finally, treat privacy and fairness as joint responsibilities, reviewed together, not separate checklists. When reviewing a tool, ask both "does it protect personal data?" and "does it serve diverse users fairly?" and look for decisions improving both, such as synthetic diverse test sets, which protect privacy while improving fairness testing, or local-first design, which protects privacy while improving access. Joint review reveals win-win designs that separate reviews miss, and it reveals trade-offs early, while they can still be balanced thoughtfully, rather than discovered late, when one goal has already been sacrificed for the other. Privacy and fairness together, reviewed jointly, produce tools that are both safer and fairer, which is the standard responsible builders should aim for.

Section 29: Data Safety for Evaluation

Evaluation, as in Chapter 21, involves running tools on test sets, recording outputs, and sharing results, and each step can create risks if test data contains personal information and outputs, logs, or reports are shared without privacy checks. A builder who protects test data during building, but then shares outputs containing full test inputs, or logs with personal details, as in Section 12, has undone that protection at reporting, because outputs are often shared more widely than test data itself, in the name of transparency, as in Chapter 23. Evaluation data safety therefore requires applying this chapter's practices to the entire evaluation workflow, from test set to report, not just to the test set alone.

Start with test set choice, as in Section 11. Prefer synthetic cases, and consented, minimised real cases, over raw personal data, because synthetic cases can be shared without privacy risk, while raw cases cannot, and often test as well for local tools. When real cases are genuinely needed, use them privately, and report only aggregates and synthetic examples, rather than raw cases, so transparency is achieved through aggregates and methods, not by exposing personal test data.

Next, check evaluation outputs before sharing them, for privacy rather than hallucinations. Search outputs, logs, and reports for personal details, and remove or replace them before sharing, or share only aggregate scores and synthetic examples illustrating failure patterns without real details. That output check is especially important for failure examples, which builders often share to illustrate problems, because failure examples are frequently the most personal cases, containing the messy, distinctive details that caused the failure in the first place, and sharing them raw, to illustrate a technical point, can expose the very people whose data caused the difficulty, which is unfair as well as unsafe.

Also control evaluation retention, as in Section 23. Outputs, logs, and raw runs should be kept only as long as needed, such as comparing versions, then deleted or minimised, as in Section 10, rather than kept indefinitely in project folders, where they accumulate into a forgotten store of personal data. Keep aggregate evaluation records, such as dated scores and breakdowns, which carry little privacy risk, for longer, because they preserve evaluation history without personal content, while deleting raw outputs sooner, because their privacy risk outweighs their long-term value once aggregate results have been recorded and verified. That distinction, aggregates kept, raw outputs deleted sooner, balances evaluation history with data safety, in a way that serves both goals.

Finally, document evaluation data safety in the evaluation report, as in Chapter 21. State what test data was used, whether synthetic, consented, or minimised, what outputs were shared and in what form, and how long raw data will be kept, so readers can judge validity and privacy care without seeing raw personal data. That documentation, combined with aggregate reporting and synthetic examples, lets evaluation be both transparent and private, which is the standard responsible evaluation should meet, because transparency that exposes people is not responsible, and privacy that hides methods is not transparent. Evaluation data safety, practised this way, protects test data providers while preserving evaluation's value as shared, checkable evidence.

Section 30: When to Say No to Data

Saying no to data means refusing to collect, use, share, or keep personal data, even when offered or easily available, because its privacy, fairness, or safety risk outweighs its benefit for the task. Saying no is responsible, not a failure of ambition, because uncollected data cannot be leaked or misused, and a tool working without sensitive data is safer and more trustworthy than one working with it but unable to protect it reliably, as in Section 22. Builders should practise saying no, clearly and politely, in several common situations, where the temptation to accept data is strong but the responsible choice is to refuse, or to accept only a minimised, synthetic alternative.

One situation is data offered without clear consent or purpose. If someone offers personal notes or records, without explaining whose data it is, whether those people consented, or what purpose it serves, using it would violate consent, as in Section 4, and expose third parties, as in Section 2, without their knowledge. Ask for clarification, accept only data with clear consent and purpose, or decline and suggest synthetic alternatives, as in Section 11. That refusal may feel awkward, especially when data is offered helpfully, but it protects both the offerer, who may not understand implications, and third parties mentioned, who never offered it.

Another situation is sensitive and unnecessary data. If a task can be done with non-sensitive data, but sensitive data is offered as a shortcut, such as real health or location details to test a generic summariser, when synthetic dates or places would test the same skill, saying no to the sensitive version, and yes to synthetic, is responsible, achieving the same value without sensitive risk. Builders accepting sensitive shortcuts, because convenient, create liabilities persisting long after the test ends, in backups and logs, as in Sections 3 and 13, while builders insisting on synthetic alternatives keep projects lean and safe, without losing quality.

A third situation is sharing requested beyond original consent. If a collaborator asks to share test data consented for testing only, with a wider audience, for a new purpose, saying no, or "only minimised, synthetic form," protects original consent, as in Section 4, and prevents stretching it beyond what providers agreed to, which would destroy trust. That refusal should be explained plainly, with consent basis and safer alternative offered, so the requester understands it is principled protection of providers' control, as in Section 1, not personal rejection. Most collaborators, when they understand the consent limit, will accept the safer alternative, especially if it serves their purpose nearly as well as raw data would.

Finally, document refusals, like other data decisions, with a brief note stating what was refused, why, and what alternative was offered, so future builders understand and do not reverse it casually without reconsidering risks. That documentation also helps the builder themselves, by making refusal a deliberate, recorded practice, rather than a vague feeling that fades under pressure to accept data for a deadline or a demo. A builder who can say no clearly, politely, and with alternatives, practises data safety fundamentally, because safest data is never collected, and trustworthy builders collect only what is needed, consented, and protectable, refusing the rest, even when inconvenient.

Section 31: A Worked Example: Cleaning a Note Set

Imagine a folder of twenty notes, shared by friends for testing a summariser, which you want to clean for safe testing and sharing. Cleaning means reviewing each note, removing or replacing personal details, checking consent, and documenting what you did, so the cleaned set can be used without exposing authors. That worked example, done step by step, shows how this chapter's practices fit together in a concrete, local task, and it gives you a routine you can repeat for any note set, test set, or example collection you handle in future projects.

First, inventory the set. List each note's filename, author, consent basis, and purpose, as in Section 6. Suppose fifteen notes have clear consent for testing, three were shared casually without clear terms, and two mention third parties who never consented. That inventory tells you what to do: use the fifteen within consent, ask the three casual sharers for clarification before further use, as in Section 21, and remove third-party details in the two, as in Section 2, before sharing. Inventory first, use second, is the rule that prevents most cleaning mistakes, because it surfaces consent and third-party problems before they are baked into a test set that is then hard to clean retrospectively.

Next, clean each note, detail by detail. Replace real names with invented names in the same format, so structure stays testable without real identities. Replace real dates, phone-like numbers, and distinctive places with invented equivalents, preserving format, so date handling can still be tested without real schedules. Remove sensitive sentences not needed for testing, rather than merely anonymising them, because a sensitive sentence, even with names removed, may still identify its author, as in Section 9. Keep a cleaning log, noting what was replaced in each note, in general terms, without recording the original personal details in the log itself, because a log that repeats originals defeats its own purpose.

Then test the cleaned set, by trying to re-identify it, as in Section 9, and by running your summariser on it, to confirm it still tests needed patterns. Ask a trusted colleague, who does not know original authors, to read cleaned notes and say whether any author seems identifiable, and if so, clean further. That re-identification test, done before sharing, is the key quality check for cleaning, because the cleaner, who knows the originals, cannot judge identifiability reliably on their own. If the test passes, and the summariser still handles dates, names, and lists correctly on cleaned notes, then cleaning has preserved testing value while removing privacy risk, which is the goal.

Finally, document and share the cleaned set responsibly, as in Sections 8 and 19. Write a short note stating source, consent, cleaning method, test result, and retention, and share the cleaned set, not originals, with recipients who need it, with expectations about further sharing and deletion. Delete originals, or store them separately under stricter access, as in Section 10, and record that decision, so future builders know which version is shareable.

Section 32: Data Safety for Agents

Agents, as in Chapters 18 and 19, take multiple steps, reading files, searching notes, summarising, and acting, such as saving or sending outputs, and their multi-step nature creates risks single-step tools do not. An agent might read a personal file in step one, copy sensitive details into a summary in step two, then share that summary in step three, expanding exposure at each step, as in Chapter 22's snowballing, but for privacy. An agent might also read more files than its task requires, because its search step is broad, or because it follows links or references to unrelated personal files, exposing data its user never intended it to touch. Agent data safety therefore requires controlling what an agent reads, what it copies, and what it shares, at each step, not just at its final output.

Start agent data safety with least access, as in Section 26. Give the agent explicit folders to read, rather than broad access to an entire home directory, and explicit output folders, rather than permission to write anywhere, so search cannot wander into unrelated files, and action cannot overwrite or share outside its scope. That scoping is the single most effective agent privacy control, because it limits over-reading and over-sharing to a narrow, known set of files, whose contents and consent you already checked, as in Sections 6 and 11. An agent with broad, default access is a privacy risk, however good its steps may be.

Next, check data at step boundaries, as in Chapter 22, but for privacy. When search retrieves a passage, check for personal details before passing it to summary, and if found, minimise it, passing only needed facts, rather than the full passage with personal context. When summary produces an output, check it before passing to an action step that might share or save it, and remove details not needed for that action's purpose. Those boundary checks, simple scripts flagging names and sensitive words, plus human review for important agents, prevent personal data travelling through a chain unnoticed, where each step assumes the previous already checked it.

Also limit agent actions by sensitivity, similar to safety gates in Chapter 22. An agent should require human approval before it shares, deletes, or overwrites files, especially personal data, than before merely reading or summarising locally, because sharing and deletion are irreversible in ways reading is not. Design agents so that high-sensitivity actions, such as sharing a summary externally, pause for human review, with the summary and its sources shown, so the human can check for personal details before approving, as in Section 24 of Chapter 23. That approval gate, applied to sensitive actions only, keeps agents useful for routine local tasks, while preventing their most risky actions from proceeding automatically on personal data that was never meant to leave the machine.

Finally, log agent activity minimised, as in Section 12, recording steps run, folders accessed, and actions taken, without full personal inputs or outputs, so behaviour can be audited without the log itself leaking. Review logs regularly for over-reading outside scope, or over-sharing to unexpected recipients, and fix scope or steps when patterns appear. A scoped, checked, gated, minimised-log agent is data-safe; one without those controls is a privacy risk, because multi-step nature amplifies single-step leaks.

Section 33: Keeping Secrets Out of Code

Keeping secrets out of code means never writing passwords, private details, or other sensitive information into program files, comments, test files, or shared examples, because code is often shared, copied, stored in version history, and forgotten, while secrets in it persist and spread far beyond original context, as in Section 7. A password written in a comment "for testing" may be copied into a shared project, posted for help, or included in a backup, long after the test ended, and anyone who receives that code can then use the secret, even if the original author never intended to share it. That persistence, combined with code's tendency to travel, makes code one of the most common and most dangerous places for secrets to leak, despite builders' good intentions when they first wrote the secret down "temporarily."

The most common secrets in code are not just passwords. They include private names and addresses used as example data, written into test files as "just examples," while actually real details travelling with code. They also include file paths with personal names, and old commented-out code with details from an earlier sensitive version, which builders forget to remove before sharing. Checking code for secrets therefore means searching not only for password-like strings, but also for personal details, paths, and comments that could identify someone, as in Section 43, before any code is shared, even with a trusted collaborator.

Preventing secrets in code starts with synthetic examples by default, as in Section 11. When you need example data, invent names, dates, numbers, and places, in realistic formats, rather than copying real personal details, because synthetic examples test the same code paths without exposing anyone, and can be shared freely. If an example genuinely needs a real pattern synthetic data cannot mimic, generalise it, or obtain explicit consent, as in Section 4, and note consent in documentation, so future sharers know its basis and limits.

Also use habits that catch secrets before sharing. Search code for secret patterns, such as "password" or "key" near values, and for personal details from test data, before sharing, using simple text search, as in Section 43. Remove secrets found, and check version history and backups, as in Sections 10 and 13, because a secret removed from current code may still live in an old version or shared copy, which must also be cleaned or treated as still exposed, with affected people notified, as in Section 17. That history check is often overlooked, yet essential, because sharing often includes history, where "temporary" secrets hide longest.

Finally, if a secret leaks through code, act promptly, as in Section 17: remove it from current code and, where possible, history and shared copies, change the secret itself if it could still be used, notify affected people plainly, and fix the habit causing the leak, such as switching to synthetic examples by default, or adding a pre-sharing secret search to your checklist. A leaked secret, handled promptly and honestly, causes far less harm than one hidden hoping no one noticed, because hidden secrets remain usable, while changed secrets do not, and honest notification lets affected people protect themselves. Keeping secrets out of code, by synthetic defaults, pre-sharing searches, history checks, and prompt response, is fundamental for every builder sharing code.

Section 34: Data Safety Over Time

Data safety over time means protections today must be maintained, reviewed, and updated as data, tools, users, and risks change, because a dataset safe last year may become risky this year, through new combinations, new sharing, new users, or unforeseen vulnerabilities. A test set anonymised well enough for one collaborator may become re-identifiable when combined with new public information, as in Section 9. A folder private on a solo-used computer may become shared when that computer is shared, as in Section 6. Data safety is therefore not a one-time setup, but an ongoing practice, like fairness and accuracy maintenance in earlier chapters, requiring regular review.

Schedule data safety reviews, alongside other project reviews, monthly or at each major version, using a checklist, as in Section 20. During a review, check the inventory, as in Section 6, for new folders, copies, or sharing not in the original plan. Check retention dates, as in Section 23, and delete datasets whose purpose ended. Check access, as in Section 26, and revoke access for finished collaborators. Check logs and backups, as in Sections 12 and 13, for personal data accumulated since the last review, and clean them. That scheduled review, done consistently, catches drift in data safety before it becomes a leak, because it forces builders to look at their data holdings as they actually are now, not as they were when first collected.

Also update practices when circumstances change, not just on schedule. When a tool gains new users, especially vulnerable users, as in Section 14, strengthen protections to match new risk. When it starts handling a new kind of data, such as photos or location, as in Section 22, add protections specific to that kind, rather than assuming old text protections suffice. When a collaborator joins or leaves, update access and sharing records immediately, rather than waiting for the next scheduled review, because access changes are time-sensitive: a collaborator who retains access after leaving can still copy data, however good the scheduled review would eventually have been. Event-driven updates, triggered by changes in users, data types, or access, complement scheduled reviews, by ensuring that the most urgent changes are handled promptly, while routine drift is caught by schedule.

Finally, keep data safety records over time, with dates, so progress and decisions can be tracked, as in Chapter 21. A record showing a dataset collected with consent in May, reviewed in June, minimised in July, and deleted in August, tells a clear story of stewardship, while a project with no records cannot demonstrate it, even if practised informally, because there is no evidence of what was done, when, or why. Records also help future builders, who inherit a project, understand its data history without having to guess, and they help data providers, who ask what happened to their data, receive a specific, dated answer, rather than a vague reassurance. Data safety over time, maintained through scheduled reviews, event-driven updates, and dated records, is what turns initial privacy care into lasting privacy protection, for as long as data is held, and beyond, through proper deletion when its purpose ends.

Section 35: Privacy Checklist for Users

Users need a privacy checklist too, because builders cannot protect every user's data in every situation, and users are the last defence before their own data is shared, copied, or exposed through a tool they use. A user's checklist must be short, memorable, and focused on questions revealing privacy risks quickly, without technical knowledge, because users are often busy, and a long checklist will be ignored, especially when a tool is new or urgently needed, when risks are most likely overlooked. Three to five questions, used consistently, protect users far better than a comprehensive list never opened.

First, what data does this tool read, and does it need all of it? If a summariser asks for your entire home folder, when it only needs one notes folder, restrict it, or choose a tool asking for less, as in Section 26. Second, where does my data go, and who else can see it? If a tool sends, syncs, or shares data, ask to whom, for how long, and under what protections, as in Section 15, and prefer local tools, as in Section 38, unless external sharing is genuinely needed and explained plainly. Third, how long is my data kept, and how can I delete it? If a tool keeps data indefinitely, with no clear deletion path, as in Sections 10 and 23, ask for a retention period and deletion method before relying on it for personal data.

Fourth, what happens if this tool's data leaks, and for whom? If a leak would expose sensitive details about you, or people mentioned in your data, as in Section 2, check extra carefully, use minimised or synthetic data where possible, and avoid highly sensitive data until protections are clear. Fifth, can I control and correct this tool's data use? A privacy-respecting tool should let you view what it stored, correct mistakes, withdraw consent, and request deletion, with concrete steps, as in Section 19, rather than vague promises. A tool that cannot be controlled, or whose practices are hidden, is less safe, however good its outputs look, because you have no recourse when handling goes wrong.

Teach this checklist to others, especially in shared work, where one casual sharing can expose a whole team's data. Put it where users will see it: a shared guide or tool help text. A visible, shared checklist becomes a habit protecting everyone, because each user's checking catches risks others missed, and a team checking together demands better practices with more weight.

Finally, user privacy checking is not distrust of AI; it is using AI as a helper, not an oracle to trust blindly with personal data, as in Chapter 22. Summarising, searching, and sorting tools save enormous time, even handling personal data, provided users check what data they read, where it goes, how long kept, and how to delete it, before relying on them for sensitive tasks. That partnership, tool for speed, human for privacy judgement, is the mature model for responsible use, far more sustainable than blind trust, which allows unchecked leaks, or total avoidance, which wastes genuine benefits. Check for privacy, in short, not because tools are useless, but because they are useful enough to be worth checking, for yourself and for the people mentioned in your data.

Section 36: What to Do If Data Leaks

If data leaks, meaning personal data you held has been shared, copied, or accessed without authorisation, act quickly, calmly, and honestly, following the breach steps from Section 17, adapted to the specific leak you face. First, contain the leak as far as possible. Remove a mistakenly shared file or link, revoke access to a shared folder, change passwords for affected accounts where relevant, or retrieve a device if you can, before investigating further, because continued exposure increases harm, while investigation can proceed after containment. Containment may not undo exposure that already happened, but it stops new exposure, which is the most urgent goal in the first hours after a leak is discovered.

Next, assess the leak in plain terms. What data was involved? Who was mentioned in it? Was it sensitive, as in Section 2, or ordinary? Who may now have it: one mistaken recipient, a small group, a public audience, or an unknown party? Write those answers down briefly, because they guide notification and fixing, and because affected people deserve specific information, not vague reassurance. If you do not know who accessed the data, say so honestly, rather than claiming it was only one person, because false reassurance, discovered later, destroys trust more than honest uncertainty, which at least lets affected people take protective steps proportionate to the unknown risk.

Then notify affected people promptly, in plain language, stating what happened, what data was involved, what you have done to contain it, and what they can do to protect themselves. Notification should not wait for a perfect investigation, because affected people need to know early enough to act, for example by changing a password, watching for misuse, or contacting you with questions. Keep notification specific and free of minimising language, such as calling a sensitive leak "minor," because minimising language signals that the builder cares more about reputation than about affected people, while honest, specific notification signals the opposite, and builds trust even in a difficult situation, as in Section 17.

Finally, fix the cause and record the leak, so it does not recur and so future builders can learn from it. If the cause was mistaken sharing, add a recipient check before sending sensitive files. If it was broad shared-folder access, tighten access, as in Section 26, and revoke access for people who no longer need it. If it was a forgotten backup, clean backups, as in Section 13, and add backup review to your schedule, as in Section 34. Record cause, containment, notification, and fix, with dates, in your project records, and tell affected people what was fixed, when, so they can see the leak was taken seriously, not merely apologised for. A leak handled this way, contained, assessed, notified, fixed, and recorded, causes far less lasting harm than one hidden, delayed, or minimised, however embarrassing prompt honesty may feel in the moment.

Section 37: Data Safety and Trust

Data safety and trust are closely linked, because users trust builders who handle their data carefully, and distrust builders who handle it carelessly, even when the careless handling causes no immediate, visible harm. Trust in data safety is built through consistent, visible practices: minimising collection, as in Section 5, obtaining meaningful consent, as in Section 4, storing data in known, protected folders, as in Section 6, sharing only minimised data with clear expectations, as in Section 8, and deleting data when its purpose ends, as in Section 10. Each of those practices, done consistently, signals to users that their control over their information, as in Section 1, will be respected, while each practice neglected signals the opposite, even if no leak has yet occurred, because users judge trustworthiness by practices they can see, not just by outcomes they cannot yet observe.

Trust is also built through transparency about limits, as in Section 19. A builder stating plainly what data a tool uses, where it goes, how long kept, and what cannot be guaranteed, such as full deletion of shared copies, as in Section 10, gives users information to decide trust, and that honesty itself builds trust, showing risks are not hidden. A builder who claims perfect privacy, with no limits, no retention details, and no deletion method, invites suspicion, because experienced users know perfect privacy is rarely achievable, and because a claim of perfection, when it inevitably fails, destroys trust far more than an honest limit would have, which users could have planned around from the start.

Trust is lost quickly when data safety fails, especially when failure is hidden, delayed, or minimised, as in Sections 17 and 36. A leak that is promptly contained, honestly notified, and fixed at its cause, as those sections teach, damages trust, but it also demonstrates accountability, which can rebuild trust over time, as users see that problems lead to changes. A leak that is hidden, discovered by users themselves, or dismissed as unimportant, destroys trust far more thoroughly, because it shows users that the builder prioritised reputation over their protection, and because it leaves users exposed without knowledge, while they could have been taking protective steps. How a builder responds to failure therefore matters as much as how they prevent it, for long-term trust, because response reveals values in a way that prevention alone cannot.

Finally, trust built through data safety benefits builders as well as users, in practical ways. Users who trust a builder are more willing to contribute test data, to report problems, and to recommend the tool to others, which improves the tool and expands its audience, as in Chapter 23's discussion of trust and collaboration. Users who distrust a builder will withhold data, avoid the tool for sensitive tasks, or abandon it entirely when an alternative appears, however good its outputs may be, because data safety, once doubted, outweighs output quality in users' decisions about personal data. Investing in data safety, therefore, is not just an ethical cost; it is a practical investment in a tool's adoption, longevity, and community, because trustworthy tools are used, shared, and improved, while distrusted tools, however clever, are avoided, especially for the personal tasks where AI tools could help most.

Section 38: Local-First as a Privacy Choice

Local-first means designing a tool to work on the user's own machine, on their own files, without sending data to external services, syncing it elsewhere, or requiring accounts, and it is a strong privacy choice, because it keeps control of data with its owner, by default, rather than passing control to third parties, as in Section 16. This book's tools are local-first by design, using only Python's standard library and the user's own files, with no external calls, no accounts, and no transmission, which means their privacy risk is limited to local access, storage, and sharing decisions that the user and builder can directly control, rather than to the practices of distant services that neither fully understands nor controls. That limitation is a genuine privacy advantage, and builders should recognise it as such, rather than treating local-first as a temporary limitation to be outgrown by adding cloud features whenever they seem convenient.

Local-first also simplifies privacy work. Because data never leaves by design, many risks, such as third-party breaches and service policy changes, simply do not arise, and builders can focus on remaining risks, such as local access, backups, logs, and sharing, as in Sections 6, 12, and 13, which are more manageable and visible than distant service risks. Local-first also makes privacy verifiable: a user or reviewer can read the tool's code, see that it makes no external calls, and confirm that data stays local, rather than trusting a promise about a service's practices, which cannot be verified from the outside. That verifiability builds trust, as in Section 37, because it replaces a promise with a checkable fact, which is far stronger evidence of privacy care.

Local-first does not mean privacy is automatic, however, and builders should not claim it does. A local tool can still leak through broad file access, as in Section 26, logs with personal details, as in Section 12, backups in synced folders, as in Section 13, or sharing outputs with personal details, as in Section 8, all without sending data externally by design. Local-first removes third-party risk, but it does not remove local risk, and builders who claim local-first alone guarantees privacy will mislead users, who may then handle local data carelessly, believing it to be automatically safe, when it still requires the practices this chapter teaches, applied consistently to local storage, access, logs, and sharing.

Finally, preserve local-first wherever possible, by asking, for each proposed new feature, "does this feature genuinely require data to leave the machine, or can it be done locally?" Often, features that seem to require external services, such as searching, summarising, or sorting, can be done locally, with standard library tools, on the user's own files, as this book demonstrates, without any loss of core functionality, and with a large privacy gain. When a feature truly requires external data, such as fetching a public fact, consider whether that fact can be fetched without sending personal data, or whether the user can provide the fact locally instead, so that personal data still stays local even when public data is fetched. That disciplined questioning, applied to every new feature, keeps local-first design from drifting into cloud-first design by convenience, and it ensures that privacy remains a deliberate choice, not an accidental casualty of feature growth.

Section 39: Data Safety in Summaries

Summaries create data safety risks, because they copy, condense, and often share information from original notes, and a summary shared more widely than its original can expose personal details safe in their original, limited context, but unsafe more broadly. A note for personal use, containing a private detail, may be safe in its author's private folder, but its summary, if shared with a team, posted publicly, or included in a test set, can expose that detail to people who should never have seen it, even if accurate and well-written. Summary safety therefore requires checking summaries for personal details before sharing, and deciding who should see each, based on contents, not just accuracy.

Start summary safety by checking the original's sensitivity before summarising. If a note contains sensitive details, as in Section 2, ask whether they are needed in the summary, or whether it can serve its purpose without them, by generalising, omitting, or replacing them, as in Section 5. A meeting summary may need decisions and dates, but not personal opinions, health details mentioned in passing, or private contact information irrelevant to its purpose. Omitting irrelevant sensitive details is a powerful privacy practice, reducing exposure where information is most likely shared, while preserving usefulness, which rarely requires every sensitive detail from the original.

Next, check the summary itself, after writing it, for details that slipped through, by reading it as a stranger would, asking "who is mentioned, what sensitive details appear, and would the original author be comfortable with this being shared with its intended audience?" That stranger-reading, similar to Chapter 22's detective reading, catches details the summariser, knowing original context, may have included without realising their sensitivity more broadly, such as a name identifying a vulnerable person, or a date and place combination identifying someone even without a name, as in Section 2. If a summary fails the stranger test, revise it, removing, generalising, or replacing the problematic detail, before sharing, rather than sharing first and hoping recipients handle it carefully, because recipients cannot unsee a detail once shared.

Also label summaries with sensitivity and sharing limits, where appropriate. A summary with personal details, even after minimisation, should carry a note, such as "contains personal details; share only with named recipients, do not forward," so recipients understand handling requirements, as in Section 8, rather than assuming casual forwarding is safe. Labelling does not technically prevent forwarding, but it sets a clear expectation and a basis for asking deletion if shared beyond limits, as in Section 17. For wide sharing, write a separate public version, with personal details removed or replaced synthetically, as in Section 11, keeping the fuller version only for its original audience.

Finally, store summaries with the same protections as originals, or stricter, because summaries are often stored in shared folders, test sets, or archives, where they may be more accessible than originals, as in Sections 6 and 11. A summary in a shared folder, while its original remains private, has effectively expanded access, even if shorter, because shared-folder users without original access can now read it. Decide summary storage deliberately, based on contents and audience, not convenience, and delete summaries with personal details, as in Section 10, when their purpose ends, rather than keeping them as convenient examples.

Section 40: Data Safety in Search Tools

Search tools create data safety risks, because they index files, record queries, and return results, each exposing personal data in overlooked ways. Indexing creates a searchable copy of contents, which may include personal details from files never intended to be indexed, such as private notes in a folder also containing public notes, if the index covers the whole folder by default, rather than only chosen folders, as in Section 26. Query recording, as in Section 12, logs what users searched for, revealing interests or plans, even when files are never shared, because queries themselves are personal data. Result sharing can expose contents to people without original-file access, if results include full passages rather than titles or minimised snippets.

Start search safety with scoping, as in Section 26. Let users choose explicitly which folders to index, rather than an entire home directory by default, and show clearly which are indexed, so users can remove folders with personal data they do not want searchable, before building. That explicit scoping, plus a clear display of indexed folders, prevents the common failure where a tool indexes everything, including private files, because its default was broad and its user never realised what was included, until a search result reveals a private file in an unexpected context, such as a shared screen.

Next, minimise what search stores and returns. An index storing full contents, for fast searching, is more sensitive than one storing only titles, keywords, or minimised snippets, because a full-content index is effectively a copy of all indexed files, with its own access, backup, and deletion challenges, as in Sections 6, 10, and 13. For local tools, an index storing paths and key words, rather than full text, often serves searching well enough, while reducing exposure if shared or leaked, because key words reveal less than full passages. Similarly, results should show minimised snippets, enough to judge relevance, rather than full passages with personal details, unless the user explicitly requests the full passage for a file they already have access to, in which case showing it is safe, because they could have opened the file directly anyway.

Also handle query privacy carefully. Do not log full queries by default, as in Section 12; log counts or categories, if needed, rather than query text, which may reveal sensitive interests. Do not share query history, even in aggregate, without checking whether aggregates could identify individuals, especially in small groups, as in Section 9. And do not let results leak through shared screens, histories, or autocomplete revealing previous queries to later users, by clearing history, or keeping history private per account, as in Section 7.

Finally, delete indexes and histories when their purpose ends, or when indexed folders change, as in Section 10. An index built for a temporary project, left after the project ends, continues to expose indexed files, even if originals were deleted, because the index may contain their contents. Deleting the index when its project ends, and rebuilding only when needed, for chosen folders, keeps search privacy current, rather than allowing old indexes to linger as forgotten copies. Search tools scoped explicitly, indexed minimised, queried privately, and deleted when done, are data-safe; tools indexing broadly, logging fully, returning fully, and persisting indefinitely, are risks, however useful their searching.

Section 41: Privacy in Test Data

Test data needs privacy care, because test sets are often shared, reused, and stored for long periods, and personal details in them can therefore expose people far beyond original context, as in Sections 11 and 29. A test set built from real notes, emails, or messages, even for a small local project, contains authors' personal details, and if shared with collaborators, included in an archive, or used in teaching, those details travel with it, potentially to people authors never expected to see them. Test data privacy therefore starts with a simple question, asked before any test set is built: "is this data synthetic, public, or consented for testing and sharing?" If the answer is none of those, the data should not be used as a test set, however convenient it may be, because convenience does not create consent, and because synthetic data, as in Section 11, usually tests a local tool just as well, without any privacy risk at all.

When test data must use real patterns, such as realistic names, dates, or topics, use synthetic patterns mimicking reality without copying real people, as in Section 11. Invent names, places, and dates, combine them in realistic ways, and check, as in Section 9, that no synthetic person accidentally matches a real person in a recognisable way, especially in small communities, where a plausible invented name may coincidentally be a real neighbour's name, combined with a plausible invented detail that matches that neighbour's life. Synthetic test data, checked for accidental matches, gives a tool realistic patterns to process, without exposing any real person, and can be shared freely, making testing easier, not harder, because builders need not restrict access to a test set containing no personal data.

If real data is used for testing, with genuine consent, as in Section 4, apply minimisation, access control, and retention limits from Sections 5, 26, and 23, specifically to the test set. Minimise it to only fields and cases needed to test behaviour, rather than full original records, because full records contain irrelevant personal details adding risk without testing value. Restrict test set access to only the testers who need it, for only the testing period, and delete the test set, as in Section 10, when testing ends, rather than keeping it as a permanent project asset, because test sets kept indefinitely become forgotten copies of personal data, as in Section 13, whose consent and purpose may long since have expired.

Finally, document test data, as in Section 19, with source, consent basis, cleaning method, access list, and retention period, so future builders can tell whether a test set is safe to reuse, share, or extend, or should be deleted and replaced with synthetic data. A documented test set, with clear limits, is reusable, because its safety can be verified before reuse, while an undocumented test set, with unknown source and consent, is a liability, because no one can safely reuse it without investigating provenance, often impossible after original builders moved on. Test data privacy, therefore, is not just about protecting people in the test set today; it is about creating assets remaining safe, reusable, and trustworthy over time, a practical benefit, as well as a privacy duty, for every builder testing with data.

Section 42: Data Safety and Children

Children's data deserves extra care, because children are vulnerable users, as in Section 14, and data collected about children can affect them far longer than data about adults, since a child's data, collected at age ten, may still be in a system at twenty, with consequences unforeseeable when collected. Builders should therefore avoid collecting children's data unless there is a clear, specific, justified purpose, with appropriate adult consent and protection, and they should never use children's personal data for testing, training examples, or demonstrations, however convenient it may seem, because children cannot give informed consent in the same way adults can, and because examples using children's data, once shared, cannot be reliably recalled, as in Section 10, even if the builder later regrets sharing them.

If a tool may be used by or about children, design it to minimise data from the start, as in Sections 5 and 18. Collect only what is strictly necessary, avoid names, locations, schools, or other identifying details unless genuinely required, and do not store children's data longer than its immediate purpose requires, as in Section 23, because longer retention increases risk without benefit. Use synthetic examples, as in Section 11, for all teaching, testing, and demonstration involving children, rather than real children's work, photos, or notes, because synthetic examples teach the same skills, without exposing any real child, and because they avoid the consent and retention problems that real children's examples create, even when collected with good intentions.

Also protect children's data in outputs and sharing, as in Sections 8 and 39. Do not include children's names, locations, or identifying details in summaries, search results, or shared outputs, even when those details appeared in original inputs, because outputs are more likely to be shared than originals, and because a child's identifying detail, once shared, can be copied and spread in ways that are difficult to control, as in Section 36. If a tool's output must refer to a child, use a non-identifying label, such as "Student A," rather than a name, and check, as in Section 9, that the label, combined with other details in the output, cannot identify the child to people who know the context, such as classmates, teachers, or community members, who may recognise a child from a combination of seemingly ordinary details.

Finally, give adults clear control over children's data in tools they manage, as in Section 19. Provide a plain-language explanation of what data the tool collects about children, why, how long it is kept, and how an adult can view, correct, or delete it, with concrete steps, not vague promises, and make deletion genuinely effective for local data, as in Section 24, by removing the data and its copies, not just hiding it from view. If a builder cannot provide that control, or cannot guarantee that children's data will be protected to the standard this section describes, the responsible choice is to not collect children's data at all, and to design the tool to work without it, because a tool that cannot protect children's data safely should not hold it, however useful that data might seem for improving the tool, and because protecting children is a duty that outweighs convenience, in every case, without exception.

Section 43: Auditing a Tool for Data Safety

Auditing a tool for data safety means systematically checking what data it collects, stores, shares, and retains, against this chapter's practices, so problems can be fixed before causing a leak, rather than discovered after one, as in Section 36. An audit is not one-time, but regular, as in Section 34, because data, tools, and risks change, and a tool safe at launch may become unsafe through new features or data, as in Section 38. A good audit is simple, concrete, and documented, so findings can be acted on and future audits can check whether previous findings were fixed, as in Chapter 21, applied to privacy.

Start an audit by listing the tool's data flows, in plain language. What inputs does it read, and from which folders? What does it store, and where, including logs, indexes, backups, and test sets, as in Sections 12, 40, and 41? What does it output, and to whom, including summaries, search results, and saved files, as in Sections 39 and 40? What does it retain, and for how long, as in Section 23? Write those flows down, even for a small local tool, because writing reveals flows invisible in casual use, such as a log storing personal details, an index storing full contents, or a backup copying a private folder to a synced location.

Next, check each flow against this chapter's practices, using Section 20's checklist, plus specific checks for the tool's type, such as agent checks from Section 32, summary checks from Section 39, or search checks from Section 40. For each flow, ask: minimised collection, as in Section 5? Meaningful consent, as in Section 4? Restricted access, as in Section 26? Limited retention, as in Section 23? Effective deletion, as in Section 24? Minimised, labelled sharing, as in Sections 8 and 39? Record, for each flow, whether it passes, fails, or needs improvement, with a brief note, so findings are specific and actionable, rather than vague impressions, such as "privacy seems okay," which cannot be acted on or re-checked, as in Chapter 21.

Then fix the largest findings first, as in Chapter 23's fairness fixing, prioritising by harm, not ease. Broad access exposing private folders, as in Section 26, matters more than a minor log wording issue, even if easier to fix, because broad access can cause a large leak, while log wording causes a smaller one. Fixing by harm ensures audit effort reduces real risk, rather than merely producing a long list of trivial fixes looking productive but leaving major risks untouched, a common audit failure, where builders fix easy items first, feel accomplished, and never return to hard, important items actually protecting users.

Finally, record the audit and its fixes, with dates, and schedule the next audit, as in Section 34. A record showing what was checked, found, and fixed gives future builders evidence of data safety care, as in Section 37, and makes the next audit easier, starting from previous findings rather than scratch. It also protects the builder, showing privacy was taken seriously even if a later leak occurs, while a project with no audit records cannot demonstrate that care, even if practised informally. Auditing is therefore not bureaucratic overhead; it is the practical way to keep data safety real over time.

Section 44: Data Safety Mistakes to Avoid

Common data safety mistakes are worth learning explicitly, because they are made repeatedly, by careful builders as well as careless ones, often through convenience or oversight, rather than bad intentions, and recognising them in advance makes them easier to avoid. The first common mistake is collecting everything, just in case, as in Section 5, because a builder collecting all available data, hoping it may be useful later, creates a large, sensitive store, whose purpose is unclear, whose consent is doubtful, and whose deletion is difficult, because no one knows what is still needed. The fix, as Section 5 teaches, is to collect only what is needed for a stated purpose, and delete it when that purpose ends, even if collecting again later for a new purpose, because re-collecting with clear purpose is safer than hoarding without purpose.

The second common mistake is trusting defaults, as in Sections 6, 12, and 26, because tools often default to broad access, full logging, synced storage, or public sharing, convenient for the tool maker, but unsafe for the user, whose data may be exposed by a default they never chose or noticed. Builders should therefore set defaults deliberately, choosing narrow access, minimised logging, local storage, and private sharing, requiring explicit action to broaden them, rather than accepting broad defaults hoping users will narrow them, which most will not do, as in Section 35.

The third common mistake is forgetting copies, as in Sections 10, 13, and 24, because work often focuses on the original file, while forgetting backups, logs, indexes, shared copies, and test sets with the same details, persisting after the original is deleted. A builder deleting an original, but leaving its backup in a synced folder, details in a log, contents in a search index, and summary in a shared folder, has not actually deleted the data, however sincerely they believed they had, because deletion must cover all copies, as Section 24 teaches, and forgotten copies frequently cause leaks long after the original was supposedly gone.

The fourth common mistake is sharing first, checking later, as in Sections 8, 39, and 40, because builders often share a file, summary, or search result, assuming it is safe, and only check afterwards, when recipients have already seen it, and when recall, as in Section 10, is unreliable. The fix is to check before sharing, every time, using pre-sharing checks from Sections 20 and 39, even when sharing feels urgent or routine, because routine reduces attention, and a single mistaken share can expose data to people unlikely to delete it reliably, however sincerely they promise to afterwards.

Finally, the fifth common mistake is assuming local means safe, as in Section 38, because builders keeping data local may conclude privacy is therefore guaranteed, while overlooking local risks, such as shared computers, broad folder access, logs, backups, and shared outputs, all leaking local data without external transmission. Local-first is a strong choice, as Section 38 teaches, but not a substitute for this chapter's full practices, applied to local data just as carefully as to shared data, because local data, handled carelessly, leaks just as surely through local channels. Avoiding these five mistakes will prevent most failures, before they happen, far better than fixing them after a leak, as in Section 36.

Section 45: Data Safety for the Future

Data safety for the future means building habits and tools remaining safe as technology, data, and risks change, rather than solving only today's problems, while leaving tomorrow's unaddressed, as in Section 34. Future risks are difficult to predict precisely, but their general shape can be anticipated: more data will be collected, by more tools, from more sources, in more contexts, including voice, location, and behaviour, as in Section 22, and that data will be combined in new ways, as in Section 9, creating new re-identification risks from combinations seeming harmless today, but becoming identifying when combined with future public information. Builders who design for the future, therefore, should favour minimisation, local-first design, and clear deletion, as in Sections 5, 38, and 10, because those practices protect against unknown future risks, by reducing the amount of data available to be combined, shared, or leaked, whatever the future combination may be.

Future-proof data safety also means designing tools that are transparent and controllable, as in Sections 19 and 35, so users can understand and adjust data handling as risks change, rather than being locked into practices they cannot see, change, or leave. A tool letting users view what it stored, delete it, restrict access, and export or remove data, gives users control remaining valuable, even as technology changes, because control allows users to respond to new risks, by deleting, restricting, or withdrawing data, when they judge risk has grown. A tool that hides its data practices, or that makes deletion and restriction difficult, removes that future control, and leaves users exposed to risks they cannot mitigate, however good the tool's data practices may seem today, when its risks are still known and manageable.

Also design for future accountability, as in Sections 37 and 43, by keeping records, audits, and documentation, as in Section 34, so future builders, users, and reviewers can understand what data was collected, why, with what consent, and with what protections, even after original builders moved on, and even when original context changed. Records and audits, kept over time, allow future decisions about a tool's data, such as whether to reuse a test set, extend a feature, or share a dataset, to be made with knowledge, rather than guesswork, essential for long-term safety, because guesswork about old data, whose source and consent are forgotten, is a frequent cause of future leaks, when old data is reused for new purposes, without original limits remembered or respected.

Finally, remember data safety is a continuing practice, not a finished achievement, as this chapter has taught throughout, from minimisation in Section 5, through access, deletion, and auditing, to this final section. The builder treating data safety as a checklist to complete once, then forgotten, will become unsafe over time, as data, tools, and risks change, while the builder treating it as a habit, reviewed regularly, documented honestly, and applied to every new feature, will remain safe, or at least detect and fix problems promptly, when they arise, as in Section 36. That habit, more than any single technique, is this chapter's lasting lesson, and it will protect users, builders, and communities, as AI tools grow more powerful and widespread, provided builders choose, consistently, to put data safety first, even when convenience, speed, or profit tempt otherwise.

Section 46: Data Safety and Collaboration

Collaboration creates data safety challenges, because sharing data with collaborators expands access, copies, and retention, each increasing exposure, as in Sections 8, 13, and 27, and because collaborators may have different privacy practices, tools, and understandings of a dataset's limits, leading to accidental sharing, retention, or reuse beyond original purpose, even with good intentions. A dataset shared with five collaborators, each copying it to their own machine, backing it up, and keeping it after the project ends, has effectively become six datasets, each with its own access, backup, and deletion challenges, and each must be protected, deleted, or returned, for the original safety plan to hold, which is why collaboration requires explicit agreements, not just informal sharing, however trusted collaborators may be.

Start collaboration safety with an explicit agreement, in plain language, before sharing data, stating what is shared, for what purpose, with whom, for how long, and with what limits on further sharing, copying, and retention, as in Section 8, plus who is responsible for deletion, as in Section 10, when collaboration ends. That agreement, even if just a short written note, shared with all collaborators, gives everyone the same understanding of limits, gives the data owner a basis for asking deletion or correction, as in Section 17, if limits are breached, and prevents the common failure where one collaborator assumes data can be shared further, while another assumes it cannot, because assumptions were never written down and compared, until a further share reveals disagreement, too late to undo it.

Also agree on storage, access, and deletion practices for collaboration, as in Sections 6, 26, and 24, so collaborators store shared data in known, protected folders, restrict access to only collaborators needing it, and delete it, including copies and backups, when collaboration ends, rather than keeping it indefinitely as a convenient resource, as in Section 13. Agree, in advance, how deletion will be confirmed, for example by a brief message stating data and copies have been deleted, on a stated date, because confirmation, even if informal, gives the data owner evidence deletion happened, and gives collaborators a clear deadline, rather than a vague hope data will be deleted eventually, which, in practice, often means never, as Section 34 shows.

Finally, review collaboration data safety over time, as in Section 34, especially when collaborators change, purpose changes, or collaboration extends beyond its original period, because each change may require a new agreement, minimisation, or deletion, and data shared under an old agreement, for an old purpose, should not automatically be reused for a new purpose, without fresh consent, as in Section 4, and fresh minimisation, as in Section 5. Collaboration with explicit agreements, minimised sharing, agreed storage and deletion, and periodic review, is data-safe, allowing people to work together without exposing personal data unnecessarily, while informal collaboration, with full datasets, vague limits, and no deletion plan, is a privacy risk, however trusted collaborators may be, because trust alone does not control copies, backups, or future reuse.

Section 47: Data Safety in Evaluation

Evaluation creates data safety risks, because evaluation often uses real data, outputs, and user interactions, each possibly containing personal details, as in Section 29, and because evaluation results, reports, and examples, if shared, can expose those details to people without original-data access, as in Sections 39 and 40. A builder who evaluates a summariser on real personal notes, then shares evaluation examples showing its outputs, may inadvertently share personal details from those notes, even if the evaluation itself was conducted carefully, because examples chosen for their illustrative value often contain the most interesting, and therefore the most sensitive, details, which is why evaluation data safety requires the same minimisation, consent, and sharing care as other data work, applied specifically to evaluation sets, outputs, and reports, as in Chapter 21's evaluation discipline, extended to privacy.

Start evaluation safety by choosing evaluation data carefully, as in Section 41. Use synthetic evaluation sets, as in Section 11, whenever they can test the behaviour in question, because synthetic sets can be shared freely, in evaluation reports, without privacy risk, and allow evaluation to be repeated, reviewed, and extended by others, without requiring those others to protect personal data. If real evaluation data is genuinely needed, because synthetic data cannot capture a specific pattern, use it only with consent, as in Section 4, minimised to only cases needed, as in Section 5, restricted to only evaluators needing it, as in Section 26, and deleted, as in Section 10, when evaluation ends, rather than kept as a permanent asset, whose consent and purpose may expire, while copies persist, as in Section 13.

Next, check evaluation outputs and examples before sharing, as in Sections 39 and 20, by reading them as a stranger would, asking whether they contain personal details, identifying combinations, or sensitive information, as in Section 2, exposing original subjects, if shared with the report's audience. If an output contains such details, generalise, omit, or replace them synthetically, as in Section 11, before sharing, or choose a different example, without sensitive details, still illustrating the evaluation point, because most points can be illustrated with a safe example, and because a report exposing personal data, however accurate its evaluation, has failed its data safety duty, and undermined trust in the evaluation itself, as in Section 37, by showing authors prioritised illustration over protection.

Finally, delete evaluation data, outputs, and intermediate files, as in Section 10, when evaluation ends, or when retention, as in Section 23, expires, including logs, indexes, and shared copies, as in Sections 12, 13, and 40, because evaluation files, left after evaluation, become forgotten copies of personal data, whose purpose has ended, but whose exposure continues, until discovered, often by accident, long afterwards. Evaluation safety, scoped, minimised, checked before sharing, reported in safe aggregates, and deleted when done, protects people whose data made evaluation possible, while preserving its value, because safe evaluation can be shared, repeated, and trusted, while unsafe evaluation, however thorough, cannot be shared safely, and therefore cannot contribute fully to learning, evaluation's whole purpose.

Section 48: Data Safety and Transparency Reports

Transparency reports are plain-language summaries of a tool's data practices, for users, collaborators, and reviewers, stating what data it collects, why, where it goes, how long kept, and how it can be deleted, as in Section 19, plus incidents, audits, or changes, as in Sections 36 and 43, and they are powerful, because they make practices visible, checkable, and accountable, rather than hidden, vague, or merely promised. A transparency report, even a short one, for a small local tool, gives users information to decide whether to trust the tool with their data, gives collaborators a shared understanding of data limits, as in Section 46, and gives future builders a record of data decisions, as in Section 34, essential for long-term safety, when original builders have moved on and new builders must decide whether old practices remain safe.

Write transparency reports in plain words, as this chapter does, avoiding jargon, legalistic language, or vague assurances, such as "we take privacy seriously," which convey no actionable information, and which users rightly distrust, because they have heard them from tools that leaked data, while claiming to take privacy seriously. Instead, state specifics: "this tool reads only the folder you choose, stores summaries in your chosen folder, logs only counts, not file contents, shares nothing externally, and deletes its index when you delete the project folder," because specifics can be checked, against code and behaviour, while vague assurances cannot, and specifics allow users to make informed decisions, such as choosing a different folder, or declining to use the tool for sensitive data, if specifics do not meet their needs, a valid, informed choice, transparency should support, not prevent, by hiding specifics behind reassurance.

Include, in a transparency report, what has changed, gone wrong, and was fixed, as in Sections 36 and 43, because a report only listing good practices, while omitting incidents and audits, is incomplete, and will be distrusted when incidents are later discovered, as in Section 37. A report stating, for example, "in June, a log was found to contain personal details; it was fixed by minimising logging, and old logs were deleted," demonstrates accountability, building trust, even though it admits failure, because it shows failures lead to fixes, while a report omitting that incident, discovered later by users, demonstrates concealment, destroying trust, even if the failure itself was minor, because concealment suggests other failures may also be hidden, which users cannot check, and therefore cannot trust.

Update transparency reports regularly, as in Section 34, when practices change, when new features are added, as in Section 38, when audits find new issues, as in Section 43, or when incidents occur, as in Section 36, because an outdated report, describing practices no longer holding, misleads users, who may rely on old assurances, while new practices expose their data in ways they did not expect, worse than having no report at all, because it creates false confidence, leading users to share data they would not have shared, had they known current practices. A dated report, with a clear "last updated" date, and a brief change log, allows users to tell whether it is current, and allows builders to track how practices have evolved, valuable for future audits, as in Section 43, and for future accountability, as in Section 37.

Section 49: Building a Data Safety Culture

A data safety culture is a shared habit, within a team, community, or project, of treating personal data with care, by default, in everyday work, rather than as a special task, done only when a leak occurs, or an audit is due, as in Sections 43 and 36, and it is built through leadership, routine, and example, not rules alone, because rules without culture are ignored when no one is watching, while culture, internalised as habit, guides behaviour even when no rule is explicitly recalled, which is how most everyday data decisions are made, quickly and routinely, without formal review. Building such a culture starts with leaders modelling data safety visibly, by minimising data, as in Section 5, checking before sharing, as in Section 20, deleting data when done, as in Section 10, and admitting and fixing mistakes promptly, as in Section 36, because team members copy what leaders do, far more than what leaders say, and a leader sharing personal data carelessly, while preaching privacy, teaches carelessness, however good their preaching may be.

A data safety culture also needs routine, by embedding checks into everyday workflows, as in Section 20's checklist, Section 43's audit, and Section 34's review, so checking becomes part of normal work, like checking code for errors, rather than an extra burden, done only when time allows, which, in practice, means rarely, because time rarely allows extra work, when deadlines press. Routine checks, such as a pre-sharing check before every share, a deletion check at every project end, and a quarterly audit, as in Section 43, make data safety habitual, far more reliable than relying on individual vigilance, because vigilance varies with fatigue, urgency, and familiarity, while routine, once established, persists through those variations, and routine checks catch mistakes vigilance misses, especially routine mistakes, as in Section 44, precisely the mistakes vigilance, dulled by routine, is least likely to catch.

Culture also needs learning, by sharing data safety lessons, openly, when mistakes occur, as in Section 36, and when audits find issues, as in Section 43, so the whole team learns from one person's mistake, rather than each person learning the same lesson separately, through their own leak, which is wasteful, and exposes more people, than shared learning would have, had the first mistake been shared openly. Sharing lessons openly requires a blame-free environment, where admitting a mistake is treated as responsible, not shameful, because a culture punishing admission will drive mistakes underground, where they cannot be fixed or learned from, while a culture rewarding admission, by fixing causes promptly and sharing lessons widely, will surface mistakes early, when still fixable, and when harm is still limited, a practical benefit, as well as a humane one, for every team handling personal data.

Section 50: Data Safety in Plain Words: A Summary

Data safety, in plain words, means treating personal data with the care it deserves, because personal data is not just information; it is part of people's lives, identities, relationships, and safety, as in Section 1, and handling it carelessly can harm people, even without intent, through leaks, re-identification, or misuse, as in Sections 9, 17, and 36. This chapter has taught data safety as practical habits, not abstract theory, starting with understanding what counts as personal data, as in Section 2, obtaining meaningful consent, as in Section 4, collecting less, as in Section 5, storing safely, as in Section 6, controlling access, as in Section 26, sharing minimised data with clear limits, as in Section 8, and deleting data, including all copies, as in Sections 10 and 24, when purpose ends, because those habits, applied consistently, prevent most failures, before they happen, far better than fixing them after a leak.

Data safety also means being honest about limits, as in Section 19, because no builder can guarantee perfect privacy, anonymisation, as in Section 9, is never absolute, deletion, as in Section 10, cannot recall shared copies reliably, and local-first design, as in Section 38, does not automatically guarantee privacy, however strong a choice it is, and because honest limits allow users to make informed decisions, to protect themselves, and to trust honest builders more than builders claiming perfection, then failing, as in Section 37. Honesty about limits, combined with prompt, honest response to failures, as in Section 36, is the foundation of trustworthy data handling, because it shows users builders respect them enough to tell the truth, even when uncomfortable, and gives users information needed to protect themselves, when builders' protections are insufficient, for their specific situation, a partnership, as in Section 35, between builder and user, each protecting data in their own role.

As you move to Chapter 25, on AI safety basics, remember data safety is a core part of AI safety, not a separate topic, because AI tools handling personal data carelessly are unsafe, however accurate their outputs may be, and because AI safety, broadly, means building tools helping people, without harming them, their privacy, their fairness, as in Chapter 23, or their ability to check and control tools they use, as in Chapters 21 and 22. Carry this chapter's habits forward, into every tool you build, dataset you handle, and summary, search, or agent you create, and you will build AI tools not just clever, but trustworthy, the highest praise an AI builder can earn, and the most valuable contribution to users, communities, and AI's future, which depends, ultimately, on trust, earned through care, honesty, and consistent, practical protection of people whose data makes AI possible.
