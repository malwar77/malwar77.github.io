Chapter 5: Rules, Decisions and Simple Models

Section 1: Rules You Can Read

A rule is a statement of the form "if this, then that" that a program can follow and a person can read. If a message contains a question mark, label it a question. If a task is overdue, flag it as urgent. Each rule is small, explicit, and testable on its own, which is why rules remain useful even in an age of complex learned models. A rule-based system is a collection of such statements, ordered so that when several apply, the system knows which to follow. That simplicity is a virtue: rules can be explained to users, debated by experts, audited by regulators, and fixed by editing a single line, without retraining anything.

Rules work best when the job is governed by clear, stable criteria that experts can state. A company's expense policy, a school's grading rubric, and a shop's sorting policy are all rule-governed jobs, where the right answer is defined by the rules themselves, not discovered from data. In such jobs, learning from data is not only unnecessary, it is risky, because a learned model might discover patterns that contradict the policy, or that reflect historical bias in past decisions rather than the policy as written. Rules, by contrast, implement the policy directly, and any disagreement about a decision can be resolved by pointing to the rule that produced it and debating whether that rule is correct. That auditability is why rules dominate in finance, law, and medicine, where decisions must be justified, not merely predicted accurately on average.

Rules also work well as safety limits around learned models. A learned classifier might sort messages, but a rule can guarantee that messages containing sensitive words are always reviewed by a human, regardless of what the model predicts. A learned recommender might suggest content, but a rule can guarantee that harmful content is never suggested, regardless of predicted engagement. Those guardrail rules, simple and absolute, provide safety that learned models alone cannot guarantee, because learned models are probabilistic and can always be wrong, while a well-chosen rule can enforce a hard constraint that must never be violated. That combination, learning for flexibility and rules for safety, is a pattern you will see in well-designed systems, and it is the pattern the capstone will follow, where certain actions always require approval by rule, no matter what any model suggests.

Learning to write good rules is a skill in itself. Good rules are specific enough to decide clearly, general enough to cover many cases, and ordered sensibly so conflicts are resolved predictably. Bad rules are vague, overlapping, or contradictory, leading to unpredictable decisions that are hard to debug. Writing good rules requires thinking about edge cases in advance: what happens when no rule applies, when several rules apply, or when a rule's condition is ambiguous? Those questions, answered explicitly with fallbacks and priorities, turn a pile of rules into a system. As you build rule systems in this chapter, practise answering those questions for every rule you write, and your systems will be more reliable than many professional ones, where edge cases are left to chance until users discover them the hard way.

Section 2: Decision Lists: Rules in Order

A decision list is a rule system where rules are tried in order, and the first rule whose condition matches decides the outcome. That ordering is powerful, because it resolves conflicts simply: earlier rules take precedence over later ones, so a designer can put specific, high-priority rules first and general, fallback rules last. For a message classifier, a decision list might start with a rule for messages that are clearly questions, then a rule for clear tasks, and end with a fallback rule that labels everything else as a note. That structure is easy to read top to bottom, like a checklist, and easy to test: for any message, you can trace which rule fired first and why earlier rules did not, giving a clear explanation for every decision.

Decision lists are also easy to maintain, because adding a new rule means inserting it at the right position in the order, rather than retraining a model or rewriting a whole system. If users report that birthday messages are being mislabelled, you can add a birthday rule near the top, test it on your test set, and deploy it without affecting other rules, except for messages that now match the new rule earlier than they matched old ones, which is exactly the intended effect and is easy to check by re-running the test set. That local editability contrasts with learned models, where adding training data can change many predictions in ways that are hard to predict, requiring full retesting to understand the impact. For small tools and for jobs where rules change frequently, that local editability is a major advantage.

Order, however, is also a decision list's main risk: a rule placed too early can shadow later rules, capturing cases it should not, while a rule placed too late may never fire, because earlier, broader rules capture its cases first. Shadowing is a common bug, diagnosed by tracing: for mislabelled messages, check which rule fired and whether an earlier rule should have been narrower or later. Fixing shadowing usually means narrowing the early rule's condition, or reordering rules so specific rules precede general ones, a principle known as specificity ordering: most specific first, most general last, with a fallback at the very end. That principle, applied consistently, prevents most shadowing bugs, and it is worth stating as a design rule for your own lists, so future editors do not reorder carelessly and reintroduce shadowing.

Decision lists also teach a general lesson: order matters. The same set of rules, in a different order, can produce different decisions for the same inputs, because the first match wins. That order dependence is a design dimension to be used deliberately, by placing rules that reflect priorities, safety, or specificity earlier. A safety rule, such as "if a message contains a password, never file it publicly", should come before any sorting rule, because safety outranks sorting. A specific rule, such as "if a message is from the manager and contains 'urgent', flag it immediately", should come before a general urgency rule, because specificity outranks generality. Thinking about order as priority, not just as sequence, turns decision lists from arbitrary lists into expressions of values and policies, which is what makes them powerful tools for responsible design.

Section 3: If, Elif and Else in Python

In Python, decision lists are written with if, elif, and else, a structure that mirrors their logic exactly. An if statement checks a condition; if it is true, its block runs and the rest of the chain is skipped. An elif, short for else if, checks a further condition only if previous conditions were false, allowing a chain of alternatives to be tried in order. An else catches everything that matched none of the previous conditions, serving as the fallback. That structure is not just syntax; it is the decision list itself, written in code, and reading it top to bottom tells you exactly how decisions are made, in what order, and what happens when nothing matches. Mastering if-elif-else is therefore mastering decision lists in Python, and it is a skill you will use in every rule system you build.

The program below shows a decision list for messages, written as if-elif-else. It checks first for a clear question, then for a clear task, then falls back to note. Notice how conditions are ordered by specificity: the question check requires both a question mark and a question word, making it specific, while the task check requires only an action word, making it broader. That ordering means a message that is both a question and a task, such as "How do I buy tickets?", will be labelled question, because the question rule comes first, which is a deliberate priority choice, documented in a comment, so future readers know it was intentional, not accidental. Documenting priorities in comments is good practice, because order without explanation looks arbitrary, while order with explanation reveals policy.

def classify_decision_list(message):
    text = message.lower()
    words = text.split()
    if "?" in text and words and words[0] in ["what", "how", "why", "when", "where"]:
        return "question"
    elif any(word in words for word in ["buy", "call", "finish", "send", "book"]):
        return "task"
    else:
        return "note"

for sample in ["What time is dinner?", "Buy milk", "How do I buy tickets?", "Lovely day"]:
    print(sample, "->", classify_decision_list(sample))

Common mistakes with if-elif-else include using separate if statements instead of a chain, which allows multiple rules to fire for one input, producing conflicting outputs, when a decision list should stop at the first match. Another mistake is forgetting the else fallback, which leaves some inputs with no decision, causing a program to return nothing or to crash, when it should return a sensible default. A third mistake is writing conditions that are too broad early in the chain, shadowing later rules, which is diagnosed by tracing which branch fired for mislabelled inputs. Avoiding those mistakes requires discipline: use elif for alternatives, always include else, order by specificity, and test with tracing, printing which branch fired for each test message, so shadowing becomes visible rather than hidden.

If-elif-else also scales: chains can be long, with many elif branches, and they can be nested, with if statements inside other if blocks for sub-decisions, such as first deciding message type, then, within tasks, deciding urgency. Nesting should be used sparingly, however, because deeply nested chains become hard to read and test, and a flat decision list, or a table-driven approach where rules are stored as data rather than code, may be clearer for long lists. That table-driven idea, storing rules as rows with conditions and outcomes, will be explored in a later section, as a way to manage large rule sets without tangled code. For now, master flat if-elif-else chains, with clear conditions, sensible order, and always an else, and you will have a solid foundation for all rule systems, however large they later grow.

Section 4: Thresholds: When Enough Is Enough

A threshold is a cut-off value that turns a score into a decision: above the threshold, decide one way; below it, decide another. For a scored classifier, a threshold might be: if the task score exceeds the question score by at least two, label it task; otherwise, label it unsure. For a spam filter, a threshold might be: if the spam probability exceeds ninety percent, file as spam; otherwise, keep it in the inbox for review. Thresholds are everywhere in decision-making, because most real decisions involve turning graded evidence, scores, probabilities, or measurements, into discrete actions, and the threshold is where that conversion happens. Choosing thresholds well is therefore a central decision skill, because a good model with a bad threshold performs poorly, while a mediocre model with a well-chosen threshold, matched to user costs, can perform usefully.

Thresholds embody trade-offs, as Chapter 4 discussed for precision and recall. Lowering a threshold makes a system more liberal: it catches more true cases, increasing recall, but also includes more false alarms, decreasing precision. Raising a threshold makes it more conservative: it reduces false alarms, increasing precision, but misses more true cases, decreasing recall. Neither direction is universally better; the right threshold depends on costs. If missing a case is costly, such as missing an urgent message, lower the threshold to catch more, accepting false alarms as the price. If false alarms are costly, such as falsely flagging an important message as spam and hiding it, raise the threshold to reduce false alarms, accepting some misses as the price. Those cost calculations should be made explicitly, with users, not by defaulting to fifty percent, which rarely matches real costs.

Choosing a threshold in practice involves trying several values on validation data, not test data, and comparing precision, recall, and overall usefulness for each, then picking the value that best matches user priorities. That trial, plotted as a curve or tabulated as a table, shows the trade-off concretely, and it allows stakeholders to see what they gain and lose at each threshold, which makes the choice a shared decision rather than a builder's guess. For small projects, trying three thresholds, low, medium, and high, and reporting results for each, is enough to understand the trade-off and to choose sensibly. For larger projects, finer tuning is standard, but the principle is identical: thresholds are tuned on validation, confirmed on test, and documented with reasons, never tuned directly on test, which would inflate test scores and hide true performance.

Thresholds also need maintenance, because as data drifts, a threshold that was optimal last year may become suboptimal this year, as score distributions shift. Monitoring threshold performance over time, by tracking precision and recall on fresh data, and retuning when they degrade, is part of model maintenance, and it should be scheduled, not left to chance. A threshold that is never revisited will slowly become mismatched to current data and costs, degrading a tool's usefulness silently, while its model, unchanged, appears stable. That silent degradation is a common failure mode for deployed decision systems, and it is prevented by the simple routine of periodic threshold review, which this book will encourage for the capstone tools, where thresholds for urgency, relevance, and importance will need regular checking as your notes and tasks evolve.

Section 5: Scoring Rules: Adding Up Evidence

Scoring rules combine multiple pieces of evidence into a single score, which is then compared with a threshold or with other scores to decide. Your scored classifier from Chapter 1 is a scoring rule system: each clue adds points to a label's score, and the label with the highest score wins. That simple idea generalises widely: credit scores add points for income, history, and debts; triage scores add points for symptoms and vital signs; relevance scores add points for matching words and recency. Scoring rules are popular because they are transparent, each clue's contribution is visible, they are flexible, allowing many weak clues to combine into a strong decision, and they are easy to adjust, by changing weights or thresholds, without retraining a complex model. They are also a bridge between rules and learning: weights can be set by hand, based on expert judgement, or learned from data, by counting associations, as Chapter 4 showed, making scoring rules a hybrid that combines human knowledge with data evidence.

Designing a scoring rule involves choosing features, assigning weights, and setting a decision rule. Features are the clues: for messages, question marks, question words, and action words; for triage, fever, pain, and breathing rate. Weights are how much each clue counts: a question mark might count two points, a question word one point, reflecting that question marks are stronger evidence than question words alone. The decision rule turns scores into actions: highest score wins, or score above a threshold triggers an action, or scores are reported as a ranking, allowing users to prioritise. Each of those design choices should be documented and tested, because changing any one can change many decisions, in ways that should be understood before deployment, not discovered afterwards by users.

Scoring rules have limitations that should be understood. They assume clues add up independently, which is often false: words that appear together may overlap in evidence, so adding their scores double-counts that evidence, overconfidently. They assume weights are stable across contexts, which may be false if a clue's meaning changes by group, time, or topic. And they can be gamed, if users learn which clues earn points and manipulate them, such as adding urgent words to a message to get it flagged, even when it is not urgent. Those limitations do not make scoring rules useless; they define where they fit best: jobs where clues are roughly independent, weights are stable, and gaming is unlikely or harmless, or where gaming can be detected and penalised. For jobs where clues interact strongly, or where gaming is a serious risk, more sophisticated models or additional safeguards, such as human review for high scores, may be needed, and those should be considered during design, not after gaming is discovered in use.

A practical exercise is to design a scoring rule for urgency in messages, from scratch, with features, weights, and a threshold, then test it on your test messages and compare its confusion matrix with your classifier's. Which features helped most? Which weights were too high or too low, as revealed by mistakes? How did threshold choice affect precision versus recall for urgent messages? That exercise teaches scoring design by forcing you to make weight and threshold choices and revise them based on evidence.

Section 6: Decision Trees Revisited, in Code

Decision trees, introduced in Chapter 4, can be implemented in code in several ways, from hand-written if-elif-else chains that follow a tree's structure, to learned trees built automatically from data by choosing splits that best separate labels. Hand-written trees are appropriate when experts know the decision logic and want it explicit, auditable, and editable, such as a triage protocol or a sorting policy. Learned trees are appropriate when experts do not know the logic, or when data reveals patterns experts missed, and the tree is learned by searching for splits that reduce impurity. Both forms share the same structure, questions at nodes, branches for answers, labels at leaves, and both share the same virtues, transparency and interpretability, and the same risks, overfitting without pruning and instability when data changes slightly.

A hand-written tree in Python is simply nested if statements, but it should be written carefully to remain readable: use functions for subtrees, name conditions clearly, and keep nesting shallow, by flattening where possible. That structure, written as nested ifs, is readable if each level is clearly commented and if functions separate major branches, but it becomes unreadable if nesting goes five levels deep with complex conditions at each level. When a tree grows that deep, consider a table-driven representation, where the tree is stored as data, such as a list of nodes with conditions and outcomes, and a small interpreter walks the tree for each input. That separation makes large trees manageable and editable without changing code.

Learned trees are built by an algorithm that, at each node, tries candidate splits, such as "contains word X" or "length greater than Y", and chooses the split that best separates labels in the current subset, measured by impurity reduction. That algorithm involves counting labels in subsets, computing impurity, and recursing on subsets until a stopping rule is met, such as a minimum subset size or a maximum depth. Implementing a tiny learned tree yourself, even for a toy dataset with a few features, is illuminating, because it shows that learning a tree is not magic, but systematic search for good questions, guided by a simple criterion, repeated until groups are pure enough. It also shows why learned trees overfit: without stopping or pruning, the algorithm will keep splitting until each leaf contains a single example, memorising training data perfectly and generalising poorly.

Pruning a learned tree means cutting back branches that do not improve validation performance, even if they improve training performance, which is a concrete application of the bias-variance trade-off: a pruned tree is simpler, with higher bias but lower variance, and it generalises better. Pruning can be done by holding out validation data, building a full tree on training data, then removing branches one at a time, keeping removals that do not worsen validation accuracy. That procedure requires discipline: pruning on test data would leak test information, so validation must be used, and test reserved for final confirmation. For hand-written trees, pruning has an analogue: reviewing branches that rarely fire or that cause mistakes, and simplifying or removing them, based on test data and user feedback. Both forms share a principle: a tree should be as simple as possible while still performing well, and complexity that does not earn its keep on honest tests should be removed.

Section 7: Simple Models Beat Complex Ones, Often

Simple models, such as rules, scoring rules, decision trees of limited depth, and counting classifiers, often beat complex models, such as large neural networks, on small data, on narrow jobs, and on jobs where interpretability matters, and that fact surprises beginners who assume complexity equals quality. The reasons trace back to principles from earlier chapters. Simple models have fewer parameters, so they overfit less on small data, where complex models memorise noise. Simple models are easier to debug, because their decisions can be traced to specific rules, scores, or branches, while complex models' decisions are distributed across many parameters that are hard to inspect. Simple models are faster to run and cheaper to maintain, and they are easier to explain to users and regulators, which builds trust and enables accountability, while complex models' opacity can undermine trust even when their accuracy is slightly higher.

That claim should be tested, not assumed, for each job, by the baseline principle from Chapter 4: build a simple model first, measure its honest test performance, then try a more complex model, and adopt complexity only if it clearly beats the simple baseline on test data, considering non-accuracy criteria as well. That test often reveals that complexity helps little or not at all, especially with small data, where complex models' extra capacity is spent memorising rather than generalising. When complexity does help substantially, such as for image or speech tasks where simple models cannot capture patterns at all, then complexity is justified, and its costs, opacity, computation, and maintenance, should be accepted consciously, with mitigations such as explanations, monitoring, and human oversight, rather than ignored because accuracy improved.

Simple models also have a pedagogical advantage: they teach principles clearly, because their workings are visible. A student who understands a decision list, a scoring rule, and a counting classifier understands the core ideas of AI decision-making, pattern finding, evidence combination, and honest testing, in forms she can trace and debate. A student who starts with a complex model, whose workings are opaque, may learn to run it and to report its scores, but not to understand why it succeeds or fails, which limits her ability to fix it, to explain it, or to judge when it should not be used. That is why this book starts with simple models and stays with them for five chapters: simple models build understanding that complex models require, and that understanding transfers, while mere familiarity with complex tools does not.

Finally, simple models are often more fair, because their biases are visible and fixable. A rule that discriminates can be seen and removed; a scoring weight that penalises a group can be inspected and adjusted; a tree branch that splits on a proxy for a protected group can be pruned. A complex model's bias, by contrast, is distributed across parameters, harder to locate, and harder to fix without retraining on new data that may itself be biased. That visibility advantage makes simple models preferable for high-stakes, fairness-sensitive jobs, even when complex models score slightly higher, because fairness, like interpretability, is part of quality, not a separate concern to be traded away for accuracy. When you choose a model, weigh fairness visibility alongside accuracy, and prefer the model whose fairness you can check and guarantee.

Section 8: When Rules Fail

Rules fail in predictable ways, and recognising those failures helps you decide when to move beyond rules to learning, or to hybrid systems. Rules fail when the job's criteria are not clear or stable, such as sorting messages by tone, where tone is subjective and varies by person and context, and no expert can state reliable rules that cover all cases. Rules fail when the number of exceptions grows large, such as a sorting policy with hundreds of special cases, where the rule list becomes so long and tangled that no one understands it fully, and changes risk breaking unrelated cases. Rules fail when patterns are subtle and statistical, such as recognising handwriting, where the mapping depends on many weak clues combined, rather than on a few clear conditions. And rules fail when the world changes quickly, such as slang evolving monthly, where rules written last month become stale and must be constantly updated by hand.

Each failure mode suggests a different remedy, not a single move to learning. Unclear criteria suggest involving users and experts to clarify the job, or reframing it as a learning job where patterns are discovered from labelled examples, provided labels can be obtained reliably. Exception explosion suggests refactoring rules, by grouping exceptions into more general rules, or by moving to a learned model for the messy parts while keeping rules for clear cases and safety limits, a hybrid that preserves rule clarity where it works and uses learning where it does not. Subtle statistical patterns suggest learning directly, with features that capture weak clues and models that combine them. Rapid change suggests a learning system with regular retraining on fresh data, plus monitoring for drift, so the system adapts as the world changes, rather than a static rule list that decays.

Rules also fail through human error in writing them: a rule may contain a typo, a wrong threshold, or a misunderstood condition, causing systematic mistakes that are easy to miss if testing is narrow. That kind of error, a condition that is too broad, is diagnosed by examining false positives and asking what they share that true positives do not, then narrowing the condition, such as checking only the subject line or the first sentence, rather than the whole message.

Another rule failure is conflict and interaction: two rules that are individually correct may interact badly when both apply, producing an outcome neither intended, especially if order is not carefully designed. For example, a rule that files messages from a manager as urgent and a rule that files messages about routine reports as low priority may conflict for a routine report from a manager, and the outcome depends on order, which may not reflect the intended priority. Resolving such interactions requires thinking about rule priorities explicitly, and testing conflict cases deliberately, by constructing test messages that trigger multiple rules and checking that the outcome matches intention. Those conflict tests, added to your test set, prevent surprises in real use, where conflicts are common, because real messages often trigger multiple rules, and the system's behaviour on conflicts is therefore a central part of its quality, not an edge case to be ignored.

Section 9: Combining Rules and Learning

Combining rules and learning, in a hybrid system, often beats either alone, because rules and learning have complementary strengths: rules are clear, auditable, and reliable for known cases and safety limits, while learning is flexible, data-driven, and capable of handling fuzzy, varied cases where rules are hard to state. A hybrid might use rules first, to handle clear cases and to enforce safety constraints, then learning for remaining cases, where rules are uncertain. Or it might use learning first, to suggest a label with a score, then rules to override the suggestion in specific situations, such as when a sensitive word appears or when the score is low, triggering human review.

Designing a hybrid requires deciding the interface between rules and learning: who decides first, who can override whom, and what happens when they disagree? Those interface decisions should be made explicitly and documented, because they determine the system's behaviour on disagreements, which are often the most important cases, where rules and learning conflict, indicating either a rule error, a learning error, or a genuinely ambiguous case that needs human judgement. A common interface is: rules override learning for safety, learning overrides rules for fuzzy cases where no rule applies, and disagreement between them, where both apply but suggest different outcomes, triggers human review or an unsure label, rather than a forced choice.

Hybrids also require testing each part separately as well as together, because a hybrid that performs well overall may hide a part that performs poorly, such as rules that are never triggered because learning always decides first, or learning that is never used because rules always fire first, making one part dead code that gives false confidence. Testing parts separately means measuring rule-only performance, learning-only performance, and hybrid performance, on the same test set, and comparing them, to see whether the hybrid genuinely improves over each part alone, or whether one part dominates and the other is decorative. That comparison, though extra work, is essential for justifying hybrid complexity, because a hybrid that does not beat its parts is not worth its added complexity and maintenance burden, however elegant it sounds in theory.

A practical hybrid exercise is to build a classifier that uses rules for clear questions and clear tasks, and a learned counting classifier for the rest, with a fallback to unsure when both are uncertain. Test that hybrid on your test set, compare it with rule-only and learning-only versions, and note where the hybrid helps and where it does not. That experiment, though small, teaches hybrid design concretely, because you will see cases where rules are right and learning is wrong, cases where the reverse holds, and cases where both are unsure, requiring human review. Those cases, catalogued in your notebook, become a guide for future hybrid designs, showing which kinds of cases suit rules, which suit learning, and which need humans, a classification of cases that is more valuable than any single accuracy number, because it generalises to new jobs where the same case types recur.

Section 10: Models as Tables

Models can be represented as tables, which makes them easy to inspect, edit, and share, especially for simple models like decision lists, scoring rules, and lookup tables. A decision list as a table has rows for rules, with columns for condition, outcome, and priority, ordered by priority, so reading the table top to bottom tells you the decision logic, without reading code. A scoring rule as a table has rows for features, with columns for feature, weight, and notes, so adjusting a weight means editing a cell, not changing code, which is safer and more auditable, because table edits can be reviewed and versioned like data. A lookup table has rows for input patterns and columns for outcomes, useful for jobs where inputs are discrete and outcomes are known for each pattern, such as mapping country codes to regions. Those table representations share a virtue: they separate model logic from program code, making logic editable by non-programmers, such as domain experts, who can review and update tables without touching code.

Table-driven models are implemented by a small interpreter program that reads the table, applies its rows in order or by score, and returns a decision, plus an explanation of which rows contributed. That interpreter, once written and tested, rarely changes, while tables change frequently as policies, data, or user needs evolve, which is a clean separation of concerns: stable code plus evolving data.

Tables also have limits: they work well for discrete, tabular logic, but poorly for continuous, complex, or learned models where logic cannot be expressed as rows without exploding in size. A decision tree with many branches can be represented as a table of paths, but that table may have thousands of rows, becoming unreadable, in which case a tree visualisation may be clearer. A neural network cannot be represented usefully as a table, because its logic is distributed across weights, not localised in rows. Those limits mean table-driven design is best for simple models, where tables remain human-sized, perhaps tens or hundreds of rows, not thousands, and where logic is genuinely tabular, such as policies, scoring, and lookups.

To practise table-driven modelling, convert your decision list classifier into a table: create a CSV file with columns for rule order, condition description, condition pattern, and outcome, then write a Python program that reads that table and applies its rules in order to messages, printing which rule fired for each. Test that table-driven version on your test set, compare its results with your code-based version, and note whether they match, which they should, if the table faithfully represents the code. That exercise teaches table-driven design concretely, and it produces an artefact, a rule table, that you can share with a non-programmer for review, demonstrating the collaboration benefit of tables in a tangible way. It also prepares you for the capstone, where task and note rules may be stored as tables, editable without changing the main program, which will make the capstone more maintainable and more demonstrably well-governed.

Section 11: Lookup Tables and Memorisation

A lookup table is the simplest model of all: a table that maps specific inputs to specific outputs, memorised from training data or from expert knowledge, with no generalisation beyond exact matches. For example, a table mapping exact message texts to labels, built from labelled examples, will label a new message correctly only if it matches a table entry exactly; otherwise, it must fall back to another method or to unsure. That exact-match limitation makes lookup tables poor generalisers, but excellent for cases where exactness matters, such as mapping codes to meanings, where a code must map to exactly one meaning, with no guessing, or for caching, where previously computed results are stored for reuse, saving computation. Lookup tables are therefore not general models, but specialised tools for exact, known mappings, and they should be used for those jobs, not as a substitute for general learning, where new inputs are expected to differ from training inputs.

Lookup tables also illustrate memorisation in its purest form: they memorise, they do not learn patterns, and their performance on training data is perfect, because every training input is in the table, while their performance on new data is poor, unless new data repeats training inputs exactly. That contrast, perfect training, poor test, is the extreme case of overfitting, discussed in Chapter 4, and it teaches a vivid lesson: memorisation is not learning, however perfect it looks on familiar data, because learning requires generalisation to new cases, which memorisation by definition cannot provide. A model that is mostly lookup, with a little generalisation for unmatched inputs, such as a lookup for known messages plus a classifier for unknown ones, is a hybrid that combines memorisation's precision for known cases with generalisation's coverage for new cases, which is a sensible design for jobs where some inputs repeat frequently, while others are novel and need general handling.

Lookup tables have practical uses beyond caching and code mapping: they can store corrections, such as user-corrected labels for specific messages, ensuring that a corrected message is always labelled correctly in future, even if the general model would still mislabel it, which is a form of user-driven override that builds trust, because users see their corrections respected immediately, not just incorporated slowly through retraining. They can store exceptions, such as messages that are known to be tricky and should always be reviewed by a human, regardless of model predictions, which is a safety use of lookup, ensuring that known hard cases do not slip through automatically. And they can store personalisations, such as a user's preferred labels for their own frequent messages, making a general tool feel personal without retraining a shared model on one user's data, which might harm others.

To practise lookup tables, build a small corrections table for your classifier: a dictionary mapping exact message texts that were previously mislabelled to their correct labels, checked before the general classifier runs, so corrections take precedence.

Section 12: Linear Models in Plain Words

A linear model predicts an outcome by adding up weighted features, where each feature contributes its weight times its value, plus a starting value called a bias or intercept. For number prediction, that sum is the predicted number: price equals a base value plus size times a weight plus age times a weight, and so on. For classification, that sum is a score for each label, and the label with the highest score wins, which is exactly the scoring rule from Section 5, now expressed in linear terms. That unity, scoring rules are linear models, is important, because it shows that a familiar, hand-designed scoring rule and a learned linear model are the same kind of model, differing only in how weights are set: by hand versus by learning from data through optimisation, as Chapter 4 described. Understanding linear models therefore unifies hand design and learning, showing them as two ways to set weights for the same structure, rather than as unrelated techniques.

Linear models are popular for several reasons: they are simple, with few parameters relative to complex models, so they overfit less on small data; they are interpretable, because each weight tells you how much its feature contributes, holding others fixed, which allows explanations like "each additional room adds this much to predicted price, according to the model"; they are fast, because prediction is just addition and multiplication, which computers do quickly even for many features; and they are well understood mathematically, with known methods for fitting weights, handling correlated features, and regularising to prevent overfitting, which gives builders confidence in their behaviour. Those virtues make linear models a strong baseline for many jobs, especially tabular data with numeric features, where relationships are approximately linear, or where a linear approximation is good enough, even if the true relationship is slightly curved, because the approximation's simplicity and interpretability outweigh a small accuracy loss from ignoring curvature.

Linear models also have clear limits: they assume relationships are straight lines, so they cannot capture curves, interactions, or thresholds without additional features engineered to represent those patterns, such as squared features for curves, or product features for interactions. For example, a linear model cannot capture that a feature matters only when another feature is present, unless a product feature for their interaction is explicitly added, which requires domain knowledge to anticipate interactions, or data exploration to discover them. That limitation means linear models need good feature engineering to perform well on non-linear jobs, and feature engineering, in turn, requires understanding the job, which is why linear models, though simple in form, still require thoughtful design in features. When feature engineering cannot capture a job's complexity, non-linear models, such as trees, may be needed, but they should be adopted only after linear models with good features have been tried and found insufficient, because linear models' interpretability is worth preserving unless complexity clearly earns its keep.

Section 13: Nearest Neighbours as a Simple Model

Nearest neighbours, introduced in Chapter 4 as a similarity-based method, deserves a second look as a simple model, because its simplicity and transparency make it a valuable baseline and a teaching tool, even though it is rarely the best model for large, complex jobs. As a reminder, nearest neighbours stores training examples and, for a new example, finds the k most similar training examples, according to a similarity or distance measure, and predicts by voting among their labels, or by averaging their numbers for regression. That method has no training phase in the usual sense, beyond storing data, which makes it easy to implement and to update: adding a new training example immediately affects future predictions, without retraining, which is convenient for tools that learn continuously from user corrections. It is also easy to explain: a prediction can be justified by showing its neighbours, with their labels and similarities, allowing users to judge whether those neighbours are truly relevant, which is a strong, intuitive form of explanation that many complex models cannot provide.

Nearest neighbours' performance depends critically on similarity definition, feature scaling, and choice of k, and those dependencies should be explored through testing, not assumed. Similarity for text might be word overlap, for numbers might be distance after scaling, and for mixed data might be a combination, each with trade-offs that affect which neighbours are found and therefore which labels are predicted. Feature scaling matters because distance computed over unscaled features will be dominated by features with large numeric ranges, regardless of their relevance, so scaling features to comparable ranges, or weighting them by relevance, is essential for fair similarity. Choice of k matters because small k, such as one, makes predictions sensitive to single noisy neighbours, increasing variance, while large k smooths predictions over many neighbours, increasing bias but reducing variance, which is the bias-variance trade-off in nearest-neighbour form, tuned by k. Testing different k values on validation data, and reporting the chosen k with reasons, is therefore part of responsible modelling, not an afterthought.

Nearest neighbours also illustrates the curse of dimensionality, a problem where similarity becomes less meaningful as the number of features grows, because in high dimensions, all examples become roughly equally distant from each other, making nearest neighbours arbitrary. That curse means nearest neighbours works best with few, relevant features, not with many, noisy features, and feature selection is therefore especially important for it, more so than for models that learn weights and can downweight irrelevant features automatically. For text, where vocabularies are large, nearest neighbours on raw word counts may suffer from dimensionality, unless features are selected to common, relevant words, or unless similarity is defined carefully, such as using cosine similarity, which measures angle rather than distance and is less sensitive to document length.

Section 14: Voting and Averaging Models

Voting and averaging are simple ways to combine models, and they are the core idea behind ensembles, introduced in Chapter 4, now examined as a standalone decision technique. In voting, each model casts a vote for a label, and the label with the most votes wins, with ties broken by a rule, such as preferring the most trusted model or reporting unsure. In averaging, each model outputs a score or probability, and those scores are averaged, with the highest average winning, which weights confident models more than uncertain ones, unlike plain voting, where each model counts equally regardless of confidence. Both methods are easy to implement with Python's standard library, requiring only that models' outputs be collected and combined, and both often improve over single models, provided the models are diverse, meaning they make different mistakes, so their errors cancel rather than compound, as Chapter 4 explained for ensembles.

Voting and averaging also have a democratic analogy that helps explain their strengths and limits: a group vote is wiser than a single opinion when voters are diverse and independent, but it is no wiser, and may be worse, when voters share the same bias or copy each other, because their shared error is amplified by voting rather than cancelled. That analogy maps directly to models: diverse models, trained on different data, with different features, or with different methods, vote wisely, while similar models, trained on the same data with the same method, vote redundantly, adding computation without improving accuracy. Diversity can be created deliberately, by training models on different subsets, by using different feature sets, or by combining rules, counting, and nearest neighbours, which err differently by design. Checking diversity, by comparing models' mistake patterns on test data, is therefore a prerequisite for voting: if models' mistakes overlap heavily, voting will not help much, and effort should go to creating diversity, not to adding more similar models.

Averaging requires an additional care: scores from different models must be comparable before averaging, because a model that outputs probabilities from zero to one and a model that outputs raw scores from zero to a hundred cannot be averaged meaningfully without scaling to a common range. Scaling, such as normalising each model's scores to zero to one on validation data, makes averaging fair, but it also introduces a tuning step that must be done on validation, not test, to avoid leakage. Voting avoids that scaling problem, because votes are counts, not scores, but it loses confidence information, treating a confident prediction and a hesitant one equally, which may be wasteful when confidence is informative. Choosing between voting and averaging therefore depends on whether models' confidences are trustworthy and comparable: if they are, averaging uses more information; if they are not, voting is safer, because it does not trust uncalibrated confidences. That choice, like all modelling choices, should be tested on validation data, not assumed.

Section 15: Confidence and Abstaining

Confidence is a model's estimate of how likely its prediction is to be correct, and abstaining is the decision to not predict, or to ask a human, when confidence is low. Together, they form one of the most important safety practices in simple models: a model that knows when it does not know, and says so, is more trustworthy than one that always guesses, however accurate its guesses are on average. Confidence can come from scores, such as the margin between the top two label scores in a classifier, where a large margin indicates confidence and a small margin indicates uncertainty, or from probabilities, where a high top probability indicates confidence, or from neighbour agreement in nearest neighbours, where unanimous neighbours indicate confidence and split neighbours indicate uncertainty. Each confidence signal is imperfect, but each is more informative than no signal, and each enables abstaining: if confidence falls below a threshold, the model abstains, reporting unsure or requesting human review, rather than guessing and possibly misleading a user who would otherwise trust the guess.

Abstaining requires designing what happens when a model abstains. For a message sorter, abstaining might mean placing the message in an "unsure" folder for human sorting, which is safe and useful, because humans can sort unsure messages quickly, while automatically mis-sorted messages might be missed entirely. For a medical flagger, abstaining might mean flagging for clinician review, which is essential, because a wrong automatic decision could harm a patient, while a review delay is merely inconvenient. For a search tool, abstaining might mean saying "no confident match found, here are the closest matches for you to judge", which respects user judgement rather than pretending certainty. In each case, abstaining is not a failure; it is a designed behaviour that trades coverage, the proportion of cases decided automatically, for accuracy on decided cases, which is a trade-off that should be tuned to user costs: when mistakes are costly, abstain more; when delay is costly, abstain less. That tuning, like threshold tuning, should be done on validation data, with coverage and accuracy on decided cases reported together, so users can see what abstaining buys and costs.

Confidence must also be calibrated, meaning that when a model says it is eighty percent confident, it should be right about eighty percent of the time on cases with that confidence level. Uncalibrated confidence, such as a model that says ninety-nine percent but is right only seventy percent of the time, misleads users into over-trust, which is worse than no confidence at all, because it creates false certainty. Calibration can be checked by grouping predictions by confidence level and comparing predicted confidence with actual accuracy in each group, a simple procedure that reveals overconfidence or underconfidence patterns. If calibration is poor, confidence scores can be adjusted, by scaling or by using validation data to map raw scores to observed accuracies, a process called calibration, which improves trustworthiness without changing predictions themselves, only the confidence attached to them.

To practise confidence and abstaining, add a confidence margin to your scored classifier: compute the difference between the top two label scores, and abstain, reporting unsure, when that margin is below a threshold you choose.

Section 16: Rules for Safety: Hard Constraints

Hard constraints are rules that must never be violated, regardless of what any model predicts, how confident it is, or how beneficial violating them might seem in a particular case. Examples include: never reveal a password, never delete data without a backup, never send a message without approval, never file a medical message as low priority without human review, and never use a protected attribute, such as ethnicity, as a feature for a decision that affects opportunities. Those constraints are hard because they are absolute, not weighted: they are not scores to be balanced against other scores, but boundaries that define acceptable behaviour, and any system that violates them, even once, has failed in a way that accuracy cannot compensate for. Designing hard constraints, stating them explicitly, implementing them as checks that run before or after any model, and testing that they hold under all inputs, including adversarial ones, is a core safety practice for AI tools, and it is one that simple rule systems implement naturally, because a hard constraint is simply a rule placed first, with no exceptions, in a decision list.

Hard constraints differ from soft preferences, such as preferring brevity in summaries or preferring common words in predictions, which can be traded off against other goals. Soft preferences are optimised; hard constraints are enforced. That distinction matters for design and for testing: soft preferences are judged by how well they are balanced, while hard constraints are judged by whether they ever fail, which is a binary test, pass or fail, not a graded score. A system that violates a hard constraint ninety-nine percent less often than before has still failed, if the constraint is truly hard, because the one violation may cause serious harm, such as leaking a password or deleting irreplaceable data. That binary nature means hard constraints need stronger testing, including adversarial testing with inputs designed to trick the system into violating them, and stronger implementation, such as checks in multiple places, defence in depth, rather than a single check that, if bypassed or buggy, leaves the system unprotected. Defence in depth, checking a constraint at input, at model output, and before action, is a standard safety engineering practice, and it applies to AI tools as well as to traditional software, because single points of failure are fragile, however carefully they are implemented.

Choosing hard constraints requires judgement about what is truly non-negotiable for a job, versus what is merely preferred. Over-constraining a system, by making every preference hard, makes it brittle and unusable, because it will refuse or fail on many legitimate cases where a trade-off would have been acceptable.

Section 17: Decision-Making Under Cost

Decision-making under cost means choosing actions not just by what is most likely, but by what costs least when wrong, taking into account both probabilities and consequences. A classifier that predicts a label with eighty percent probability might still be the wrong choice to act on, if acting on that label when wrong is very costly, while acting on a less likely label, or abstaining, is cheap when wrong. For example, flagging a message as spam when it is actually an important job offer is costly, because the offer may be missed, while keeping a spam message in the inbox is merely annoying, so a spam filter should require high confidence before filing as spam, even if spam is the most likely label at lower confidence. That cost-sensitive decision rule, act only when expected cost is lowest, not merely when probability is highest, is more rational than probability-only decision-making, as Chapter 2 discussed for rationality, and it is essential for tools where mistake costs are asymmetric, which is most real tools, because few mistakes cost equally in all directions.

Cost-sensitive decisions require a cost table, also called a loss matrix, that states, for each true label and each predicted action, how costly that combination is. For message sorting, the cost of mistaking a task for a note, causing it to be missed, might be ten, while the cost of mistaking a note for a task, causing extra checking, might be one, reflecting that misses are ten times worse than false alarms for tasks. With such a table, a decision rule can compute expected cost for each action, by weighting each possible true label's cost by its probability, and choose the action with lowest expected cost, which may differ from the most likely label's action when costs are asymmetric. That computation, though simple in principle, requires estimating costs, which are often subjective and context-dependent, and it requires probabilities that are reasonably calibrated, because expected cost calculations are only as good as the probabilities and costs that feed them. Estimating costs with users, through discussion of real consequences, is therefore part of cost-sensitive design, not a technical detail to be guessed by builders alone, because users bear the costs and understand them best.

Cost-sensitive thinking also changes evaluation: instead of reporting accuracy alone, report expected cost, or total cost on test data, computed from the cost table and the confusion matrix, which shows whether a model is actually useful given real costs, not just accurate in a cost-blind sense. A model with lower accuracy but lower expected cost, because it avoids costly mistakes at the expense of cheap ones, is better for users than a model with higher accuracy but higher expected cost, because it causes less harm overall, even though it makes more mistakes in total.

To practise cost-sensitive decisions, define a cost table for your message classifier, with costs for each confusion, such as task-as-note costing ten, question-as-task costing three, and note-as-task costing one, reflecting that missing tasks is worst, misrouting questions is moderate, and over-flagging notes is minor.

Section 18: Explainable Simple Models

Explainable simple models are models whose decisions can be explained in plain words, with evidence a user can check, without requiring technical expertise to understand the explanation. Rules explain by citing the rule that fired; scoring rules explain by listing contributing features and weights; decision trees explain by tracing the path from root to leaf, listing questions and answers along the way; nearest neighbours explains by showing neighbours and their labels; counting classifiers explain by showing word counts and scores for each label. Those explanations share a quality: they are faithful, meaning they reflect the model's actual decision process, not a plausible-sounding story invented afterwards, and they are checkable, meaning a user can verify the evidence, such as checking that a message does contain the cited word, or that neighbours do have the cited labels. Faithfulness and checkability are the two criteria for a good explanation, and simple models satisfy them naturally, because their processes are transparent, while complex models often require post-hoc explanation methods that approximate, rather than faithfully reproduce, their processes, with a risk of misleading explanations that sound convincing but do not reflect actual computation.

Explaining a simple model well requires tailoring the explanation to its audience and purpose, as Chapter 2 discussed for explanations in general. A user sorting messages needs a short explanation: "Labelled task because it contains 'buy' and 'tomorrow', with a task score of five versus a question score of one." A builder debugging needs more detail: full scores for all labels, all contributing features, and the threshold or decision rule applied. A regulator or auditor needs a different kind: what rules, weights, or training data were in effect, how the model was tested, and what its known limits are. One explanation does not fit all, and designing explanations for each audience, with appropriate detail and language, is part of tool design, not an afterthought. That design should be tested with real users from each audience, asking whether they understand the explanation, whether they can check it, and whether it helps them decide whether to trust the prediction, because an explanation that confuses or misleads is worse than no explanation, as Chapter 2 warned for biased explanations that hide counter-evidence.

Explanations also have a duty to include counter-evidence and uncertainty, not just supporting evidence, because a one-sided explanation can mislead users into over-trust, even when the model's decision was close or contested. For a scored classifier, that means showing not only the winning label's score, but also the runner-up's score and the margin between them, so users can see whether the decision was confident or close. For a decision tree, it means noting when a leaf contains mixed labels, indicating that the path does not guarantee the label, only makes it likely. For nearest neighbours, it means showing neighbours that disagree, not just those that agree, so users can see dissent among neighbours.

To practise explainable simple models, add explanation output to each of your classifiers: for the rule classifier, print the firing rule; for the scored classifier, print scores for all labels and the top contributing words; for the nearest-neighbour classifier, print neighbours and their labels.

Section 19: Testing Simple Models

Testing simple models follows the same honest principles as testing learned models, with some simplifications and some additional checks specific to rules and scores. The core remains: fixed test set, set aside before building or tuning, with labels written before running, accuracy and confusion matrices reported, breakdowns by label and group, and test set size stated, so scores can be interpreted with appropriate caution. Those core practices, from Chapters 1, 3, and 4, apply unchanged to simple models, because simple models can also overfit, through rule tuning on test data, or through weight tuning that chases test scores, and they can also be biased, through rules or weights that favour one group, which only group breakdowns reveal. Simplicity does not exempt a model from honest testing; it merely makes testing easier, because decisions are traceable and mistakes are diagnosable, which should make testing more thorough, not less, because there is less excuse for skipping it when tracing is easy.

Additional checks for simple models include rule coverage: which rules fire, how often, and which rules never fire, because a rule that never fires on test data may be dead, or may cover a rare but important case that test data missed, and either way, its presence should be justified or it should be removed, to keep the system clean. Weight sensitivity: how much do predictions change when weights are varied slightly, because a model whose predictions flip with tiny weight changes is fragile and should be stabilised, by smoothing weights or by using more robust decision rules, such as requiring a margin before deciding, rather than deciding on a one-point difference. Threshold sensitivity: how much do precision and recall change when thresholds are varied, because a model whose performance is highly threshold-sensitive needs careful threshold tuning and monitoring, while one that is stable across thresholds is more robust to drift. Those sensitivity checks, coverage, weight, and threshold, are simple to run for rule and scoring models, by varying one parameter at a time and retesting, and they reveal fragility that accuracy alone hides, which is why they should be part of simple model testing, not just accuracy reporting.

Testing simple models also includes adversarial and edge-case testing, as Chapter 1 urged for classifiers generally, but with rule-specific edges: empty inputs, inputs that trigger multiple rules, inputs that trigger no rules, inputs with unusual capitalisation, punctuation, or spelling, and inputs designed to bypass safety rules, such as private words in different cases or with punctuation inserted. Those edge cases are where rule systems most often fail, because rules are written for typical inputs and may not anticipate variations, and testing them deliberately, with a dedicated edge-case test set, catches failures before users do.

Finally, testing simple models includes testing explanations, as Section 18 discussed: do explanations match actual decisions, are they understandable to users, and do they help users judge trustworthiness?

Section 20: A Simple Model Toolkit

A simple model toolkit is a collection of reusable components for building rule, scoring, tree, and neighbour models, with shared utilities for loading data, splitting test sets, evaluating with confusion matrices, and explaining predictions, all using Python's standard library. Building such a toolkit, even a tiny one for personal use, is valuable, because it turns one-off model scripts into reusable infrastructure, saving time on future projects and enforcing consistent evaluation practices across projects, which improves comparability and honesty. A toolkit might include functions for reading CSV data into lists of dictionaries, for splitting data stratified by label, for computing accuracy, precision, recall, and confusion matrices, for printing explanations, and for running a model on a test set and reporting results in a standard format. Those functions, once written and tested, can be imported by each new model script, rather than rewritten each time, reducing bugs and ensuring that evaluation is done the same way every time, which is a professionalism habit that scales from personal projects to team projects, where shared evaluation code prevents disputes about whose scores are comparable.

Building a toolkit also forces you to think about interfaces: what inputs does each model function take, what outputs does it return, and how do evaluation functions consume those outputs? Good interfaces are simple and consistent: models take an input, such as a message text, and return a label plus, optionally, scores and explanation, in a standard format, such as a dictionary with keys for label, scores, and explanation, so evaluation code can handle any model uniformly, whether it is rule-based, scoring, tree, or neighbour-based. That uniformity enables model comparison, from Chapter 4, because different models can be evaluated by the same code on the same test set, producing comparable reports, and it enables ensembles and hybrids, because models with a common interface can be combined by voting or averaging without custom code for each combination. Interface design, though it sounds abstract, is therefore a practical skill that determines how easily models can be compared, combined, and maintained, and it is worth investing time in, even for a tiny personal toolkit, because it pays off in every future project that reuses it.

A toolkit should also include documentation and tests for itself: a short note describing each function, its inputs, outputs, and purpose, plus a few test cases that verify each function works as intended, such as a test that a confusion matrix function counts correctly on a known example, or that a splitting function produces stratified, non-overlapping sets. Those toolkit tests, though extra work, prevent a subtle danger: a bug in evaluation code can make all models look better or worse than they are, misleading every project that uses the toolkit, and only toolkit tests catch such bugs, because model tests alone cannot distinguish model errors from evaluation errors.

Finally, a toolkit should be versioned and shared thoughtfully, with a changelog noting what changed and why, and with a licence or sharing note if shared with others, as Chapter 3 discussed for data sharing.

Section 21: Rules in Everyday Software

Rules are everywhere in everyday software, even when they are not called AI. A word processor's autocorrect follows rules: if a word matches a known misspelling, replace it with the correction. An email client's filters follow rules: if a message is from a certain sender and contains a certain word, move it to a folder. A thermostat follows rules: if the temperature falls below a set point, turn on the heating. A spreadsheet's conditional formatting follows rules: if a value exceeds a threshold, colour it red. Those everyday rules are simple models, and they work well because their jobs are clear, their conditions are checkable, and their outcomes are predictable. Recognising rules in everyday software demystifies AI decision-making: much of what feels intelligent in software is simply rules, applied consistently and quickly, at a scale no person could match by hand.

Everyday rules also show how rules accumulate and interact in real systems. An email client with ten filters works well, but one with a hundred filters, added over years by different people for different reasons, may behave unpredictably, because filters interact, shadow each other, and conflict in ways no single author anticipated. That accumulation, rule sprawl, is the everyday version of the exception explosion from Section 8, and it is managed by the same practices: documenting rules, ordering them by specificity and priority, testing them on representative examples, and periodically reviewing and pruning rules that no longer fire or that cause mistakes. Users who manage their own filters learn those practices informally, by noticing when a filter misfiles a message and adjusting it, which is the same diagnostic loop builders use for professional rule systems, at a personal scale. That continuity, from personal filters to professional rule systems, shows that rule skills are everyday skills, formalised, not exotic expertise.

Another everyday lesson is that rules need user control. A user who cannot see, edit, or disable a rule that affects her messages, documents, or heating will feel controlled by software rather than served by it, and she may work around the rule, disable the whole system, or abandon the tool, even if the rule is usually correct. Good everyday software therefore exposes its rules in a readable form, allows users to edit them, and explains its decisions in terms of the rules that fired, which is the explanation principle from Section 18 applied to ordinary tools. That transparency is not just good design; it is respect for user autonomy, because rules that affect people's work, communication, or comfort should be inspectable and contestable by those people, not hidden in code they cannot see. Builders of AI tools should follow the same principle, exposing simple models' rules and scores to users, rather than hiding them behind confident interfaces that discourage checking.

Finally, everyday rules remind you that simple models are the backbone of software, not a primitive stage to be outgrown. Even the most advanced AI products rely on rules for input validation, output filtering, safety constraints, and fallback behaviour, because rules provide guarantees that learned models cannot. A chatbot may generate text with a learned model, but rules check that text for disallowed content before showing it.

Section 22: Decisions With Multiple Criteria

Many real decisions involve multiple criteria that must be balanced, not a single score or a single rule. Choosing a message to answer first might involve urgency, sender importance, deadline, and length, all at once. Choosing a house to buy might involve price, location, size, and condition. Choosing a treatment might involve effectiveness, side effects, cost, and patient preference. Multi-criteria decisions cannot be reduced to a single threshold without losing information, because a message that is urgent but long may deserve different handling from one that is urgent and short, and a single urgency score that adds urgency and length together may hide that distinction. Handling multiple criteria well requires keeping them separate during reasoning, then combining them transparently, so trade-offs are visible and debatable, rather than hidden inside a single number that no one can unpack.

One approach to multi-criteria decisions is lexicographic ordering: rank criteria by priority, and decide by the highest-priority criterion first, using lower criteria only to break ties. For messages, that might mean: first, urgency; if urgency ties, sender importance; if that ties, deadline; and so on. Lexicographic ordering is simple and transparent, because priorities are explicit, and it matches how people often decide: safety first, then effectiveness, then cost, for example. Its limitation is rigidity: it never allows a lower criterion to outweigh a higher one, however large the difference in the lower criterion and however small the difference in the higher one, which can lead to decisions that feel wrong, such as choosing a slightly more urgent but vastly longer task over a slightly less urgent but much quicker one, when the quicker one would have been better overall. That rigidity should be recognised and, where it matters, softened by allowing trade-offs within bands, such as treating urgencies within a small range as tied, so lower criteria can break those near-ties sensibly.

Another approach is weighted scoring, where each criterion is scored, weighted by importance, and added into a total, as Section 5 described for scoring rules. Weighted scoring allows trade-offs: a large advantage in one criterion can outweigh a small disadvantage in another, which lexicographic ordering forbids. Its limitation is that weights are hard to set correctly, because they encode value judgements about how much one criterion is worth relative to another, and those judgements are often contested, uncertain, or context-dependent. Weights set by one person may not match another's values, and weights that work for one decision may not work for another, even within the same job, because stakes change. Weighted scoring therefore requires sensitivity analysis: trying different weights and seeing how decisions change, and reporting decisions with their criterion scores, so users can see trade-offs and override weights where they disagree, rather than accepting a single weighted total as objective truth, when it is actually a value-laden combination.

A third approach is Pareto reasoning: an option is Pareto-optimal if no other option is better on all criteria, and dominated options, those worse on all criteria than some other option, can be eliminated without trade-off debate, while Pareto-optimal options require genuine value judgements to choose among, because each excels on different criteria.

Section 23: Defaults: The Power of Doing Nothing Special

Defaults are the outcomes that happen when no rule, score, or model triggers a special decision: the fallback label, the standard setting, the usual folder, the normal priority. Defaults are powerful, because most cases fall through to them, especially in systems where special rules cover only a minority of clear cases and the rest are routine. A message classifier's default of "note" will label most ambiguous messages, a task helper's default priority of "medium" will apply to most tasks without explicit priorities, and a software's default settings will govern most users' experience, because most users never change defaults, whether from satisfaction, inertia, or unawareness. That power means defaults must be chosen carefully, not as afterthoughts, because a default that is wrong for most cases will cause more harm in total than a special rule that is wrong for a few cases, simply because defaults apply so widely. Choosing defaults is therefore a central design decision, deserving the same care as choosing rules and weights, with testing on typical cases, not just on special ones.

Good defaults share several qualities: they are safe, meaning that if they are wrong, the harm is low and reversible, such as labelling an ambiguous message as a note to be reviewed, rather than filing it as spam to be hidden; they are common, meaning they match the most frequent true outcome, so they are right more often than not, such as defaulting to the majority label for ambiguous cases, which maximises accuracy on those cases, though not necessarily fairness, if the majority label reflects majority-group patterns; and they are transparent, meaning users know what the default is and can change it, rather than discovering it only when it causes a surprise, such as a message filed unexpectedly or a setting changed silently. Those qualities, safe, common, and transparent, should guide default choice for every fallback in your tools, and defaults should be documented alongside rules, so reviewers can judge whether they meet those qualities, rather than assuming defaults are neutral, when they are actually powerful policy choices disguised as technical details.

Defaults also interact with fairness, because a default that matches the majority may systematically disadvantage minorities whose typical cases differ from the majority's. For example, a classifier defaulting ambiguous messages to "note" may work well for users whose ambiguous messages are usually notes, but poorly for users whose ambiguous messages are usually tasks, causing their tasks to be missed more often. That disparity, caused by a seemingly neutral default, is a fairness issue that only group breakdowns reveal, as earlier chapters urged, and it should be addressed by considering group-specific defaults, or by abstaining, reporting unsure, rather than defaulting, for ambiguous cases where group patterns differ. Abstaining as a default, instead of guessing the majority label, is often fairer, because it does not systematically favour the majority, though it requires human review capacity for unsure cases, which is a cost to be weighed against fairness gains.

To practise default design, examine your classifier's fallback: what label does it default to, why did you choose it, is it safe if wrong, is it common, and is it transparent to users?

Section 24: Keeping Rules Up to Date

Rules, like data and models, decay over time as the world changes, policies update, language evolves, and user needs shift, so keeping rules up to date is a maintenance task that must be planned, not left to chance. A sorting rule based on last year's product names will miss this year's products. A safety rule based on last year's threats will miss new threats. A policy rule based on last year's regulations will violate this year's law if regulations changed and rules did not. That decay, rule drift, parallels data drift and model drift from earlier chapters, and it is managed by similar routines: periodic review, monitoring for rule performance, feedback from users about rule mistakes, and scheduled updates, with versioning and testing, so rule changes are deliberate, documented, and reversible, rather than ad hoc edits that introduce new errors while fixing old ones. A rule system without maintenance is a snapshot that slowly becomes wrong, however correct it was at launch, and its wrongness may go unnoticed, because rules, unlike models, do not report confidence or drift; they simply apply, confidently, even when outdated, which makes their decay especially dangerous if unmonitored.

A practical maintenance routine for small rule systems is simple: every month, or every quarter depending on how fast your domain changes, review rule performance on fresh test data, including new examples collected since the last review, and note which rules fired, which caused mistakes, and which never fired. Remove or update rules that cause mistakes, add rules for new patterns that test data reveals, and retest the whole system on both old and new test sets, to ensure updates did not break previously correct behaviour, which is regression testing for rules, analogous to regression testing for code. Document each review in a changelog, noting what changed, why, and what test results were before and after, so rule history is traceable and future reviewers can understand why rules look as they do. That routine, though it takes under an hour for a small system, prevents most rule decay, because it catches outdated rules early, when fixes are small, rather than late, when outdated rules have caused many mistakes and users have lost trust.

Rule maintenance also involves sunsetting: removing rules that are no longer needed, because the cases they covered no longer occur, or because a more general rule now covers them better, or because the policy they implemented has changed. Sunsetting is hard, because builders fear removing a rule that might still be needed for a rare case, and so rule lists grow by addition without subtraction, becoming bloated and tangled, as Section 8 warned for exception explosion.

Finally, rule maintenance should involve users and domain experts, not just builders, because users encounter rule mistakes first, in real use, and experts understand policy changes first, in their domains, and both groups' input is essential for keeping rules current.

Section 26: Rules for Text: Patterns in Words

Text rules use patterns in words, punctuation, and structure to decide, and they are among the most common rules in AI tools, because text is the most common input. A text rule might say: if a message contains a question mark, treat it as a question; if it contains an action word at the start, treat it as a task; if it is longer than a hundred words, treat it as a document rather than a note. Those rules are easy to state, easy to test, and easy to explain, which is why they appear in spam filters, sorters, and search tools everywhere, even in systems that also use learned models, where text rules often handle clear cases, safety checks, and fallbacks. Text rules also have a special advantage: they can be written and tested by people who understand language, without needing large datasets, because the patterns they encode, such as question marks indicating questions, are part of shared linguistic knowledge, not secrets hidden in data that only learning can reveal.

Text rules need care with variation, because language varies in spelling, capitalisation, punctuation, and dialect, and a rule that matches only one exact form will miss variants that mean the same thing. A rule checking for the word "buy" in lower case will miss "Buy" at the start of a sentence, unless text is lower-cased before checking, which is a standard preprocessing step for text rules, as Chapter 3 discussed for cleaning. A rule checking for a question mark will miss questions without question marks, such as "I wonder what time dinner is", which is phrased as a statement but functions as a question, and which requires a more sophisticated rule, such as checking for question words plus a verb pattern, or a learned model, to catch reliably. Those variation challenges mean text rules should be tested on varied language, including different capitalisations, spellings, dialects, and phrasings, not just on clean textbook examples, and they should be designed with preprocessing, such as lower-casing and punctuation handling, that normalises variation where it does not change meaning, while preserving variation where it does, such as question marks for question detection, which must not be stripped away by overzealous cleaning.

Text rules also need care with context, because the same word or pattern can mean different things in different contexts, as Chapter 2 discussed for ambiguity. The word "book" can mean reserve or a physical object, "bank" can mean a river edge or a financial institution, and "fine" can mean satisfactory or a penalty, and a rule that treats any occurrence of such a word as a single meaning will misclassify messages where context indicates the other meaning. Handling context with rules requires more complex conditions, such as checking neighbouring words, or checking the message's topic, or combining multiple clues, which quickly becomes a scoring rule or a decision tree, rather than a single simple rule.

Finally, text rules should be documented with examples, including both examples they handle correctly and examples they handle incorrectly, with reasons, so future editors understand their scope and limits.

Section 27: Rules for Numbers: Ranges and Bands

Number rules use ranges and bands to decide: if a value falls in a certain range, take a certain action. If a temperature exceeds thirty-five degrees, issue a heat warning. If a score is below fifty, flag it as failing. If a price is between one hundred and two hundred, classify it as mid-range. Those range rules are intuitive, because people naturally think in bands, such as cold, mild, and hot, or low, medium, and high, rather than in exact numbers, and they are easy to explain: a value is flagged because it falls in a flagged band, with the band boundaries stated explicitly, so users can debate whether boundaries are correct, rather than debating a black-box score. Range rules are also easy to test: for each band boundary, test values just below, exactly at, and just above the boundary, to ensure the rule behaves as intended at edges, where off-by-one errors and inclusive-versus-exclusive mistakes are most common and most consequential, such as a rule that should flag values above fifty but, due to a greater-than versus greater-than-or-equal error, misses exactly fifty, which may be the most important case, such as a passing threshold.

Choosing band boundaries requires judgement, data, and sometimes regulation: a heat warning threshold might be set by health authorities based on medical evidence, a passing score might be set by an exam board based on standards, and a price band might be set by market analysis based on customer segments. Those boundaries are not discovered by rules themselves; they are inputs to rules, decided by humans with domain knowledge, and they should be documented with reasons, so future reviewers can judge whether boundaries remain appropriate as evidence, standards, or markets change. Boundaries that were sensible last year may become outdated, as Chapter 24 discussed for rule maintenance generally, and range rules are especially vulnerable to boundary drift, because a small shift in data distribution can move many cases across a fixed boundary, changing a system's behaviour dramatically without any rule change, which is why range rules should be monitored: track how many cases fall in each band over time, and investigate sudden shifts, which may indicate data drift, a boundary that needs updating, or a real change in the world that the system should reflect, rather than a rule error to be fixed by changing code alone.

Range rules also interact with fairness, because band boundaries can systematically advantage or disadvantage groups whose values cluster near a boundary. For example, a test score threshold for passing, set at fifty, will pass students just above fifty and fail those just below, even though their true abilities may be nearly identical, differing only by measurement noise, and if one group's scores cluster just below the threshold due to measurement bias or unequal preparation, that group will fail disproportionately, even if the threshold appears neutral.

To practise range rules, build a tiny band classifier for message lengths: short messages under ten words, medium messages between ten and thirty words, and long messages over thirty words, with tests for values at exactly ten and thirty, to check inclusive versus exclusive boundaries.

Section 28: Combining Scores and Rules

Combining scores and rules, where a scoring system produces a number and a rule system decides what to do with that number, is a common and effective architecture, because it separates evidence combination, scoring, from action selection, rules, allowing each to be designed, tested, and updated independently. For example, a classifier might score messages for urgency, producing a score from zero to ten, and a rule system might decide: if the score is above eight, flag immediately; if it is between five and eight, file for review today; if it is below five, file normally. That separation is clean: scoring can be improved by adjusting weights or adding features, without changing action rules, and action rules can be changed by policy, such as lowering the immediate-flag threshold during a crisis, without retraining the scorer, which is valuable when policies change faster than models can be retrained, or when different users need different action thresholds for the same scores, such as a manager who wants immediate flags at seven versus a colleague who wants them at nine, personalising actions without personalising scores.

That separation also enables explanation at two levels: the score can be explained by its contributing features and weights, and the action can be explained by the rule that mapped the score to the action, such as "score eight point five, above the immediate-flag threshold of eight, therefore flagged immediately". That two-level explanation is clearer than a single black-box decision, because it separates why the evidence was strong, score explanation, from why strong evidence led to this action, rule explanation, and users can challenge either level independently: they might agree the score is high but argue the threshold should be higher, or agree the threshold is correct but argue the score is wrong due to a misleading feature. That separability of challenges makes combined systems more governable than monolithic systems, where score and action are fused and cannot be debated separately, which is why combined architectures are preferred for regulated and high-stakes jobs, where governance requires tracing decisions to distinct, debatable components, rather than to a single opaque model that cannot be decomposed for review.

Combining scores and rules also requires care about calibration, because rules that act on scores assume scores mean what they claim to mean, such as a score of eight meaning high urgency, and if scores are poorly calibrated, as Section 15 discussed for confidence, then rules acting on them will misfire, flagging low-urgency cases as urgent or missing high-urgency cases, even though the rules themselves are correct given true scores. Calibration should therefore be checked before combining: group predictions by score bands, compare scores with actual outcomes in each band, and adjust scores or thresholds until scores align with reality, on validation data, not test data, to avoid leakage.

Section 29: Simple Models in Teams

Simple models are especially valuable in teams, because they can be understood, debated, and maintained by people with varied expertise, not just by machine learning specialists. A rule list can be reviewed by a domain expert who understands the job but not programming, because rules are written in plain language or in simple if-then form. A scoring table can be reviewed by a manager who understands costs and priorities, because weights and thresholds are explicit numbers that can be debated in terms of trade-offs. A decision tree can be reviewed by a user who will be affected by its decisions, because its path can be traced and questioned: why does this question come first, why is this threshold set here, what happens for cases like mine? That broad reviewability is a major advantage for team-built tools, because it allows diverse stakeholders, experts, managers, users, and auditors, to participate in design, testing, and governance, catching biases, errors, and unfairness that a single specialist, working alone with a complex model, might miss, however skilled, because no single perspective covers all aspects of a real job's context, values, and risks.

Team use of simple models also requires shared practices: versioning of rules and tables, as Chapter 3 discussed for data and Chapter 5 for tables, so changes can be traced to authors, dates, and reasons; testing with shared test sets, so all team members evaluate against the same standard, rather than each using private examples that favour their own changes; and documentation of design decisions, such as why a rule was added, why a weight was set, or why a threshold was chosen, so future team members, including those who join after original builders leave, can understand and maintain the system without reverse-engineering it from code alone. Those practices, though they sound bureaucratic, are what make team-built simple models sustainable over time, because they prevent knowledge loss, enable collaboration without conflict, and create an audit trail that regulators and users can inspect. A team that follows those practices will build simple models that last, while a team that builds simple models informally, without versioning, shared tests, or documentation, will find its models decay into tangled, undocumented lists that no one understands, which is the same failure mode as exception explosion, now at team scale, where coordination failures compound individual rule errors.

Simple models also facilitate division of labour in teams: one person can own rules for clear cases, another can own scoring for fuzzy cases, a third can own safety constraints, and a fourth can own testing and evaluation, with interfaces between their parts defined by scores, labels, and action rules, as Section 28 described for combining scores and rules. That division works because simple models' interfaces are explicit and checkable, unlike complex models' interfaces, which are often implicit in shared parameters that are hard to partition among owners.

Finally, simple models in teams require a culture of honest evaluation and open debate, where team members can challenge rules, weights, and thresholds without defensiveness, because those components are explicit and therefore challengeable, unlike complex models where challenges often devolve into debates about training data or architecture that are hard to resolve without retraining.

Section 30: Choosing a Simple Model

Choosing a simple model, among rules, decision lists, scoring rules, trees, nearest neighbours, linear models, and lookup tables, is a decision that should be made by matching model properties to job requirements, not by habit or fashion. Start by listing job requirements: what inputs are available, what outputs are needed, how much data exists, how important is interpretability, how important is speed, how important is fairness visibility, and what are the costs of different mistakes? Then match models to those requirements: rules fit clear, stable, auditable jobs with little data; scoring rules fit jobs with many weak clues that must combine transparently; trees fit jobs with interactions and mixed feature types where paths must be explainable; nearest neighbours fits jobs with few relevant features where showing similar examples is the best explanation; linear models fit tabular numeric jobs where weights must be interpretable; lookup tables fit exact, repeated mappings and corrections. That matching, requirements first, model second, prevents the common error of choosing a favourite model and forcing it onto a job it does not fit, which leads to poor performance, opacity, or maintenance burden that could have been avoided by a better-matched, simpler choice.

Choosing also involves testing candidates, as Chapter 4 described for model selection generally, but with simple models, testing is cheaper and more informative, because candidates can be built quickly, evaluated on the same test set with the same metrics, and compared not only on accuracy, but on interpretability, speed, fairness, and maintainability, which are easier to judge for simple models than for complex ones, because their workings are visible. Build at least two candidates for any non-trivial job, such as a rule list and a scoring rule, or a tree and a nearest-neighbour model, evaluate both with confusion matrices, precision and recall, group breakdowns, and explanation samples, and choose based on the full picture, not on accuracy alone, documenting reasons for the choice, so future builders can revisit it if requirements change. That comparative testing, though it takes extra time upfront, saves time later, because it prevents committing to a poorly matched model that must be replaced after deployment, when replacement is costly and disruptive, whereas choosing well upfront, based on evidence, produces a tool that lasts and that stakeholders trust, because its selection was justified, not merely asserted.

A useful tie-breaker when candidates perform similarly is to prefer the model that is easiest to explain to its users, easiest to maintain by its builders, and easiest to audit by its reviewers, in that order, because those ease-of-use properties compound over a tool's lifetime, while small accuracy differences often vanish on new data, as Chapter 4 discussed for simplicity as a tie-breaker.

Finally, choosing a simple model is not a one-time decision, as Chapter 4 discussed for model selection generally: as data grows, jobs change, or new models become available, the best simple model may change, and revisiting the choice periodically, with fresh evaluation on current data, keeps tools current.

Section 31: Models That Learn Rules

Some models learn rules from data, rather than having rules written by hand, and they combine the transparency of rules with the data-driven grounding of learning, which makes them valuable for jobs where both matter. A decision tree learner, for example, learns rules in the form of paths: each path from root to leaf is a rule, such as "if question mark and question word, then question", discovered because training data shows that combination predicts questions well, not because a programmer guessed it. A rule learner, more generally, searches for feature combinations that predict labels accurately and cover many examples, using criteria like support and confidence from Chapter 4, and it returns a set of rules, ordered or unordered, that can be read, debated, and edited like hand-written rules, but that are grounded in data evidence, which gives them an advantage over hand-written rules when experts' intuitions are incomplete or biased, because data may reveal patterns experts missed, such as a word combination that predicts a label strongly in a particular community's language, which an expert from another community would not have guessed.

Learning rules from data requires the same discipline as learning any model: split data honestly, learn rules on training data only, test them on fresh test data, and check them for overfitting, because a rule learner, like any learner, can memorise training quirks as rules that look plausible but do not generalise, especially if it searches many combinations and picks those that happen to fit training data by chance, a form of multiple-comparison overfitting that is easy to overlook when rules look meaningful after the fact. That overfitting is diagnosed by the usual gap: rules perform well on training data but poorly on test data, or rules that seemed meaningful on training data fail on new examples, and it is mitigated by requiring rules to have sufficient support, meaning they cover enough training examples to be reliable, and by pruning rules that do not improve validation performance, as Chapter 4 discussed for trees generally. Support thresholds, such as requiring a rule to cover at least ten training examples, are simple but effective guards against chance rules, and they should be set based on dataset size and job stakes, with higher thresholds for smaller datasets or higher-stakes jobs, where chance rules are more dangerous.

Learned rules also need human review before deployment, because data-driven rules can encode bias, as Chapter 4 warned for learned models generally: a rule that predicts a label from a dialect word, learned because training data associated that dialect with that label due to sampling bias, is not a genuine pattern but a bias laundered into a rule that looks objective because it was learned from data.

To practise learning rules, take your training messages and search, by hand or with a small program, for two-word combinations that predict labels well, such as a question word plus a question mark, or an action word plus a time word, counting their support and confidence on training data, then test those learned rules on test data, comparing their performance with hand-written rules from Chapter 1.

Section 32: Keeping Models Simple on Purpose

Keeping models simple on purpose, sometimes called simplicity as a design principle, means choosing the simplest model that meets a job's requirements, and resisting pressure to add complexity, features, or parameters that do not clearly improve honest test performance or user outcomes. That principle is not anti-progress; it is pro-reliability, because simple models are easier to understand, test, debug, maintain, explain, and govern, as this chapter has argued repeatedly, and those properties compound over a model's lifetime, outweighing small accuracy gains from complexity that may not persist on new data, may introduce fairness or privacy risks, and may make maintenance harder for future builders who must understand a complex system they did not create. Simplicity as a principle should therefore be stated explicitly in project goals, such as "prefer the simplest model that achieves at least ninety percent of the best model's test performance, considering fairness and interpretability", so that complexity must justify itself against a clear simplicity default, rather than being adopted by default because it sounds advanced, which is a common failure mode in AI projects where teams chase state-of-the-art accuracy without considering whether that accuracy is needed for the job, or whether it comes at unacceptable costs in transparency, speed, or maintainability.

Practising simplicity on purpose involves several habits: start with a baseline simple model, such as a majority classifier or a single rule, and record its performance, so complexity can be judged against a floor, not against zero; add complexity incrementally, one feature, rule, or parameter at a time, testing each addition on validation data, and keeping only additions that clearly help; document why each complexity was added, with test evidence, so future builders can judge whether it is still needed, or whether it can be removed as data or jobs change; and periodically simplify, by reviewing models for components that no longer help, such as rules that never fire, features that no longer predict, or parameters that can be removed without worsening test performance, a process called simplification or pruning, applied to whole models, not just to trees.

Simplicity also helps with communication: a simple model can be explained to users, managers, and regulators in a few sentences, with a table or a tree diagram, while a complex model may require pages of technical explanation that non-specialists cannot follow, leading to distrust, misunderstanding, or rubber-stamp approval without genuine review, all of which undermine governance.

Finally, simplicity on purpose is a learning strategy for you as a student: by building simple models first, thoroughly, with evaluation and reflection, you learn principles deeply, in forms you can trace and debate, which prepares you to understand complex models later, when you meet them, because you will recognise their components, such as scoring, weighting, and optimisation, as elaborations of simple ideas you already master, rather than as mysterious new techniques.

Section 33: Debugging Rule Systems

Debugging a rule system means finding why it made a wrong decision, which, for rules, is usually traceable to a specific rule, a specific ordering, or a specific condition, unlike learned models where faults may be distributed across parameters. The debugging workflow starts with reproducing the mistake on a single input, then tracing which rule fired, or which rules were considered and rejected, and why, by printing the decision path, including conditions checked, their true or false values, and the order in which they were tried. That trace, like a debugger's stack trace for code, reveals the fault's location: a rule that fired when it should not have, because its condition was too broad; a rule that did not fire when it should have, because its condition was too narrow or because an earlier rule shadowed it; or no rule firing, because the fallback is wrong for this case, or because a needed rule is missing entirely. Each diagnosis suggests a different fix: narrow a broad condition, broaden a narrow condition, reorder shadowed rules, or add a missing rule, and each fix should be tested on the original mistake plus the full test set, to ensure it fixes the case without breaking others, which is regression testing for rules, as Chapter 24 discussed for maintenance generally.

Debugging rule systems also involves checking for interactions, where two rules, each correct in isolation, combine badly, as Section 8 discussed for conflicts. Interaction bugs are diagnosed by constructing test inputs that trigger multiple rules and checking outcomes against intention, and by reviewing rule order and priorities for those inputs, asking whether the firing rule's priority truly outranks the other triggered rules, given the job's values and policies. If priorities are wrong, reordering or adding a more specific rule for the interaction case may fix it, but reordering must be tested broadly, because changing order affects all inputs, not just the interaction case, and a reorder that fixes one conflict may create another elsewhere, which only full test set retesting reveals. That broad retesting after any order change is essential, because rule order is global, not local, and local fixes to order can have global consequences that are invisible without comprehensive tests, which is why rule systems need test sets that cover interactions, not just single-rule cases, and why test sets should grow as new interactions are discovered, becoming a record of the system's interaction history, not just its individual rule coverage.

Another debugging tool for rule systems is rule coverage analysis, from Section 19, which shows which rules fire, how often, and which never fire, on test and on real data, revealing dead rules that can be removed, overused rules that may be too broad, and underused rules that may be too narrow or shadowed.

Finally, debugging rule systems requires patience with edge cases, because rules are often written for typical cases and fail on edges, such as empty inputs, extreme values, unusual spellings, or inputs that combine features in novel ways that no rule anticipated.

Section 34: Simple Models and Fairness

Simple models and fairness interact in both directions: simple models can be fairer, because their biases are visible and fixable, as Section 7 argued, but they can also be unfair, because their simplicity may hide or encode bias in ways that are easy to overlook if fairness is not checked explicitly. A rule that uses a seemingly neutral feature, such as postcode, may systematically disadvantage a group that lives in certain postcodes, due to historical segregation, even though postcode itself is not a protected attribute, and that disadvantage will be invisible if fairness is judged only by whether protected attributes are used directly, rather than by outcomes by group. A scoring rule that weights a feature highly, such as formal language use, may disadvantage speakers of informal dialects, even though formal language seems like a neutral quality indicator, because formality correlates with education and background, not just with message quality. Those indirect biases, through proxies and correlated features, affect simple models as much as complex ones, and they require the same fairness checks as complex models: break down performance, predictions, and outcomes by group, look for disparities, investigate their causes in rules, weights, and data, and fix them, even if fixing lowers overall accuracy slightly, because fairness is part of quality, not a separate trade-off to be sacrificed for accuracy, as earlier chapters have argued consistently.

Fairness checking for simple models is actually easier than for complex models, because rules and weights can be inspected directly for proxies: read each rule's condition and ask whether it correlates with group membership, read each weight and ask whether it penalises a group's typical language or behaviour, and read each threshold and ask whether it falls in a range where one group's values cluster, causing disproportionate outcomes for that group, as Section 27 discussed for threshold bias. That direct inspection, combined with group breakdowns of outcomes, catches many biases that would require statistical probing in complex models, where proxies are hidden in parameters. It also enables direct fixes: remove a biased rule, adjust a biased weight, soften a biased threshold with a review band, or broaden training data for a disadvantaged group, each fix traceable to a specific component, with test results before and after, showing whether fairness improved without unduly harming overall performance.

Fairness for simple models also involves participation: involving affected groups in rule design, weight setting, and threshold choice, because those groups understand their own language, needs, and risks best, and can spot biases that builders from other groups miss, such as a rule that treats a dialect feature as an error, or a weight that penalises a cultural communication style, or a threshold that assumes a schedule that does not fit their lives.

Finally, fairness for simple models requires ongoing monitoring, because fairness, like accuracy, drifts as data, language, and user populations change, and a model that was fair at launch may become unfair as a new group adopts it, or as an existing group's language evolves, or as a threshold that was fair for one distribution becomes unfair for a shifted distribution.

Section 35: Simple Models, Summed Up

Simple models, summed up, are the foundation of AI decision-making: rules that state clear criteria, decision lists that order rules by priority, scoring rules that combine evidence transparently, trees that ask questions in sequence, nearest neighbours that copy similar examples, linear models that weight features, lookup tables that memorise exact mappings, and hybrids that combine those forms with learning and human review, each with strengths, limits, and appropriate jobs, which this chapter has explored in detail, with examples, code, and evaluation practices that you can apply immediately to your own tools. That foundation is not a preliminary stage to be rushed through on the way to complex models; it is a complete toolkit for many real jobs, especially personal, small-scale, and regulated jobs, where transparency, maintainability, fairness, and safety matter as much as accuracy, and where simple models often outperform complex ones, once all criteria, not just accuracy, are considered, as Sections 7 and 30 argued with evidence from overfitting, interpretability, and governance, not just from intuition or preference for simplicity for its own sake.

Section 41: Rules for Prioritising Tasks

Rules for prioritising tasks turn a list of tasks into an order for action, using criteria such as due date, importance, effort, and dependencies, which are the factors people naturally weigh when deciding what to do first, and which simple models can formalise without obscuring judgement. A task rule might say: if a task is overdue, it is urgent; if it is due within two days, it is high priority; if it is due within a week, it is medium priority; otherwise, it is low priority, unless it is flagged important, in which case it rises one level. Those rules, combined into a decision list or a scoring rule, produce a priority order that can be explained task by task: this task is high priority because it is due tomorrow and flagged important, that task is low priority because it is due next month and unflagged, and so on, with explanations that users can check against their own judgement, disagreeing where rules miss context, such as a task that is due soon but actually unimportant, or a task that is due later but blocks other work, which dependency rules should catch, by raising tasks that block others, even if their own due dates are distant, because blocked work cannot proceed until blockers are done, a principle that applies to projects as well as to personal lists.

Task prioritisation rules need care with effort and energy, because priority alone does not determine what to do next: a high-priority task that takes four hours may not be the best next action when you have only thirty minutes, while a medium-priority task that takes ten minutes might be, and a good task helper should therefore consider effort alongside priority, suggesting quick wins when time is short and deep work when time is ample, with rules such as: if available time is under thirty minutes, suggest the highest-priority task that fits within that time; otherwise, suggest the highest-priority task overall, unless a dependency blocks it, in which case suggest the blocker.

Task rules also need care with human override, because priorities change with circumstances that rules cannot see, such as a sudden personal emergency, a new opportunity, or a change of mind about a project's importance, and a task helper that rigidly enforces its priorities, without allowing reordering, snoozing, or re-flagging, will frustrate users and be abandoned, however accurate its rules are, because users need agency over their own work, as earlier sections argued for agents generally.

Section 42: Rules for Searching Your Notes

Rules for searching your notes determine how a search tool matches a query to notes, ranks matches, and presents results, and they are a practical application of simple models to a job every learner needs: finding information you have already written, quickly, without re-reading everything. A basic search rule might say: if a note contains all query words, rank it highest; if it contains some query words, rank it by how many it contains; if it contains none, exclude it, unless it contains a synonym you have defined, in which case include it with a lower score. Those rules, combined with scoring for word frequency, title matches, and recency, produce a ranked list that can be explained result by result: this note ranked first because it contains all query words, including in its title, and was written recently, while that note ranked lower because it contains only one query word, in its body, and is older, with explanations that help you judge relevance yourself, rather than trusting a ranking blindly, which is important for search, because relevance is subjective, and a ranking that seems wrong to you should be diagnosable, by checking which rules and scores produced it, and correctable, by adjusting weights or adding synonyms, rather than being an opaque list you must accept or abandon.

Search rules need care with synonyms and related words, because your notes may use different words for the same idea, such as "task" versus "to-do", or "summary" versus "overview", and a search that matches only exact words will miss notes that are relevant but worded differently, which is a recall problem, as Chapter 4 discussed for evaluation generally, where recall measures how many relevant items are found, and low recall means useful notes are missed, even if found notes are highly relevant, which is precision.

Search rules also need care with common words, because words like "the", "and", or "is" appear in almost every note, and matching them adds no information, while scoring them equally with rare, informative words would dilute rankings, making notes that match only common words appear as relevant as notes that match rare, topic-specific words, which they are not.

Section 43: Rules for Summarising Notes

Rules for summarising notes determine which sentences from a note are selected for a summary, how many are selected, and how they are ordered, and they are a practical application of scoring and rules to a job that saves time: reading a short summary instead of a long note, when you need a reminder, not a full re-read. A basic summarisation rule might say: score each sentence by keyword presence, position, and length, select the three highest-scoring sentences, order them as they appear in the original note, and label the result as a machine summary, to be checked against the original.

Summarisation rules need care with redundancy, because a note may repeat a point in several sentences, and selecting all of them would waste summary space on repetition, while missing other points, so a good summariser should penalise sentences that are very similar to already selected sentences, using a simple similarity check, such as word overlap, to ensure diversity: after selecting a sentence, lower the scores of sentences that share most of its words, so the next selection is likely to cover a different point, rather than restating the first, which is a diversity rule, sometimes called maximal marginal relevance, that balances relevance, selecting high-scoring sentences, with novelty, selecting sentences that add new information, a balance that is essential for useful summaries, because a summary that repeats one point three times, however important that point is, is less useful than a summary that covers three different points, even if each is slightly less important individually, which is a judgement about summary quality that scoring alone, without diversity, cannot capture, and which rules must therefore encode explicitly, as a step in selection, not as an afterthought.

Summarisation rules also need care with length and fairness, because summaries should be proportional to note length, not fixed at three sentences for both a ten-sentence note and a hundred-sentence note, where three sentences would be too much for the first and too little for the second, so a proportional rule, such as selecting up to twenty percent of sentences, with a minimum of one and a maximum of five, adapts summary length to note length, while keeping summaries short enough to be useful, which is their purpose.
