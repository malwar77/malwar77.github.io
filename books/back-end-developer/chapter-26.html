Chapter 26: Email, Notifications and Background Jobs

Section 1: Why Servers Send Email

Almost every web application you use sends email, even though email feels like an old technology. When you sign up for a new account, a welcome message arrives. When you forget a password, a reset link arrives. When you order something, a receipt arrives. All of that mail comes from a back-end server, not from a person sitting at a keyboard. The server sends it automatically, in response to something a user did, or something that happened in the system. Email is useful because it reaches people even when they are not looking at your application. A user might close your website and never return, but an email can still reach their inbox and remind them that something needs their attention. That is why email remains one of the most important tools a back-end developer has.

Think about a simple example. A user signs up for a task-tracking application. The moment the account is created, the server sends a welcome email that explains how to get started. A week later, the user has not logged in again, so the server sends a gentle reminder. When the user creates a task with a deadline, the server sends a reminder the day before it is due. None of this requires a human to remember anything. The server follows rules that the developer wrote, and it follows them every single time, day or night. That reliability is the point. People forget, get busy, or go on holiday. Servers do not.

Email also creates a record. A receipt email proves that a payment happened. A confirmation email proves that an order was placed. A notification email proves that a message was sent, even if the user never opened it. For businesses, that record matters. It settles arguments, helps support teams, and gives users confidence that the system worked. When something goes wrong, the first question is often, "Did you get the email?" That question only makes sense because email is a normal, expected part of how applications communicate.

There is another reason email matters so much: it works across every platform. Your application might be a website, a phone app, or both. Email works with all of them. A user does not need your app installed to read an email. They only need an inbox, and almost everyone has one. That makes email the most universal way to reach a user. Push notifications need an app and permission. Text messages cost money per message. Email is cheap, universal, and familiar, which is why it is usually the first notification channel a developer builds.

Sending email also teaches you something bigger about back-end work. A server is not just a machine that answers requests. It is a machine that does things on behalf of people, sometimes long after the request is finished. Email is one of the clearest examples of that. A request comes in, the server answers it, and then, behind the scenes, more work happens. Learning to send email well means learning to think about reliability, timing, and what happens when things fail. Those lessons apply to almost every other part of back-end development too.

# A welcome email is just data until a mail server sends it.
welcome = {
    "to": "ada@example.com",
    "subject": "Welcome to TaskDay",
    "body": "Hello Ada, your account is ready. Start with your first task.",
}
print(welcome["subject"])

Section 2: What Happens When a Server Sends Mail

When a user clicks a button and an email arrives a few seconds later, a whole chain of machines has done work in between. Understanding that chain helps you fix problems when email does not arrive, because you can work out which link in the chain broke. It starts with your application. Your code builds a message: who it is to, who it is from, a subject, and a body. Then your code hands that message to a mail server. Your application usually does not deliver the message itself. It asks a specialised mail server to do the delivery, because delivering mail reliably is a hard job on its own.

That mail server is called an SMTP server, and we will look at SMTP properly in the next section. For now, think of it as a post office. Your application drops a letter at the post office, and the post office works out how to get it to the destination. The destination is the recipient's mail provider, such as Gmail or Outlook. The sending server looks up where that provider lives on the internet, connects to it, and hands over the message. The receiving provider then stores the message in the recipient's mailbox, where it waits until the user opens their email app. The whole journey usually takes seconds, but it involves several independent systems, each of which can fail.

One important detail is that email delivery is not instant or guaranteed in the way a web response is. When your server answers a web request, it knows straight away whether it worked. Email is different. Your application hands the message to a mail server and gets a quick answer saying the message was accepted, not that it was delivered. Delivery happens later, and it can fail for many reasons: the address might not exist, the recipient's mailbox might be full, or the receiving provider might decide the message looks like spam and reject it. This is why good email code does not assume success just because the message was accepted. It records what happened and handles failures.

Another detail is that the "from" address on an email is not proof of who sent it. Anyone can write any name on an envelope, and email works in a similar way. That is why receiving providers check extra signals, such as whether the sending server is authorised to send for that domain. We will cover those checks later in this chapter. For now, remember that sending email involves trust, and trust has to be earned with proper setup, not just claimed in code.

It also helps to know that reading email and sending email are different jobs. Sending uses SMTP. Reading uses different protocols, and your application rarely needs to read a user's inbox at all. Most back-end work only sends mail. That keeps things simpler. Your code builds a message, hands it to a mail server, and moves on. If you understand that handoff, and you understand that delivery can fail later, you already understand the most important ideas about email in back-end development.

# The journey of one message, as simple steps.
journey = ["app builds message", "mail server accepts it",
           "receiving provider stores it", "user reads it"]
for step in journey:
    print(step)

Section 3: SMTP in Plain Words

SMTP stands for Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, and it is the language mail servers use to talk to each other when sending email. You do not need to memorise its commands, but you should understand what it does, because every email your application sends will pass through it. Think of SMTP as the rules of the post office: how a letter is addressed, how it is handed over, and what answers mean. Your code speaks SMTP to a mail server, and that mail server speaks SMTP to the recipient's provider. It is a simple, text-based conversation, and it has worked in roughly the same way for decades.

A typical SMTP conversation goes like this. Your code connects to a mail server and says hello. It says who the message is from, then who it is to. Then it sends the message itself: the subject, the body, and some extra headers. The server answers each step with a short code. A code starting with 2 means success, a code starting with 4 means a temporary problem, and a code starting with 5 means a permanent problem, such as an address that does not exist. Those codes matter, because they tell your code whether to try again later or give up. A temporary failure might fix itself. A permanent failure will not, and retrying forever would just waste effort.

You will also meet two numbers again and again: ports 25, 587, and 465. A port is like a door into a server, and different doors are used for different kinds of mail traffic. Port 25 is the traditional door servers use to talk to each other. Port 587 is the door applications normally use to submit mail, usually with a login and encryption. Port 465 is an older door that is also used with encryption. In practice, when your application sends mail, it will almost always use port 587 with encryption and a username and password. That combination keeps the login safe and proves your application is allowed to send.

Encryption matters here. Without it, your login details and your message travel across the internet in plain text, readable by anyone in the middle. Modern mail servers therefore require a secure connection before they accept a login. Python's standard library handles this for you, and we will see how in the next section. The lesson is simple: never send mail, or a password, over an unencrypted connection. If a mail service offers you a plain, unencrypted option, treat that as a warning sign, not a convenience.

SMTP is also the reason email can be slow or unreliable. Because it is a store-and-forward system, each server can hold a message and retry delivery later if the next server is busy. That is good for reliability, but it means delivery time varies. A message might arrive in seconds or in minutes. Your application should never promise a user that an email has arrived just because it was sent. It should say the email was sent, and let the user check their inbox. Small wording choices like that prevent a lot of support messages.

# SMTP reply codes: the first digit tells you the story.
codes = {250: "accepted", 450: "temporary problem, try later",
         550: "permanent problem, do not retry"}
for code, meaning in codes.items():
    print(code, "-", meaning)

Section 4: Sending Email with Python's smtplib

Python's standard library includes a module called smtplib, which lets your code speak SMTP to a mail server without installing anything extra. That makes it a perfect learning tool. You will not use a bare smtplib script for a large production system, but the steps it teaches are exactly the steps every larger email system follows: connect securely, log in, build a message, send it, and close the connection. Once you understand these steps, using a bigger email service later will feel familiar, because the ideas are the same and only the details change.

The first step is connecting. You create an SMTP object with the server's address and port 587, then you start encryption with a method called starttls. The name stands for Transport Layer Security, and it turns an ordinary connection into an encrypted one before any login details are sent. Only after encryption do you log in with a username and password. Those credentials belong to your mail account or your mail service, and they must never be written directly into your code. Store them in environment variables instead, which you will learn more about in a later chapter. Hard-coding a password in source code is one of the easiest ways to leak it, because code gets copied, shared, and stored in places you forget about.

The second step is building the message. Python has another standard library module, email.message, which gives you an EmailMessage object. You set its subject, its from address, its to address, and its content. Using EmailMessage instead of building raw text yourself is important, because email has strict formatting rules. A missing blank line or a badly formed header can make a message unreadable or suspicious to spam filters. The library handles those details for you, so your job is to provide good content, not to memorise the format.

The third step is sending. You call send_message with your message object, and the server either accepts it or raises an error. Wrap this in error handling, because networks fail. If sending fails, your code should log the failure and, depending on the situation, retry later or tell the user. Finally, close the connection. Forgetting to close connections is a small mistake that adds up: every open connection uses resources on the server, and a busy application can run out of them. A tidy habit is to use a context manager, which closes the connection automatically even if an error happens.

Here is a complete, small example. It reads credentials from environment variables, builds a message, and sends it. In real life you would run this against a real mail service, but the shape of the code is the same everywhere.

import os
import smtplib
from email.message import EmailMessage

msg = EmailMessage()
msg["Subject"] = "Welcome to TaskDay"
msg["From"] = "hello@taskday.example"
msg["To"] = "ada@example.com"
msg.set_content("Hello Ada, your account is ready.")

with smtplib.SMTP("smtp.example.com", 587) as server:
    server.starttls()
    server.login(os.environ["MAIL_USER"], os.environ["MAIL_PASS"])
    server.send_message(msg)

Keeping credentials out of code also makes it easy to rotate a password later without touching or redeploying the application itself.

Section 5: Plain Text Versus HTML Email

Every email has a body, but that body can come in two forms: plain text and HTML. Plain text is exactly what it sounds like: ordinary words, no colours, no fonts, no images, no layout. HTML email uses the same language as web pages, so it can include headings, colours, buttons, and images. Most emails you receive from companies are HTML, because they look nicer and can carry branding. But plain text still matters, and a good back-end developer understands when to use each, and why sending both is often the best choice.

Plain text has real strengths. It is small, it loads fast, and it works everywhere, including old email apps and screen readers used by people with visual impairments. It is also much harder to hide something nasty inside plain text. There are no hidden images that report back when you open the message, and no links disguised as buttons. For that reason, many security-conscious people prefer plain text, and some email apps display it by default. If your message is simple, such as a password reset link or a short notification, plain text is often all you need, and it is the honest, lightweight choice.

HTML email has strengths too. A welcome email with your logo, a clear heading, and a big button is easier to scan than a wall of text. A receipt with a tidy table of items is easier to read than the same information in plain lines. HTML lets you guide the reader's eye to the one action you want them to take. But HTML email is harder to build well. Different email apps display HTML differently, so a design that looks perfect in one app can look broken in another. Images may be blocked by default, so any information that only exists inside an image might never be seen. That is a common beginner mistake: putting the important message in a picture instead of in real text.

The safest approach, and the one most serious applications use, is to send both versions in a single email. The email contains a plain text part and an HTML part, and the reader's app picks the one it can display. Python's EmailMessage supports this with a simple method: you set the plain text content first, then add an HTML alternative. That way, everyone gets a readable message, no matter what app they use. It is a little more work, but it is the professional choice, and it shows respect for your readers' different tools and needs.

One more rule matters for both forms: never put sensitive information in an email that you would not want seen by someone else. Email is not a private channel. Messages can be forwarded, stored for years, or read on a shared screen. A password reset link is acceptable because it expires and only resets access. An actual password, sent by email, is not acceptable. Keep emails useful, short, and free of secrets, and your users will trust them more.

from email.message import EmailMessage

msg = EmailMessage()
msg["Subject"] = "Your weekly summary"
msg["From"] = "hello@taskday.example"
msg["To"] = "ada@example.com"
msg.set_content("You completed 5 tasks this week. Well done!")
msg.add_alternative(
    "<h1>Weekly summary</h1><p>You completed <b>5 tasks</b> this week.</p>",
    subtype="html")

Section 6: Welcome Emails and Confirmations

A welcome email is often the first message a user ever receives from your application, so it sets the tone for everything that follows. A good welcome email does three things: it confirms that the account was created, it tells the user what to do first, and it gives them a way to get help. That is all. It does not need to sell ten features, list every page on your site, or include a long essay about your company. Users skim email quickly, so the best welcome emails are short, clear, and focused on one next step. If you try to say everything, the user will remember nothing.

Confirmation emails are a close cousin of welcome emails, but they have a different job. A confirmation email asks the user to prove they own the email address they signed up with, usually by clicking a link. This matters because a mistyped address means your messages go to a stranger, and a fake address means someone can create accounts they never have to answer for. Confirmation also protects you: if you only send important mail to confirmed addresses, fewer of your messages bounce, and mail providers are more likely to trust you. A confirmed address is a small but real signal that a user is genuine.

Building a confirmation link safely takes care. The link should contain a random token, a long string of characters that is impossible to guess, stored in your database alongside the user and an expiry time. When the user clicks the link, your server looks up the token, checks it has not expired, and marks the address as confirmed. The token must expire, because a link that works forever is a link that can be misused forever. An hour or a day is a sensible lifetime. Never use something guessable as a token, such as the user's id or their email address itself, because anyone could construct that link and confirm an address they do not own.

There is also a question of timing. Should a user be blocked from using your application until they confirm? For some applications, yes, especially where security matters. For others, it is friendlier to let them start using the app straight away and remind them to confirm later. There is no single right answer, but there is a wrong one: silently doing nothing. If confirmation matters, say so clearly, and make it easy to request a new confirmation email. A user who cannot find the first email should not be stuck forever. A simple "resend confirmation" action, with sensible limits so it cannot be abused, solves that problem.

Finally, test your welcome and confirmation emails the way a real user would receive them. Send them to your own inbox, open them on a phone, and click every link. Check that the links work, that the wording makes sense, and that nothing looks broken. First impressions are hard to repair, and an email full of broken links or placeholder text tells a new user that your application is not ready. A short, working welcome email tells them the opposite.

import secrets
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

# A safe confirmation token: random, stored, and expiring.
token = secrets.token_urlsafe(32)
confirmation = {"user": "ada@example.com", "token": token,
                "expires": datetime.now() + timedelta(hours=24)}
print("Send link: https://taskday.example/confirm?token=" + token)

Section 7: Password Reset Emails Done Safely

Password reset emails are one of the most sensitive emails an application sends, because they hand someone a way back into an account. If they are done badly, they become a way for an attacker to take over accounts. If they are done well, they are a quiet, boring feature that users barely notice, which is exactly what you want. The rules for safe password resets are simple, but every one of them matters, and skipping one creates a real weakness.

The first rule is about what the email contains. It should contain a link with a random, single-use token that expires quickly, usually within an hour. It should never contain the user's current password, and it should never contain a new password. A password sent by email can be read by anyone who sees that inbox, and inboxes are often open on shared devices or forwarded to other addresses. The token approach is safer because the token is useless after it is used or after it expires, while a password stays useful until someone changes it. When the user clicks the link, they choose their own new password on your website, over a secure connection.

The second rule is about what your application says when someone asks for a reset. Suppose an attacker types in someone else's email address and requests a reset. If your application says "no account found with that address", the attacker has just learned that the address is not registered, and they can try another. If it says "reset email sent", they learn the address is registered. Either answer leaks information. The safe response is to always show the same message, such as "If an account exists for that address, a reset email has been sent." That way, an attacker learns nothing, and a genuine user still gets their email when their account exists.

The third rule is about limits. A reset form with no limits can be abused to flood someone's inbox with reset emails, which is annoying and can look like an attack. Limit how often a reset can be requested for the same address, for example once every few minutes, and limit how many tokens can be active at once. When a new token is created, older ones for the same user should stop working. That way there is only ever one live reset link per user, which is easier to reason about and harder to misuse.

The fourth rule is about what happens after a reset. Once a password is changed, the user should be told, by email, that their password changed, and when. If they did not make that change, that email is their warning to act quickly. It is also good practice to end other sessions when a password changes, so that someone who was already logged in as that user gets logged out. These small steps turn a password reset from a weak point into a feature that actively protects accounts.

import secrets
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

# One live token per user: creating a new one replaces the old one.
reset_tokens = {}

def request_reset(email):
    token = secrets.token_urlsafe(32)
    reset_tokens[email] = {"token": token,
                           "expires": datetime.now() + timedelta(hours=1)}
    return token

print(request_reset("ada@example.com")[:8] + "...")

Section 8: Email Templates Without Fancy Tools

When an application sends many different emails, writing each message by hand inside code quickly becomes messy. The text gets mixed up with the sending logic, small changes require editing code, and the same greeting gets written five different ways in five different places. Templates solve this. A template is a message with placeholders, such as a name or a link, that your code fills in for each recipient. You do not need a fancy template library to understand the idea. Python's standard library can do the basics, and the basics are enough to learn the pattern well.

The simplest approach is a plain string with named placeholders, filled in with Python's string formatting. You write the message once, with spots like {name} and {link}, and then you supply the real values when you send. This keeps the message text in one place and the sending logic in another, which makes both easier to read and change. If you need to fix a spelling mistake, you change one template, not five copies of the same message scattered through your code. That single benefit alone is worth adopting templates early.

A slightly better approach is to store templates as separate files, or at least as named constants near the top of a module, rather than buried inside functions. When templates live in their own place, a non-programmer can read them, and a programmer can find them quickly. You can also keep a plain text version and an HTML version of the same message side by side, which makes it easy to keep them consistent. The key habit is treating message text as content that changes often, and keeping it separate from logic that changes rarely. That separation is a theme you will meet again and again in back-end work.

Templates also need care with user data. A placeholder filled with a user's name seems harmless, but what if the name contains characters that break your HTML version? A name like "<b>Ada</b>" would turn bold in an HTML email if you insert it raw. The fix is to escape user data when it goes into HTML, so that special characters are shown as text rather than treated as code. Python's html module has an escape function for exactly this. In plain text, this is less of a problem, but in HTML it matters. The general rule is simple: never trust data that came from a user to be safe for the place you are putting it. Escape it for that place.

Finally, keep templates short and honest. A template that says "Your order has shipped" must only be used when the order really has shipped. It sounds obvious, but when templates are reused carelessly, users get messages that do not match reality, and trust drops fast. Name your templates clearly, use each one for exactly one situation, and test each one with real data before it goes live. A template system is only as good as the messages it produces.

import html

template = "Hello {name}, your task '{task}' is due tomorrow: {link}"
message = template.format(name="Ada", task="Finish report",
                          link="https://taskday.example/tasks/12")
print(message)
print(html.escape("<b>Ada</b>"))  # safe to insert into HTML

Section 9: Sending to Many People Safely

Sooner or later, an application needs to send the same message to many people: a weekly summary, a product update, or a notice about a change. This is called bulk sending, and it is where beginners most often cause trouble, not because they mean to, but because sending to a thousand people is not the same as sending to one person a thousand times. The differences are about privacy, deliverability, and respect for the recipients' inboxes, and getting them wrong can damage your application's reputation with mail providers for a long time.

The first rule is privacy. Never put all recipients in the "to" or "cc" fields of a single message, because then every recipient can see every other recipient's address. That leaks personal data and looks unprofessional. The correct approach is to send a separate message to each person, or to use a mail service's bulk feature, which handles the separation for you. Each person should see only their own address. This is not just politeness; in many places it is a legal expectation that personal data is not shared with strangers without reason.

The second rule is pace. Mail providers watch how fast mail arrives from a sender. A brand new sender that suddenly blasts out ten thousand messages looks exactly like a spammer, and providers will start blocking or filtering that mail. The safe approach is to warm up gradually: start with small numbers, watch for bounces and complaints, and increase slowly. Even for an established sender, sending in steady batches is better than sending everything in one burst. A queue, which you will learn about later in this chapter, is the natural tool for this, because it lets you control the rate.

The third rule is permission. Bulk mail should only go to people who asked for it, or who have a genuine relationship with your application that makes the message expected. A weekly summary for active users is expected. A marketing blast to addresses collected from somewhere else is not. Every bulk message should also include a clear way to stop receiving it, which we will cover in a later section. Permission is not just a legal checkbox; it is what keeps your messages out of the spam folder, because people who asked for mail are far less likely to mark it as spam.

The fourth rule is measurement. When you send in bulk, watch the numbers: how many messages bounced, how many were marked as spam, and how many were opened. A sudden rise in bounces means your address list is stale. A rise in spam complaints means people did not expect your mail. Both are warnings to fix before the problem grows. A small sender that ignores these signals can lose the ability to reach inboxes at all, and recovering that trust takes much longer than losing it.

# One message per person: nobody sees anyone else's address.
recipients = ["ada@example.com", "ben@example.com", "chi@example.com"]
for person in recipients:
    print("Sending weekly summary to", person)

Section 10: Bounces: When Email Does Not Arrive

Not every email arrives. Some bounce, which means the receiving server refuses the message and sends it back with a reason. Understanding bounces matters because a bouncing address is information: it tells you something about your data, your setup, or your reputation. Ignoring bounces means you keep sending to addresses that will never receive anything, which wastes effort and slowly damages your standing with mail providers. Handling bounces well is a quiet mark of a professional back-end system.

There are two kinds of bounce, and the difference matters. A hard bounce is permanent: the address does not exist, or the domain does not exist. No amount of retrying will fix it, and the correct response is to stop sending to that address and mark it as invalid in your database. A soft bounce is temporary: the recipient's mailbox is full, or their server is busy, or your message was too large. Soft bounces deserve a retry, because the problem may clear up on its own. A good system retries soft bounces a few times over a day or two, and only gives up after repeated failure. Treating every bounce the same, either by retrying everything forever or by deleting every address at the first failure, is a mistake.

Bounces also come back to you in a specific way. When a message bounces, the receiving server sends a bounce message to the return address of the original mail, which is usually an envelope address your mail service manages, not the friendly "from" address the user sees. Larger mail services collect these bounces and report them through dashboards or webhooks, so your application can learn about them automatically. For a small application, you might simply watch a mailbox or a service dashboard. The mechanism varies, but the habit is the same: check bounces regularly, and act on them.

What action should you take? For a hard bounce on a new signup, the account may have been created with a mistyped address. Prompt the user to check and correct it, rather than silently keeping a useless address. For a hard bounce on an old address, mark it invalid and stop sending marketing mail to it, but be careful about important mail: if a user's address starts bouncing, they may have lost access to that inbox, and they will need another way to recover their account. For repeated soft bounces, pause sending to that address for a while and try again later. These are judgement calls, but they all follow one principle: a bouncing address is a signal, not just an error to ignore.

Finally, keep your address list clean as a habit, not as a rescue mission. Regularly remove hard-bounced addresses, confirm addresses at signup, and give users an easy way to update their address. A clean list sends fewer wasted messages, gets better delivery, and keeps your reputation with mail providers healthy. Bounce handling is not glamorous work, but it is exactly the kind of unglamorous work that separates a system that keeps working from one that slowly stops reaching people.

# Decide what to do with a bounce based on its kind.
def handle_bounce(address, kind):
    if kind == "hard":
        return f"{address}: mark invalid, stop sending"
    return f"{address}: retry later, problem may be temporary"

print(handle_bounce("nobody@example.com", "hard"))
print(handle_bounce("ada@example.com", "soft"))

Section 11: Spam Filters and Why Mail Lands in Spam

Every mail provider runs spam filters, which are automated checks that decide whether an incoming message belongs in the inbox, the spam folder, or nowhere at all. A spam filter does not read your message the way a person does. It looks for signals: who sent it, whether that sender is trusted, whether the message looks like known spam, and whether recipients have complained about similar mail before. Each signal adds or removes trust, and the total decides where the message lands. Understanding this helps you because most mail that lands in spam is not blocked by one big mistake. It is pushed there by several small signals adding up.

One strong signal is reputation. Mail providers remember how a sending domain and server have behaved in the past. If most of its mail was wanted and opened, its reputation is good, and new messages are treated kindly. If a lot of its mail bounced, or recipients marked it as spam, its reputation drops, and even good messages start landing in spam. Reputation is slow to build and fast to lose, which is why protecting it matters from the very first message you send. A new domain has no reputation at all, so providers treat it cautiously until it proves itself with small, wanted volumes.

Another signal is content. Certain patterns raise suspicion: ALL CAPS SUBJECT LINES, lots of exclamation marks, misleading subject lines that do not match the body, and messages that are mostly images with very little text. A single exclamation mark will not doom a message, but a message that combines several suspicious patterns looks like the spam the filter was trained to catch. The fix is not to learn tricks that fool filters. The fix is to write honest, plain subject lines that describe the message, keep a healthy balance of text, and avoid shouting. Filters reward mail that looks like it was written by a real person for a real reason.

A third signal is engagement. If recipients open your mail, reply to it, or move it out of spam into the inbox, providers learn that your mail is wanted. If recipients delete it without opening, or mark it as spam, providers learn the opposite. That is why sending only to people who asked for your mail matters so much. An address list full of people who never wanted your messages will train filters against you, even if every message is honest. Permission is not just polite; it is a technical input to the filter.

Finally, technical setup matters. Missing authentication records, which we cover next, make a message look untrustworthy before its content is even considered. A message from a domain with proper authentication, sent at a steady pace to people who asked for it, with an honest subject and real text, has the best chance of reaching the inbox. None of this is a guarantee, because filters change constantly, but these habits stack the odds in your favour. When a user says your mail went to spam, check these signals in order: authentication, reputation, list quality, and content. One of them is usually the cause.

# Signals a spam filter weighs, simplified.
signals = {"good authentication": +2, "recipients asked for mail": +2,
           "many spam complaints": -3, "ALL CAPS SUBJECT": -1}
print(sum(signals.values()), "overall trust score")

Section 12: SPF, DKIM and DMARC in Plain Words

If anyone can write any name on an envelope, how does a mail provider know a message really came from the domain it claims? The answer is a set of three checks with confusing names: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. They sound technical, but each one answers a simple question, and together they are the reason your application's mail can be trusted. You do not need to implement them by hand, but you do need to know what they are, because setting them up is part of launching any application that sends email, and missing them is a common reason mail lands in spam or gets rejected.

SPF, or Sender Policy Framework, answers the question: is this server allowed to send mail for this domain? The domain owner publishes a list, in the domain's DNS records, of the servers that are allowed to send on its behalf. When a message arrives, the receiving provider checks whether the sending server is on that list. If it is not, the message looks forged. Setting up SPF means adding a record that lists your mail service's servers. It is a small DNS change, and your mail provider will usually give you the exact text to add. Without it, your mail starts with a trust problem.

DKIM, or DomainKeys Identified Mail, answers a different question: was this message changed on its way here? When your mail service sends a message, it adds a digital signature to it, created with a private key that only the sender holds. The receiving provider checks that signature using a public key published in the domain's DNS records. If the message was altered in transit, the signature will not match, and the provider knows something is wrong. DKIM therefore proves two things at once: the message came from someone who holds the key, and the message arrived intact. Like SPF, it is set up through DNS records supplied by your mail service.

DMARC ties the first two together and answers the question: what should you do if a message fails these checks? The domain owner publishes a DMARC policy that says whether failing messages should be delivered anyway, sent to spam, or rejected outright. DMARC also asks providers to send reports back to the domain owner, so you can see who is trying to send mail as your domain, including attackers. A sensible path is to start with a gentle policy that only reports failures, watch the reports, fix any legitimate senders you missed, and then tighten the policy step by step. Jumping straight to rejection before you know all your senders can block your own mail.

Together, these three checks form the foundation of trusted email. They are not optional extras for large companies. Even a small application benefits from them, because they are what separate a real sender from an impersonator in the eyes of a mail provider. When you launch an application, add SPF, DKIM, and DMARC to your launch checklist, alongside buying the domain and setting up the server. Your future self, and your users' inboxes, will thank you.

# The three checks, and the question each one answers.
checks = {"SPF": "is this server allowed to send for this domain?",
          "DKIM": "is this message unchanged and signed by the sender?",
          "DMARC": "what should happen if the checks fail?"}
for name, question in checks.items():
    print(name, "-", question)

Section 13: Verifying an Email Address

Before an application trusts an email address, it should verify it. Verification means confirming that the address exists, that it belongs to the person who gave it, and that it can receive mail. Without verification, your database fills up with mistyped addresses, fake addresses, and addresses belonging to strangers who never asked to hear from you. All three cause problems: wasted messages, bounces that hurt your reputation, and confused strangers receiving mail they did not request. Verification is a small amount of work at signup that prevents a large amount of trouble later.

The most reliable verification method is the confirmation email you met earlier in this chapter. The user gives an address, your server sends a message with a unique link, and the address is only marked as verified when that link is clicked. This proves the user can receive mail at that address, which is the strongest signal available. It does not prove the address belongs to them in a legal sense, but in practice, access to an inbox is the standard proof, and it is what almost every application uses. Anything weaker, such as simply asking the user to type the address twice, catches typos but proves nothing about ownership.

There are also weaker checks that are useful as a first line of defence. Format checking, making sure the address looks like an address with an at-sign and a domain, catches obvious mistakes before any mail is sent. Domain checking, making sure the domain part actually exists, catches addresses at domains that were invented on the spot. These checks are cheap and worth doing, but they are not verification. An address can be perfectly formatted and at a real domain and still belong to someone else entirely. Only the confirmation link proves access.

Verification also needs a plan for failure. Some users never click the link. Some lose the email. Some signed up with an address they can no longer access. Your application should handle all of these gracefully. Remind unverified users, but do not nag them every hour. Let them request a new confirmation email, with limits so the feature cannot be abused to flood an inbox. And decide what unverified users can and cannot do. A common, sensible rule is to let them explore but block sensitive actions, such as changing account settings or receiving important notifications, until the address is confirmed. That balance keeps the application welcoming without trusting an unproven address too far.

Finally, re-verify when an address changes. If a user updates their email address, the new address starts out unverified, even if the old one was verified for years. The same confirmation flow runs again, and the old address should stop receiving mail once the new one is confirmed. Skipping this step would let someone change an account's address to one they control and quietly take over notifications. Verification is not a one-time event at signup. It is a rule that follows the address wherever it goes.

import re

def looks_like_email(address):
    pattern = r"^[^@\s]+@[^@\s]+\.[^@\s]+$"
    return re.match(pattern, address) is not None

print(looks_like_email("ada@example.com"))   # True, but still not verified
print(looks_like_email("not-an-address"))    # False

Section 14: Notifications Beyond Email

Email is the oldest notification channel, but it is not the only one, and for many applications it is not even the best one. A notification is simply a message that tells a user something happened or something needs their attention. Email does that, but so do push notifications, text messages, in-app messages, and even messages sent to other systems. A good back-end developer thinks about notifications as a whole system, not as a single feature, and picks the right channel for each kind of message. The channel shapes how fast the message arrives, how likely it is to be seen, and how much it costs.

Push notifications are the messages that appear on a phone's lock screen, even when the app is closed. They are fast and hard to miss, which makes them perfect for urgent, short messages: a reply to your comment, a delivery arriving, a security alert. But they have limits. The user must have your app installed, must have allowed notifications, and can turn them off at any time. They are also easy to abuse. An app that sends too many push notifications gets muted or deleted, so push is a channel that punishes overuse quickly. Use it for things the user genuinely wants to know right now, and nothing else.

Text messages, or SMS, are similar to push in speed, but they work on any phone, with no app required. That universality is their strength, and their weakness is cost: every text costs money, and costs add up fast at scale. SMS is therefore best reserved for high-value, low-volume messages, such as login codes and critical alerts. Sending a daily summary by SMS would be expensive and annoying. Sending a security code by SMS is expected and welcome. The channel should match the value of the message.

In-app notifications live inside your application: a bell icon with a count, a list of recent events, a badge on a menu item. They are cheap, they never leave your system, and the user sees them when they next open the app. They are perfect for messages that are useful but not urgent: someone liked your post, a task was assigned to you, a report is ready. Their weakness is obvious: if the user does not open the app, they never see the message. That is why in-app notifications usually pair with email or push for anything time-sensitive. The in-app list becomes the record, and the other channel becomes the nudge.

The lesson across all channels is that no single channel does everything. Email is universal and cheap but slow to be noticed. Push is fast but requires an app and permission. SMS is universal and fast but expensive. In-app is cheap and rich but invisible until the app opens. A well-designed system uses each channel for what it is good at, lets users choose their preferences, and never sends the same message on every channel at once. That last point matters: a user who gets an email, a push, and a text about the same event does not feel well-informed. They feel spammed.

# Match the message to the channel that fits it best.
messages = {"security code": "SMS", "someone replied": "push",
            "weekly summary": "email", "task assigned": "in-app"}
for message, channel in messages.items():
    print(message, "->", channel)

Section 15: Push Notifications in Plain Words

Push notifications feel like magic: a message appears on a phone that is sitting idle on a table, with no app open. The magic has a simple explanation, and understanding it helps you design push well and avoid its traps. The key idea is that your server never talks directly to the user's phone. Instead, it talks to a platform service run by Apple or Google, and that service delivers the message to the phone. Your server sends a small packet to the platform, the platform finds the device, and the phone wakes up just enough to display the message. That middle step is why push works even when your app is closed, and why you must follow the platform's rules.

To send push, your application needs a device token for each user. A device token is a long, opaque string that identifies one app on one phone. When a user installs your app and allows notifications, the app asks the platform for a token and sends it to your server, which stores it alongside the user. Later, when something happens, your server sends the message and the token to the platform, and the platform routes it to the right phone. Tokens can change, for example when a user reinstalls the app, and they stop working when a user uninstalls it or turns notifications off. Your server must therefore handle dead tokens gracefully, removing them when the platform reports them as invalid, instead of retrying forever.

The message itself is small. A push notification has a title, a short body, and optionally a badge count or a sound. It is not a place for essays. Users glance at it for a second and decide whether to act. A good push message is short, specific, and actionable: "Ada assigned you a task: Finish report, due Friday." A bad one is vague: "You have a new notification." Vague messages teach users to ignore you, and ignored messages are worse than no messages, because they train the user that your app is noise. Every push you send should pass a simple test: would the user be glad to be interrupted for this? If not, use email or in-app instead.

There are also rules you do not control. Platforms limit how many push messages you can send, they may delay low-priority messages to save battery, and they can block senders that abuse the system. Users can mute your app, or turn off a category of notifications, and your server should respect that immediately. This is why notification preferences, covered later in this chapter, are not optional decoration. They are part of the push system itself. A push system that ignores user preferences will lose its audience, one muted phone at a time.

Finally, remember that push is a privilege, not a right. The user gave you permission to interrupt them, and that permission can be withdrawn in seconds. Treat it carefully. Send less than you think you should, make every message worth the interruption, and always offer a quieter alternative. Applications that respect attention keep it. Applications that waste it lose it.

# A push payload is small: title, body, and a badge count.
push = {"title": "New task assigned",
        "body": "Ada assigned you: Finish report, due Friday.",
        "badge": 3}
print(push["title"], "-", push["body"])

Section 16: In-App Notifications and Notification Tables

In-app notifications are the messages a user sees inside your application: the bell icon, the list of recent events, the unread count. They are the simplest notification channel to build, because they need no outside service, no mail server, and no platform permission. They are just data in your database, displayed in your interface. That simplicity makes them a perfect first notification system for a learner, and a useful permanent part of almost every real application, even one that also uses email and push.

The core design is a notifications table in your database. Each row represents one notification for one user, and it holds the facts you need: who the notification is for, what happened, when it happened, whether it has been read, and optionally a link to the thing it is about. When something happens in your system, such as a task being assigned, your code inserts a row into this table. When the user opens the notification list, your code reads their unread rows and displays them. When the user clicks one, your code marks it as read. That is the whole system, and it is a beautiful example of how a database table can become a feature.

A few design choices make this system work well. First, keep the notification text simple and store the facts, not just a finished sentence. If you store "Ada assigned you a task", you cannot later change how it is displayed or translated. If you store the actor, the action, and the target, you can build the sentence at display time and change it freely. Second, think about volume. A popular user could collect thousands of notifications, so you need a plan for old ones: archive them, delete them after a period, or page through them, so the list stays fast. Third, the unread count should be cheap to compute, because it appears on nearly every page. Counting unread rows for one user is fast with the right index, but counting across a huge table without one is slow.

The unread count also teaches a user-experience lesson. A badge that says 99+ feels like a chore, not a delight. If a user has hundreds of unread notifications, the system has failed to help them prioritise. Grouping similar notifications, such as "Ada and 3 others commented", reduces noise without hiding information. And a "mark all as read" button is a kindness: it lets a returning user start fresh instead of clicking through a mountain of old items one by one.

In-app notifications also pair naturally with other channels. A common pattern is to write the notification row first, then decide whether to also send email or push, based on the user's preferences and the urgency. The row becomes the permanent record, and the other channels become the nudge. If the push fails or the email bounces, the notification still exists in the app, waiting for the user. That resilience is a quiet strength of the design: the notification system degrades gracefully instead of losing the message entirely.

import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("""CREATE TABLE notifications
              (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, user TEXT, text TEXT, is_read INTEGER)""")
db.execute("INSERT INTO notifications VALUES (1, 'ben', 'Ada assigned you a task', 0)")
db.execute("INSERT INTO notifications VALUES (2, 'ben', 'Your report is ready', 0)")
unread = db.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM notifications WHERE user='ben' AND is_read=0").fetchone()[0]
print("Unread for ben:", unread)

Section 17: SMS Notifications and Their Costs

SMS, the humble text message, is one of the oldest ways to reach a phone, and it still matters because it works on every phone, needs no app, and is almost always read quickly. When a bank sends a login code by text, or a delivery service says a package is arriving, that is SMS doing what it does best: delivering a short, urgent, valuable message to someone who may not have your app. For a back-end developer, SMS is worth understanding even if you rarely use it, because its strengths and costs teach a general lesson about choosing channels wisely.

The first thing to know is that SMS is not free. Unlike email, which costs almost nothing per message, every text message costs real money, charged by a provider, and the price varies by country. A message to one country might cost a fraction of a cent, while a message to another might cost several cents. At small volumes, that barely matters. At large volumes, it matters enormously. An application with a hundred thousand users sending a daily text would face a bill that could sink a small business. Cost alone forces a discipline that email does not: every SMS must earn its price. If the message is not worth a few cents, it should go by another channel.

The second thing to know is that SMS messages are short. The classic limit is 160 characters per message, and longer messages are split into several, each costing money. That constraint is actually a gift, because it forces clarity. A login code and a short instruction fit easily. A weekly summary does not, and should not be attempted. If you find yourself writing a long SMS, you have chosen the wrong channel. Shorten the message, or move it to email, and use the SMS only to point the user towards it, if a nudge is needed at all.

The third thing to know is that SMS delivery is handled by providers, sometimes called aggregators, who connect your application to the phone networks. Your code sends a request to the provider's service, usually over the web, and the provider delivers the text. That means your application depends on an outside service for a critical feature, and you should handle its failures the way you handle any outside service: with retries for temporary failures, logging, and a fallback plan. If a login code cannot be delivered by SMS, the user needs another way in, such as an email code or a support route, or they are locked out entirely.

Finally, SMS has rules. Many countries require the recipient's consent before marketing texts, and carriers block messages that look like spam. Even for non-marketing messages, you should only send SMS to numbers the user gave you and verified, and you should offer a way to stop. A short, wanted, consented text is welcome. An unexpected marketing text is not, and it can cost you both money and trust. Use SMS sparingly, for messages that are short, urgent, and valuable, and it will remain one of the most reliable channels you have.

# SMS forces discipline: short messages, counted, priced per message.
sms_cost_per_message = 0.02  # dollars, varies by country
messages_sent = 5000
print("Cost for", messages_sent, "texts: $", sms_cost_per_message * messages_sent)

Section 18: Choosing the Right Channel

By now you have met four notification channels: email, push, SMS, and in-app. The question that ties them together is simple to ask and hard to answer well: for this message, for this user, at this moment, which channel is right? Getting this question right is one of the most valuable skills in back-end development, because it shapes how users feel about your application. An application that notifies well feels helpful. An application that notifies badly feels like a nuisance, no matter how good its other features are.

Start with urgency. How quickly does the user need to know? A security alert, such as a login from a new device, needs to arrive fast, so push or SMS fits. A weekly summary does not need speed at all, so email fits. A comment on a post is somewhere in the middle: push if the user is active and wants it, in-app if not. Urgency is the first filter, and it immediately rules out most channels for most messages. If you skip this step and send everything by the fastest channel, you will interrupt people constantly, and they will silence you.

Next, consider value and cost. SMS costs money per message, so it must be reserved for messages worth paying for. Email is nearly free, so it can carry longer, lower-value messages. Push is free but costs attention, which is a different kind of price. In-app is free in both senses, but only reaches users who open the app. Weighing value against cost, in money and in attention, is what keeps a notification system sustainable. A message that is cheap to send but expensive in attention is not actually cheap.

Next, consider the user's context. Are they likely to have your app open? Are they on a phone or a computer? Have they told you their preferences? A user who turned off push notifications has already answered the channel question for you, and ignoring that answer is disrespectful and counterproductive. A user who only ever uses your website will never see a push notification, so email or in-app is the honest choice. Context is not a detail to add later. It is part of the decision.

A practical way to make these decisions consistently is a small set of rules in code. For each kind of event, define a default channel, and let user preferences override it. For example: security events default to push plus email, comments default to in-app plus push if enabled, summaries default to email only. Write these rules down, review them when you add a new event type, and resist the temptation to add "just one more" channel to an existing event without a reason. Every extra channel is extra noise, and noise is what drives users away.

Finally, remember that silence is also a choice. Not every event deserves a notification. Some events are only worth recording in an activity log, or showing the next time the user visits. A system that notifies about everything notifies about nothing, because the user stops paying attention. The most thoughtful notification decision is sometimes to send nothing at all.

# A simple rule table: event type -> default channel.
rules = {"security alert": "push + email", "comment": "in-app (+push if enabled)",
         "weekly summary": "email", "minor update": "in-app only"}
for event, channel in rules.items():
    print(event, "->", channel)

Section 19: Notification Preferences

If notifications are messages your application sends to users, preferences are the users talking back: telling you what they want, how often, and through which channels. Building a preferences system is not a luxury or a nice extra. It is the mechanism that keeps your notification system welcome instead of annoying, and it is also, in many places, a legal expectation. Users who can control their notifications stay subscribed longer, complain less, and trust your application more. Users who cannot control them either mute everything or leave.

A preferences system starts with a simple question: what can the user control? At minimum, they should be able to turn each major category of notification on or off, and choose a channel for it. Categories might include security alerts, comments, task assignments, and summaries. Security alerts are a special case: many applications do not allow these to be turned off entirely, because they protect the account, but they can still offer a choice of channel. For everything else, the user's choice should be respected completely. A preference that your code ignores is worse than no preference at all, because it teaches the user that your controls are decoration.

Preferences also include frequency. Some users want an email for every comment. Others want one daily digest that collects all the day's comments into a single message. Both are reasonable, and a digest option is one of the kindest features you can offer. It turns ten interruptions into one useful summary, and it dramatically reduces the number of messages you send. Building digests takes a little extra work, because your system must collect events during the day and send them together at a chosen time, but that work is exactly the background-job skill you will learn later in this chapter.

Storing preferences is straightforward: a table that links a user, a category, a channel, and whether it is enabled. When your code is about to send a notification, it checks this table first. If the user disabled that category, the notification is not sent, but it may still be recorded in-app, so the user can find it later if they want. That distinction matters: disabling email does not mean the event never happened. It means the user did not want an email about it. Keep the record, respect the channel choice, and everyone stays happy.

Preferences also need a sensible default. A brand new user has not set anything yet, so your defaults decide their first experience. Choose quiet defaults: in-app on, email for important things only, push only for security and direct mentions. A user can always turn more on, but a user overwhelmed in their first week may simply leave. And make the preferences page easy to find, ideally linked from every email you send. A preference page buried three menus deep is a preference page that does not exist in practice.

# Check preferences before sending: the user's choice wins.
preferences = {"ben": {"comments": {"email": False, "push": True},
                       "security": {"email": True, "push": True}}}

def may_send(user, category, channel):
    return preferences[user][category][channel]

print(may_send("ben", "comments", "email"))  # False: do not email
print(may_send("ben", "comments", "push"))   # True: push is allowed

Section 20: Why Some Work Must Not Block a Request

So far, this chapter has talked about sending messages. Now we turn to a deeper idea that explains why email and many other tasks are handled the way they are: some work must not block a request. When a user clicks a button, your server should answer quickly. If it instead spends ten seconds sending emails, generating a report, and resizing images before answering, the user stares at a loading screen, and may click again, or give up. Worse, if one of those slow tasks fails, the whole request can fail, even though the user's main action succeeded. Blocking work turns small problems into big ones.

Think about signing up for an application. The essential work is creating the account and answering the user. The welcome email is important, but it does not need to finish before the user sees the welcome page. If the mail server is slow that day, the user should not be punished with a slow signup. The right design is to answer the request fast, and do the email afterwards, in the background. The user gets a quick response, and the email still arrives a few seconds later. Nobody notices the difference, except that everything feels faster and more reliable.

This pattern has a name: the request should do the minimum work needed to answer, and everything else should be deferred. Deferring does not mean forgetting. It means recording the work somewhere safe, so that another part of the system can pick it up and do it, even if the original request has long finished. That "somewhere safe" is usually a queue, and the "another part" is usually a worker. You will learn both in the next sections. For now, hold onto the principle: a web request is a conversation with a waiting user, and waiting users deserve quick answers.

There is also a reliability argument. A request that does twenty things can fail in twenty ways. A request that does one thing, and queues nineteen others, can only fail in one way at the moment the user is watching. The queued tasks can each fail and retry on their own, without affecting the user or each other. A failing email service does not break signups. A slow report generator does not freeze the dashboard. Separating work by urgency makes the whole system more robust, because a failure in one part stays in that part.

How do you decide what to defer? Ask two questions. First, does the user need the result before they can continue? If yes, do it in the request. If no, defer it. Second, is the work slow or unreliable? Sending email, calling outside services, generating files, and processing images are all slow or unreliable, so they are natural candidates for the background. Fast, local database work usually stays in the request. Apply those two questions to every feature you build, and you will naturally design systems that feel fast even when they are doing a lot of work.

# Fast path: answer the user now, queue the slow work for later.
def signup(username):
    print(f"Account created for {username}")
    print("Queued: welcome email (sent in background)")
    return "Welcome page"

print(signup("ada"))

Section 21: Background Jobs in Plain Words

A background job is a piece of work that your system does outside of a web request, at its own pace, without a user waiting for it. When a user signs up and a welcome email is queued, that email becomes a background job. When a report is generated overnight, that report is a background job. When old data is cleaned up every Sunday, that cleanup is a background job. The idea is simple, but it changes how you think about your application: your system is no longer just a machine that answers requests. It is also a machine that does chores, on a schedule and in the background, whether or not anyone is watching.

Background jobs exist because not all work fits inside a request. Some work is slow, like generating a large file. Some work depends on outside services that might be slow or temporarily down, like sending email. Some work should happen at a particular time, like a daily summary at eight in the morning, long after the request that scheduled it. Trying to force all of this into requests makes your application slow and fragile. Moving it into background jobs makes requests fast and lets each job succeed, fail, and retry on its own terms. The user experience improves, and the system becomes easier to reason about.

Every background job system has three parts, and you will meet each one in detail soon. The first part is the queue, a safe place where jobs wait until they are picked up. The second part is the worker, a process that picks jobs from the queue and runs them, one at a time or several at once. The third part is the job itself, a small, clear description of the work: what to do, and what data it needs. A job might say "send a welcome email to ada@example.com" or "generate the weekly report for team 7". That description must contain everything the worker needs, because by the time the worker runs, the original request is gone, and the worker cannot ask it questions.

One important rule is that jobs should be small and specific. A job that says "do everything for this user" is hard to retry, hard to understand when it fails, and hard to run in parallel with other jobs. A job that says "send this one email" is easy to retry, easy to log, and easy to run alongside a thousand similar jobs. If a large task needs doing, break it into many small jobs. That advice applies far beyond background work, but it is especially true here, because small jobs are the unit of reliability in a background system.

Another rule is that jobs should be able to run more than once without causing harm, as much as possible. This is called idempotency, and it gets its own section later. The reason is retries: if a job fails halfway through, the system will try it again, and the second attempt should not send the email twice or charge the user twice. Designing jobs to be safe to retry is one of the most valuable habits in back-end development, and it starts with keeping jobs small, clear, and careful about side effects.

# A job is a small description of work, not the work itself.
job = {"type": "send_email", "to": "ada@example.com",
       "subject": "Welcome to TaskDay", "attempts": 0}
print(job["type"], "->", job["to"])

Section 22: A Simple Job Queue in a Database Table

You do not need a special tool to build your first job queue. A database table works surprisingly well, and it teaches you the core ideas without any new infrastructure. A jobs table holds one row per job, with columns for what the job is, what data it needs, its status, and how many times it has been tried. Your web request inserts a row, and your worker reads rows, runs them, and updates their status. That is a complete queue, built from a tool you already know, and it is enough for many small applications.

Let us design the table. Each job needs an id, so you can refer to it. It needs a type, such as send_email or generate_report, so the worker knows what kind of work it is. It needs a payload, usually stored as JSON text, holding the details, such as the recipient and the subject. It needs a status: pending, running, done, or failed. It needs an attempts count, so you can limit retries. And it needs timestamps: when it was created, and when it should run, because some jobs are scheduled for later. With those columns, you have everything a basic queue needs, and you can inspect it with ordinary database queries, which makes debugging much easier than with a mysterious black-box system.

The worker's loop is equally simple. It asks the database for the oldest pending job whose scheduled time has arrived, marks it as running, runs it, and then marks it as done or failed. Marking it as running before doing the work matters: if two workers run at once, they might both pick the same job, and the user would get two emails. Claiming the job first, in a single database update, prevents that. This is a small detail with a big consequence, and it is the kind of detail that separates a toy queue from a reliable one. Even a simple queue deserves careful claiming.

A database queue has limits, and you should know them honestly. It is slower than a dedicated queue service, because every job involves database reads and writes. It does not scale as well to thousands of jobs per second. And if your database goes down, your queue goes down with it. For a small application, none of that matters much. For a large one, you will eventually move to a dedicated queue, and the chapter on queues and workers will cover that step. But the ideas transfer directly: a job, a status, a claim, a retry, and a result. Learning them on a database table means you will understand the dedicated tool when you meet it, instead of treating it as magic.

There is one more advantage worth naming: visibility. Because jobs are rows in a table, you can answer questions easily. How many jobs are waiting? Which jobs failed? What was the oldest pending job? Those answers are simple queries, and they are exactly the questions you will ask when something goes wrong. A queue you can inspect is a queue you can fix, and that is worth more than raw speed in the early life of an application.

import sqlite3, json

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("""CREATE TABLE jobs (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, type TEXT,
              payload TEXT, status TEXT, attempts INTEGER)""")
db.execute("INSERT INTO jobs VALUES (1, 'send_email', ?, 'pending', 0)",
           (json.dumps({"to": "ada@example.com"}),))
job = db.execute("SELECT * FROM jobs WHERE status='pending' ORDER BY id LIMIT 1").fetchone()
print("Next job:", job)

Section 23: Workers: The Quiet Helpers

A worker is a process that runs separately from your web server, picks jobs from the queue, and does them. It has no web pages, no users watching it, and no need to answer quickly. It just loops: take a job, do it, record the result, and take the next one. That simplicity is its strength. Because a worker is separate, it can run on its own schedule, at its own pace, and if it crashes, it does not take the web server down with it. Users keep browsing while the worker restarts and carries on.

In development, a worker can be as simple as a Python script you run in a terminal. It connects to the same database, queries for pending jobs, and handles each one with a small function per job type. For a send_email job, it calls your email-sending code. For a generate_report job, it calls your report code. That mapping from job type to function is the heart of the worker, and keeping it tidy matters: each handler should be small, focused, and easy to test on its own. A worker with fifty tangled handlers is hard to maintain. A worker with fifty small, named handlers is a pleasure to work with.

In production, workers need a little more care. They should start automatically when the server starts, and restart automatically if they crash. They should log what they do, so you can see which jobs ran and which failed. And you should be able to run more than one worker at once when the queue grows, so that jobs are processed in parallel. That last point is a major advantage of the design: if your queue is backed up, you add workers, and the work gets done faster, without changing any code. Scaling background work is often as simple as running the same script twice.

Workers also need to be careful about long jobs. A job that takes an hour blocks a worker for an hour, during which it cannot do anything else. If all your workers are blocked by long jobs, short jobs, like welcome emails, wait behind them. The fixes are the usual ones: break long jobs into smaller ones, run different kinds of jobs on different workers, or give urgent job types their own workers. That separation, one pool of workers for emails, another for reports, is a simple way to protect urgent work from slow work, and it is a pattern you will see in real systems of every size.

Finally, workers teach a lesson about ownership. In a web request, the user is waiting, so failures are visible immediately. In a worker, nobody is waiting, so failures can go unnoticed unless you log and monitor them. A worker that silently fails jobs is worse than a worker that crashes loudly, because at least a crash gets noticed. Build your workers to log every outcome, alert on repeated failures, and never swallow an error quietly. Quiet helpers still need to speak up when something goes wrong.

# A tiny worker loop: claim a job, handle it by type, record the result.
handlers = {"send_email": lambda job: print("Email sent to", job["to"])}

def run_worker(queue):
    while queue:
        job = queue.pop(0)
        handlers[job["type"]](job)
        print("Done:", job["type"])

run_worker([{"type": "send_email", "to": "ada@example.com"}])

Section 24: Retrying a Failed Job

Jobs fail. Mail servers go down, outside services return errors, databases get busy, and networks drop connections. A background job system that treats the first failure as final would lose work constantly, because temporary problems are a normal part of running on the internet. Retrying is the answer, but retrying well takes thought. Blindly retrying forever is as bad as never retrying: it wastes resources, can flood a struggling service with repeated requests, and can turn a small problem into a large one. Good retry logic is patient, bounded, and honest about when to give up.

The first idea is to distinguish temporary failures from permanent ones. A mail server that is busy will probably accept the message in a few minutes, so a retry makes sense. An email address that does not exist will never accept the message, so retrying is pointless. Your job handler should return or raise different kinds of errors for these cases, and your worker should only retry the temporary kind. Permanent failures should be marked as failed straight away, with a clear reason recorded, so a human can look at them later. That distinction alone prevents a huge amount of wasted work.

The second idea is backoff: waiting longer between each attempt. If a service is struggling, hitting it again one second later adds to its load and probably fails again. Waiting a minute, then five minutes, then thirty minutes, gives the service time to recover and spreads the load out. A common pattern is exponential backoff, where each wait is roughly double the previous one. The exact numbers matter less than the principle: be patient, and do not hammer a service that is already down. Backoff is a kindness to the services you depend on, and it makes your own system more likely to succeed.

The third idea is a limit. Every job should have a maximum number of attempts, for example three or five. After that, the job is marked as permanently failed and moved aside, to a place where a human can inspect it. That place is often called a dead letter queue, and it gets its own section later. The limit protects you from jobs that retry forever, filling your queue and hiding the fact that something is genuinely broken. A job that fails five times is not having a bad day. It has a real problem, and a human should look at it.

Finally, record every attempt. Each retry should update the job's attempt count and log what happened. Without that record, debugging is guesswork: you cannot tell whether a job failed once or fifty times, or whether it is failing for the same reason each time. With it, patterns become visible. A job that always fails at the same step has a bug. A job that fails at different steps has an unreliable dependency. Retries are not just a reliability feature. They are a source of information about the health of your system.

import time

# Exponential backoff: wait longer after each failure.
def send_with_retries(send, max_attempts=4):
    wait = 5
    for attempt in range(1, max_attempts + 1):
        try:
            send()
            return "sent"
        except Exception as e:
            print(f"Attempt {attempt} failed: {e}; waiting {wait}s")
            time.sleep(0)  # in real code, sleep(wait)
            wait *= 2
    return "failed permanently"

print(send_with_retries(lambda: (_ for _ in ()).throw(RuntimeError("server busy"))))

Section 25: Scheduling Jobs with Time

Not every job should run immediately. Some jobs should run at a specific time: a reminder the day before a deadline, a summary every Monday morning, a cleanup every Sunday night. Scheduling is the ability to say "do this work, but not yet", and it is a natural extension of the queue you have already built. Instead of a job being pending and ready right now, it carries a run time, and the worker ignores it until that time arrives. That one extra field turns a simple queue into a scheduler.

The simplest scheduled job is a delayed job. When a user creates a task with a deadline, your code queues a reminder job with a run time of one day before the deadline. The worker's query changes slightly: instead of taking any pending job, it takes pending jobs whose run time has arrived. Everything else stays the same. The job still has a type, a payload, and retries. The only difference is patience. Delayed jobs are easy to understand and extremely useful, because so many real features are just "do this later": reminders, follow-ups, expiry notices, and trial endings.

Recurring jobs are the next step. A weekly summary is not one job but a series of jobs, one per week. There are two ways to build this. One way is for each job, when it runs, to schedule the next one. That self-scheduling pattern is simple and robust, because if the system is down for a week, the chain simply resumes when it comes back, without trying to catch up on every missed week. The other way is a separate scheduler process that creates jobs on a timetable, which is more flexible but adds a moving part. For a small application, self-scheduling is often enough, and it is easier to reason about.

Scheduling also needs care around time zones and daylight saving. A summary scheduled for eight in the morning should arrive at eight in the user's morning, not eight in the server's morning, and those are often different. Store times in a standard way, usually UTC, and convert to the user's time zone when displaying or scheduling. This is a detail that is easy to get wrong and annoying to fix later, so build the habit early: store UTC, display local. A user who receives a "morning" summary at midnight will not think your scheduling is clever. They will think it is broken.

Finally, scheduled jobs need the same reliability as immediate jobs. A reminder that never fires is worse than no reminder feature at all, because the user trusted it and stopped remembering for themselves. That means scheduled jobs must survive restarts: they live in the database, not in memory, so a server restart does not lose them. It also means you should monitor them, and alert when a scheduled job is overdue. A queue of overdue jobs is a visible symptom of a worker that has stopped, and catching it early saves your users from missed reminders and missed summaries.

from datetime import datetime, timedelta

# A delayed job: a reminder one day before the deadline.
deadline = datetime(2026, 11, 5, 9, 0)
reminder = {"type": "send_reminder", "to": "ada@example.com",
            "run_at": deadline - timedelta(days=1)}
print("Reminder scheduled for:", reminder["run_at"])

Section 26: Cron Jobs and Repeated Work

Cron is an old, simple, and very widely used way to run repeated work on a schedule. The name comes from the Greek word for time, and the idea is a timetable: a list of commands and the times they should run. Most servers have a cron service built in, and you tell it things like "run this script every day at midnight" or "run this cleanup every Sunday at three in the morning". Cron then runs the command at those times, whether or not anyone is logged in. It is not glamorous, but it powers an enormous amount of the internet's background work, and every back-end developer should understand it.

A cron timetable is written as five fields: minute, hour, day of the month, month, and day of the week, followed by the command. Each field can be a number, a star meaning every value, or a pattern like every fifteen minutes. The format looks cryptic at first, but it becomes readable quickly with practice. The important thing is to understand that cron is a clock that runs commands, and that your command can be a Python script that does real work.

Cron is excellent for maintenance work: cleaning up old data, generating nightly reports, checking for overdue tasks, sending daily digests. It is less good for work that must happen at an exact second, because cron's smallest unit is a minute, and because a busy server might run a job slightly late. It is also less good for work that must happen exactly once: if a server restarts during a cron job, or if two servers both run the same cron timetable, a job can run twice. For work where running twice is harmless, like cleaning up already-deleted rows, that is fine. For work where running twice is harmful, like sending an email, you need a safeguard, such as a lock or a record of what has already been sent.

That safeguard is worth understanding, because it appears in many forms. One simple approach is a lock file or a database flag: when the job starts, it claims the work, and if another copy starts, it sees the claim and exits. Another approach is to make the work idempotent, so running it twice has the same effect as running it once. A cleanup that deletes rows older than thirty days can run twice safely, because the second run finds nothing left to delete. Designing cron jobs to be safe to run twice is the simplest way to make them reliable, and it is a habit that serves you well beyond cron.

Finally, cron jobs need the same care as workers: logging, monitoring, and alerts. A cron job that fails silently every night for a month is a common and embarrassing failure mode. Its output should be logged somewhere you actually look, and its failures should alert someone. Some teams send cron output to a monitoring service, or have the job report its own success. However you do it, do not let a scheduled job become invisible. The whole point of automation is that work happens without you watching, but that only works if something watches the automation.

# A cron-style decision: should this cleanup run now?
# Cron line "0 3 * * 0" means: 03:00 every Sunday.
def is_sunday_3am(weekday, hour):
    return weekday == 6 and hour == 3

print(is_sunday_3am(6, 3))  # True: run the weekly cleanup
print(is_sunday_3am(2, 3))  # False: not Sunday, skip

Section 27: Sending Email in the Background

You have now met email and background jobs separately. This section brings them together, because sending email in the background is the single most common use of a job queue, and it is the pattern that makes most other background work make sense. The rule is simple: when a user action should trigger an email, the request queues a job, and a worker sends the email. The user gets a fast response, the email is sent reliably, and failures are handled by retry logic instead of by showing the user an error page.

Let us trace a signup. The user submits the form. The request creates the account, inserts a send_email job into the jobs table, and returns the welcome page. The request is done in a fraction of a second. Meanwhile, a worker picks up the job, builds the message, connects to the mail server, and sends it. If the mail server is temporarily down, the worker retries with backoff, and the email arrives a few minutes later. The user never sees the retry. They only see a fast signup and, shortly after, a welcome email. From their point of view, everything just worked, which is the highest praise a system can earn.

There are details that make this pattern work well. First, the job's payload should contain everything the worker needs: the recipient, the subject, and the body, or enough information to build the body. It should not contain a reference to something that might change before the worker runs, unless the worker re-reads the current data and that is what you want. For example, if a task's title changes between queuing and sending, should the email show the old title or the new one? There is no universal answer, but you should choose deliberately, because the default behaviour of your code is an answer whether you thought about it or not.

Second, the worker should send each email exactly once, as far as possible. That means claiming the job before sending, marking it done after sending, and being careful about the gap in between. If the worker crashes after sending but before marking the job done, the job will be retried and the email may be sent twice. That is a real risk, and the honest answer is that exactly-once delivery is hard. The practical answer is to make duplicates rare, by claiming carefully and marking done promptly, and to make duplicates harmless where you can, for example by including a note that a message is a reminder rather than an action. Users forgive a duplicate welcome email. They do not forgive a duplicate charge.

Third, keep the email-sending code in one place, used by both the worker and any place that must send mail immediately. Duplicated sending code drifts apart over time, and one copy will eventually have a bug the other does not. A single function, tested once, used everywhere, is the professional choice. The worker becomes a thin wrapper around that function: it takes a job, calls the function, and records the result. That separation keeps the system easy to understand and easy to fix.

# The request queues; the worker sends. Same function, two callers.
def send_email(to, subject, body):
    print(f"Sending '{subject}' to {to}")
    return True

def signup(username, email):
    print(f"Account created for {username}")
    job = {"type": "send_email", "to": email,
           "subject": "Welcome", "body": "Hello!"}
    return job  # worker later calls send_email(job["to"], ...)

job = signup("ada", "ada@example.com")
send_email(job["to"], job["subject"], job["body"])

Section 28: Generating Reports in the Background

Reports are a classic background job, because they combine everything that makes work unsuitable for a request: they can be slow, they can involve a lot of data, and nobody needs the result the instant they click. A weekly summary, a monthly statement, an export of all tasks as a file, or a dashboard snapshot are all reports, and they all follow the same pattern: a user asks for one, or a schedule triggers one, the request queues a job, a worker builds the report, and the user is told when it is ready. Understanding this pattern prepares you for a whole family of features.

The first step is deciding what a report is. A report is a snapshot: data gathered at a particular moment, shaped into a readable form. It might be an email, a file the user can download, or a page they can view. The worker's job is to gather the data, usually with database queries, shape it, and store the result somewhere the user can reach it. Storing the result matters, because the worker and the user are not in the same moment. The user might ask for a report on Monday and download it on Wednesday. The result must wait for them, in a file or a database row, with a link or a notification pointing to it.

The second step is handling size. A report over a year of data might involve thousands of rows, and building it might take minutes. That is fine for a worker, which has no user waiting, but it means you should think about memory. Loading every row into memory at once can exhaust a small server. A better approach is to process data in batches, or to let the database do the summarising with queries that count, sum, and group, returning a small result instead of a huge one. The database is very good at summarising. Let it do the heavy lifting, and your worker will stay fast and light.

The third step is telling the user the report is ready. The most common approach is a notification: an email or an in-app message with a link to the report. That notification is itself a small job, or part of the same job's final step. The user experience should be clear: "Your report is being prepared" when they ask, and "Your report is ready" when it is done, with a link. A user left wondering whether their report is coming will ask support, or ask again, creating duplicate jobs. A clear status, even a simple one, prevents that.

The fourth step is cleanup. Reports take up storage, and old reports that nobody downloads are waste. A sensible policy is to keep reports for a period, such as thirty days, and then delete them, ideally with another background job. Tell users the retention period when they download, so there are no surprises. And think about privacy: a report may contain personal data, so its download link should require the user to be logged in, and should not be guessable. A report is not just a file. It is someone's data, and it deserves the same protection as the rest of your application.

db.execute("CREATE TABLE tasks (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, done INTEGER)")
db.executemany("INSERT INTO tasks (done) VALUES (?)", [(1,), (1,), (0,)])
total, done = db.execute("SELECT COUNT(*), SUM(done) FROM tasks").fetchone()
print(f"Weekly report: {done} of {total} tasks completed")

Section 29: Cleaning Up Old Data in the Background

Every application accumulates data it no longer needs: expired sessions, old notifications, soft-deleted records, temporary files, and logs from months ago. Left alone, this data grows forever, slowing queries, filling disks, and making backups larger and slower. Cleaning it up is unglamorous work, and it is exactly the kind of work that belongs in the background, on a schedule, where it can run quietly and regularly without bothering any user. A system that never cleans up is a system slowly drowning in its own history.

The first decision is what to clean and how long to keep it. Different data has different lifetimes. Sessions might expire after a day. Notifications might be kept for ninety days. Completed jobs might be kept for a week, long enough to debug problems, but not forever. Old reports might be kept for thirty days. These lifetimes should be written down as policy, not invented each time someone remembers to clean up. Policy turns cleanup from a panic response to a routine, and it gives you a clear answer when someone asks why a particular record is gone. It also forces a useful conversation: what data do we actually need, and for how long?

The second decision is how to delete safely. Deleting a large amount of data in one go can lock tables and slow the application for everyone. A gentler approach is to delete in batches, a few hundred or a few thousand rows at a time, pausing briefly between batches. That keeps the system responsive while the cleanup runs. It is also wise to be careful with relationships: deleting a user should also handle their tasks, or leave them in a sensible state, depending on your design. A cleanup job that deletes the wrong rows is worse than no cleanup at all, so test it on a copy of the data first, and log exactly what it deleted.

The third decision is what to do instead of deleting. Sometimes the right answer is not deletion but archiving: moving old data to a cheaper, slower store, or summarising it before removing the detail. A year's worth of daily activity might be summarised into monthly totals, and then the daily rows removed. That keeps the useful information and discards the bulk. Archiving is more work than deleting, so reserve it for data that has genuine long-term value, such as financial records. For the rest, honest deletion, after the retention period, is the right choice.

Finally, cleanup jobs should be visible. Log how many rows were deleted, and from which tables, so you can spot a cleanup that suddenly deletes far more than usual, which might indicate a bug or a change in the data. Alert if a cleanup fails, because a failed cleanup that nobody notices will silently let data grow again. Cleanup is maintenance, and maintenance only works if someone notices when it stops happening. A small, regular, logged, monitored cleanup job is one of the cheapest investments you can make in the long-term health of an application.

import sqlite3
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

# Delete old notifications in a batch, and log how many went.
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("CREATE TABLE notifications (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, created TEXT)")
db.execute("INSERT INTO notifications VALUES (1, '2025-01-01')")
db.execute("INSERT INTO notifications VALUES (2, '2026-10-30')")
cutoff = (datetime.now() - timedelta(days=90)).isoformat()
cursor = db.execute("DELETE FROM notifications WHERE created < ?", (cutoff,))
print("Deleted", cursor.rowcount, "old notification(s)")

Section 30: Job Status and Progress

When work moves to the background, the user loses sight of it. They clicked a button, the request answered quickly, and now something is happening somewhere else, invisibly. That invisibility creates anxiety: is my report being built, or did it fail? Is my import running, or stuck? Job status and progress are the answer. They give the user, and the developer, a window into background work, turning an invisible process into something that can be watched, understood, and trusted.

The simplest form of status is the job's state, which you already have in the jobs table: pending, running, done, or failed. Exposing that state is often enough for simple features. A page that says "Your report is being prepared" while the job is pending or running, and "Your report is ready" when it is done, answers the user's main question. If the job failed, the page can say so honestly, and offer a way to try again. Honest failure messages are far better than endless loading screens, because they let the user act instead of waiting forever for something that will never arrive.

Progress goes one step further. For a long job, such as importing a thousand records, the worker can update a progress field as it goes: how many records are done, out of how many in total. The user sees a percentage, or a count, and can judge whether the job is moving or stuck. Progress updates must be cheap, though. Updating the database after every single record can slow the job down. A common approach is to update every hundred records, or every few seconds, which gives a smooth sense of movement without drowning the database in updates. Progress is a courtesy, not a precise instrument, and approximate progress is fine.

Status and progress also help developers. When a user reports that a report never arrived, the first thing to check is the job's status. If it is pending, the worker may be down or backed up. If it is running for an unusually long time, it may be stuck. If it failed, its error log tells you why. Without status, debugging is guesswork. With it, you can answer support questions in minutes. That is why even internal jobs, the ones users never see, deserve a status. A system you can inspect is a system you can fix.

There is one more audience for status: the system itself. A job that has been running far longer than usual may be stuck, and a monitoring check can flag it. A job that has been pending far longer than usual may mean the workers have stopped. These checks turn status from a display feature into a health signal. Build them early, even in simple form, such as a daily check for jobs older than a day that are still pending. The earlier you notice a stuck system, the smaller the damage, and the easier the fix.

# A job's status tells the user and the developer what is happening.
job = {"type": "generate_report", "status": "running",
       "done": 400, "total": 1000}
percent = job["done"] * 100 // job["total"]
print(f"Report: {job['status']}, {percent}% complete")

Section 31: Idempotency: Doing a Job Only Once

Idempotency is a long word for a simple and powerful idea: doing the same job twice should have the same effect as doing it once. A job is idempotent if running it again does not cause harm, such as sending a duplicate email, charging a user twice, or creating two copies of the same record. This matters because background jobs are retried, workers can crash at awkward moments, and cron jobs can run twice. In a world where duplicates happen, idempotency is what keeps them harmless. It is one of the most valuable design habits in back-end development, and it is worth learning early.

The classic example is sending an email. If a worker sends a welcome email and then crashes before marking the job done, the job will be retried, and the user gets the email twice. A duplicate welcome email is mildly annoying but harmless. A duplicate payment charge is not harmless at all. The difference is that email has no built-in protection against duplicates, while a well-designed payment system does: it uses a unique key for each payment, and if the same key arrives twice, the second attempt returns the original result instead of charging again. That unique key is called an idempotency key, and it is a pattern you can use in your own systems wherever duplicates would be harmful.

There are several practical ways to make jobs idempotent. One is to check before acting: before creating a record, check whether it already exists, using a unique field such as an email address or an order reference. If it exists, skip the creation. Another is to use database constraints: a unique constraint on a column makes the database itself refuse duplicates, turning a second attempt into a harmless error you can ignore. Another is to design the action so that repeating it is naturally safe: setting a status to "done" twice has the same result as setting it once, while adding ten to a balance twice does not. Prefer actions that set a final state over actions that add to a running total, wherever you can.

Idempotency also shapes how you think about job payloads. A job that says "send the welcome email for user 42" can be checked: has user 42 already received a welcome email? A job that says "send an email" with no details cannot be checked at all. Including a clear identity in the payload, such as the user, the event, and a unique reference, makes it possible to detect duplicates. That identity is cheap to add at queue time and invaluable at retry time. It is the difference between a system that guesses and a system that knows.

Finally, accept that perfect idempotency is sometimes impossible. Some outside services do not support it, and some actions, like sending an email, cannot be undone. In those cases, the goal is to make duplicates rare and harmless, rather than impossible. Claim jobs carefully, mark them done promptly, and use idempotency keys where the stakes are high. A system that tries to be idempotent, even imperfectly, will produce far fewer embarrassing duplicates than a system that never thought about the problem at all.

# An idempotency key: the same key twice means the same result, not double work.
sent = {}

def send_once(key, to):
    if key in sent:
        return "already sent, skipping duplicate"
    sent[key] = to
    return f"sent to {to}"

print(send_once("welcome-42", "ada@example.com"))
print(send_once("welcome-42", "ada@example.com"))

Section 32: Handling Slow Third-Party Services

Almost every real application depends on services it does not control: a mail provider, a payment service, a maps service, a social network. These are called third-party services, and they are wonderful, because they let a small team offer features that would take years to build. They are also a source of risk, because they can be slow, they can fail, and they can change their behaviour without asking you. A back-end developer's job is to use these services well while protecting the application from their worst days. The key skill is treating every outside call as something that might be slow or fail, and designing for that from the start.

The first protection is a timeout: a limit on how long your code will wait for an answer before giving up. Without one, a call to a service that never answers will hang your request, or your worker, forever, holding resources and blocking everything behind it. With a timeout, the call fails cleanly after a few seconds, and your code can retry, fall back, or tell the user. Every outside call should have a timeout, chosen sensibly: long enough for a slow but working service, short enough that a dead service does not freeze your system. A timeout is not pessimism. It is a boundary that keeps one slow service from becoming your whole system's problem.

The second protection is the background job. Calls to outside services belong in workers, not in requests, for the reasons you have already learned: they are slow, they can fail, and the user should not wait for them. If a mail service is down, a queued email job retries later, and the user never knows. If the same call happened inside a request, the user would see an error for a problem they did not cause and cannot fix. Moving outside calls into the background is one of the simplest ways to make an application feel reliable, because it hides the unreliability of the internet behind a queue.

The third protection is a fallback. Ask what should happen when the service is unavailable. For a welcome email, the fallback is to retry later, because the email is not urgent. For a login code sent by text, the fallback might be to offer an email code instead, because the user is waiting. For a map display, the fallback might be to show a simple message instead of a map. Not every feature needs a fallback, but every important feature should have an answer to the question "what if this service is down?" A feature with no answer will produce that answer at the worst possible moment, in front of a user.

The fourth protection is monitoring and limits. Watch how often each outside service fails and how slow it is, because a service that is slowly getting worse is a warning worth hearing early. Limit how many calls you make, both to respect the service's rate limits and to control costs. And keep the code that talks to each service in one place, so that when the service changes its rules, you have one place to update instead of twenty. Outside services are partners, not parts of your code. Treat them with the respect, and the suspicion, that a partnership deserves.

import urllib.request

# Always set a timeout on outside calls: never wait forever.
try:
    with urllib.request.urlopen("https://example.com", timeout=5) as response:
        print("Status:", response.status)
except Exception as e:
    print("Service unavailable, will retry later:", type(e).__name__)

Section 33: Timeouts and Giving Up Gracefully

Timeouts deserve a section of their own, because they are easy to forget and expensive to lack. A timeout is a decision made in advance: if this work has not finished by a certain time, stop waiting. That decision protects your system in ways that are not obvious until you have seen a system without timeouts. A single slow call can hold a worker, a held worker can block a queue, a blocked queue can delay every other job, and suddenly one slow service has frozen your whole application. Timeouts break that chain at the first link, by refusing to wait forever.

There are different kinds of timeout, and it helps to know them. A connection timeout limits how long you wait to connect to a service at all. A read timeout limits how long you wait for an answer once connected. A total timeout limits the whole operation. For a learner, the key point is not the exact categories, but the habit: whenever your code waits for something outside itself, set a limit. Databases, mail servers, web services, and file operations can all hang, and a limit turns a hang into a clean, catchable failure that your code can handle.

Giving up gracefully is the second half of the skill. When a timeout fires, your code should not crash the worker or show the user a technical error. It should log what happened, mark the job for retry if the failure looks temporary, and, if the user is involved, tell them something useful. "We could not send that right now, but we will try again" is a graceful failure. A blank page, a crash, or a raw error message is not. The difference is not just politeness. A graceful failure keeps the user's trust, because it shows the system noticed the problem and has a plan. An ungraceful failure makes the user feel the system is broken and nobody is in charge.

There is also a question of how long a timeout should be. Too short, and you give up on work that would have succeeded, creating failures out of impatience. Too long, and you hold resources while a dead service keeps you waiting. The right value depends on the service and the situation. A mail send might deserve thirty seconds. A quick lookup might deserve five. A background job can afford a longer timeout than a web request, because nobody is waiting. The exact numbers matter less than the practice of choosing them deliberately, writing them down, and revisiting them when a service's behaviour changes.

Finally, timeouts interact with retries. A job that times out and retries three times, each with a long timeout, can take a very long time in total, holding a worker throughout. That is why timeouts, retries, and backoff must be designed together, not separately. A short timeout with a few patient retries is usually better than a long timeout with many retries. Think of the whole journey of a job, from first attempt to final failure, and make sure that journey is bounded and sensible. A system that gives up gracefully, after a fair effort, is a system that stays available for everything else.

# A timeout turns a hang into a clean failure you can handle.
import urllib.request

try:
    with urllib.request.urlopen("https://example.com", timeout=3) as r:
        print("OK:", r.status)
except Exception:
    print("Timed out: logging it and queuing a retry, not crashing")

Section 34: Logging Background Jobs

Logging is the practice of writing down what your system does, as it does it, so that later you can reconstruct what happened. For background jobs, logging is not optional decoration. It is the only way to know what a worker did, because nobody was watching. When a user says an email never arrived, the log is where you look first. When a job fails five times, the log tells you whether it failed the same way each time. A worker without logging is a black box, and black boxes are miserable to debug.

Good job logs answer a few simple questions. Which job ran? When did it start and finish? Did it succeed or fail? If it failed, why? How many attempts has it had? A single log line per job, containing the job id, type, outcome, and duration, answers most of these. Extra detail, such as the error message on failure, should be logged too, but keep it structured and searchable. A log that says "something went wrong" is barely better than no log. A log that says "job 482, send_email to ada@example.com, failed: connection refused by smtp.example.com, attempt 3 of 5" tells you exactly what to do next.

There are also things logs must not contain. Never log passwords, tokens, or secret keys, even when debugging. A log file that contains a password is a password leak waiting to happen, because logs get copied, stored, and shared more casually than databases. Never log full personal data unnecessarily either. An email address in a job log is often necessary to debug delivery, but a full message body containing private content usually is not. Log the facts you need to fix problems, and no more. That restraint is part of being a responsible developer.

Logs also need a home and a lifetime. A worker's logs should go to a file or a logging service where you can actually find them, not just to a terminal window that closes when you log out. They should be kept long enough to debug problems, which usually means days or weeks, not forever, because logs themselves take up storage. A rotation policy, which archives or deletes old logs automatically, keeps the storage under control. And logs should be searchable: when you need to find all failures for one job type, or one user, you should be able to search, not read thousands of lines by hand.

Finally, logs and monitoring work together, which is a theme of the next chapter. Logging records what happened. Monitoring watches those records and alerts you when something looks wrong, such as a sudden rise in failures. A worker that logs well but is never monitored will still fail quietly. A worker that is monitored but logs poorly will alert you without telling you why. Build both, and your background system becomes something you can trust, because when it breaks, you will know, and you will know why.

import logging

# One clear line per job outcome: id, type, result, and reason.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(message)s")
logging.info("job 482 send_email to ada@example.com: sent in 1.2s")
logging.error("job 483 send_email to ben@example.com: failed, connection refused, attempt 3/5")

Section 35: Priorities: Urgent Jobs First

Not all jobs are equally urgent. A password reset email should go out in seconds, because a user is waiting to get back into their account. A weekly summary can wait an hour without anyone noticing. A cleanup job can wait until the middle of the night. If all these jobs sit in a single queue and are processed in the order they arrived, an urgent job can end up waiting behind a thousand routine ones. Priorities solve this. They let the system do the most important work first, even when it arrived last.

The simplest priority system has a few levels, such as high, normal, and low. When a job is queued, it is given a level. When a worker picks a job, it takes the oldest high-priority job first, then normal, then low. That is all there is to it, and it works well. The key is to use high priority sparingly. If everything is high priority, nothing is, and the system is back to first-come, first-served, with extra complexity. Reserve high priority for work where a user is actively waiting, or where a delay causes real harm, such as security alerts and password resets. Everything else is normal or low.

A more advanced option is separate queues for separate kinds of work. One queue for emails, one for reports, one for cleanup, each with its own workers. That separation protects urgent work even better than priorities, because a flood of report jobs cannot block email workers at all: they are different workers, reading different queues. It also lets you scale each kind of work independently. If reports are slow, add report workers. If emails are backed up, add email workers. Separate queues cost a little more setup, but they are the standard approach in larger systems, and they are worth understanding even if you start with a single queue.

There is a danger to watch for, called starvation. If high-priority jobs keep arriving without pause, low-priority jobs may never run at all. A cleanup job that waits forever is not a disaster, but a weekly summary that never sends is. The fixes are simple: occasionally promote old low-priority jobs, or reserve some worker capacity for them, so that even the lowest job eventually runs. A system that is fair as well as fast is a system that users can rely on for everything, not just for the urgent things.

Finally, priorities should be visible. When a job is queued, its priority should be recorded alongside it, so that when someone asks why a report is late, you can see that it was low priority and that high-priority work was ahead of it. That transparency turns a confusing delay into an understandable one. And when you review your system, check whether your priorities still match reality. A feature that was once urgent may have become routine, and a routine job may have become important as the application grew. Priorities are not set in stone. They are a living part of your design.

# Workers take high-priority jobs first, whatever order they arrived in.
jobs = [{"type": "cleanup", "priority": "low"},
        {"type": "password_reset", "priority": "high"},
        {"type": "weekly_summary", "priority": "normal"}]
order = {"high": 0, "normal": 1, "low": 2}
jobs.sort(key=lambda j: order[j["priority"]])
print([j["type"] for j in jobs])

Section 36: When a Queue Grows Too Long

A queue that grows faster than workers can empty it is a warning sign. At first, the symptom is small: emails arrive a little late, reports take a little longer. If the growth continues, the delay becomes minutes, then hours, then a backlog so large that jobs expire before they run. A growing queue is not just a performance problem. It is a sign that something in the system has changed: more users, slower jobs, a failing dependency, or a worker that stopped. Learning to notice and respond to a growing queue is a core operational skill.

The first step is measurement. You cannot manage what you do not measure, so your system should report two numbers regularly: how many jobs are waiting, and how old the oldest waiting job is. The count tells you the size of the backlog. The age tells you how long users are waiting. A queue with ten thousand jobs that are all a minute old is a busy system catching up. A queue with ten jobs that are all a day old is a stuck system. Both numbers matter, and both should be visible on a dashboard or in a regular report, not hidden in a database you only check during a crisis.

The second step is diagnosis. Why is the queue growing? The common causes are worth memorising. Workers may have stopped, because of a crash or a failed deployment. A dependency may have slowed down, making each job take longer. Traffic may have genuinely increased, because the application grew. Or a bug may be creating jobs in a loop, flooding the queue with work that should never have existed. Each cause has a different fix, and guessing wrong wastes time. Check the workers first, then the job durations, then the traffic, then the job creation. That order, from the most common to the least, will solve most backlogs quickly.

The third step is response. If workers stopped, restart them. If jobs are slow, find out why, and consider splitting them or adding workers. If traffic grew, add workers, which is the beauty of the design: more workers mean faster processing, with no code changes. If a bug is flooding the queue, fix the bug and clear the flood, carefully, so you do not delete legitimate jobs. In an emergency, you can also shed load: pause low-priority jobs, such as cleanups and summaries, so that urgent jobs get through. Shedding load is a deliberate choice to do less, well, rather than everything, badly.

The fourth step is prevention. Set an alert for queue depth and age, so you hear about growth before users do. Review queue numbers weekly, so trends are visible before they become crises. And design for growth from the start: keep jobs small, keep workers easy to add, and keep priorities working, so that when growth comes, the system bends instead of breaking. A queue that grows is not a failure. It is information. The failure is only in ignoring it.

# Two numbers tell the story of a queue: depth and oldest age.
queue = [{"age_minutes": 5}, {"age_minutes": 40}, {"age_minutes": 120}]
print("Jobs waiting:", len(queue))
print("Oldest job age (minutes):", max(j["age_minutes"] for j in queue))

Section 37: Dead Letter Queues

Some jobs fail no matter how many times you retry them. The address does not exist, the data is corrupt, the outside service rejects the request permanently. After the maximum number of attempts, such a job needs somewhere to go, because deleting it would lose the evidence, and retrying it forever would clog the queue. That somewhere is called a dead letter queue. The name comes from postal services, where undeliverable mail ends up in a dead letter office. In software, it is a separate place where permanently failed jobs wait for a human to look at them.

A dead letter queue is usually just another table, or another queue, with the same job data plus extra information: how many times it was tried, what the last error was, and when it failed for the last time. That extra information is what makes it useful. A human, or a developer, can look at the dead jobs and see patterns. Ten jobs failing with the same error probably share a cause, such as a changed service or a bad data import. One job failing with a unique error is probably a unique problem, such as a single corrupt record. Without the dead letter queue, those patterns are invisible, because failed jobs would simply vanish.

What happens to dead jobs depends on the situation. Some can be fixed and retried by hand: correct the data, then requeue the job. Some should be discarded: the address will never exist, so the job is pointless, and the right action is to mark the address invalid and move on. Some reveal a bug, and the fix is in the code, after which the dead jobs can be retried with the fixed version. The key point is that the decision is made by a person, with full information, not by an automated loop that retries blindly. Automation handles the routine. Humans handle the exceptions. A dead letter queue is where the two meet.

Dead letter queues also need care of their own. They should be checked regularly, ideally daily, because a growing dead letter queue is a sign of a problem that is getting worse. They should alert when they grow suddenly, because a sudden flood of dead jobs usually means something broke. And they should not grow forever: jobs that have been dead for a long time, and that nobody will ever retry, should eventually be archived or deleted, with a record of what they were. A dead letter queue that nobody checks is just a second place for work to be forgotten. Its value comes entirely from the habit of looking at it.

Finally, think of the dead letter queue as part of your system's honesty. A system that pretends every job succeeds is lying to itself. A system that admits some jobs fail, keeps them visible, and gives someone a chance to fix them, is a system that can be trusted. Failure is normal. Hiding failure is the problem. Dead letter queues are a simple, proven way to face failure openly and deal with it properly.

# After max attempts, a job moves to the dead letter queue, not to oblivion.
dead_letter_queue = []

def give_up(job, error):
    job["last_error"] = error
    dead_letter_queue.append(job)
    return f"Job {job['id']} moved to dead letters: {error}"

print(give_up({"id": 99, "type": "send_email"}, "address does not exist"))
print("Dead letters waiting for review:", len(dead_letter_queue))

Section 38: Testing Email Without Spamming People

Testing email features is a problem with an obvious trap: if you test with real addresses, real people get test messages. If you test with your own address, your own inbox fills with test mail. And if a bug in your test sends mail to your whole user list, the damage is real and embarrassing. Fortunately, testing email without spamming anyone is a solved problem, with several good approaches, and learning them early will save you from a mistake that every developer dreads making.

The simplest approach is to never send real mail from development at all. Instead, configure your development setup to write outgoing messages to a file, or to print them to the terminal, rather than handing them to a mail server. Python makes this easy: your email-sending function can take a "send" step that, in development, just saves the message. You can then read the saved message, check its subject, its body, and its links, and confirm it looks right, without any message leaving your computer. This approach is fast, free, and completely safe, and it is enough for most day-to-day development.

The next approach is a fake mailbox: a small local service that pretends to be a mail server, accepts your messages, and shows them in a web page you can browse. You send mail to it exactly as you would to a real server, but the messages never leave your machine. They appear in the fake mailbox's inbox, where you can open them, check their formatting, and click their links. This is closer to real behaviour than writing to a file, because it exercises your actual sending code, including the SMTP conversation, while still being completely safe. Several free tools provide fake mailboxes, and they are a standard part of a developer's toolkit.

A third approach is a real mail service's test mode, sometimes called a sandbox. Some mail providers let you send to a small list of verified test addresses, or capture messages without delivering them. This is useful when you need to test deliverability, such as whether your authentication records are correct, because a fake mailbox cannot tell you whether a real provider would accept your mail. Test mode is more realistic, but it still requires care: only verified test addresses should ever be used, and production credentials should never be used in development.

Whatever approach you choose, follow two rules. First, make it impossible, by configuration, for development code to send to real users. Development and production should use different settings, different credentials, and different mail servers, so that a mistake in one cannot reach the other. Second, test the unhappy paths too: what happens when the mail server is down, when an address bounces, when a template has a missing value? Those paths are where real bugs hide, and they are safe to test with a fake mailbox. A developer who tests email carefully never has to apologise for a test message sent to a thousand strangers.

# In development, save the message instead of sending it.
import os

def send_or_save(message, to):
    if os.environ.get("APP_ENV") == "development":
        with open("/tmp/outgoing-mail.txt", "a") as f:
            f.write(f"To: {to}\n{message}\n---\n")
        return "saved, not sent"
    return "sent via mail server"

print(send_or_save("Hello Ada, welcome!", "ada@example.com"))

Section 39: Fake Mailboxes in Development

A fake mailbox deserves a closer look, because it is one of those tools that seems like a luxury until you use it, and then becomes something you never want to work without. A fake mailbox is a small program that runs on your own computer, listens for SMTP connections like a real mail server, and stores every message it receives in a place you can browse. Your application sends mail to it exactly as it would to a real server, with the same code and the same settings, except for the server address. The messages never leave your machine. They pile up in the fake mailbox, where you can inspect them at your leisure.

The value of a fake mailbox is that it tests the real path. Writing messages to a file tests your message content, but it skips the SMTP conversation, the login, and the connection handling. A fake mailbox tests all of that, because your code really connects, really logs in, and really sends. If your code has a bug in how it connects, or forgets to close the connection, or mishandles an error, the fake mailbox will reveal it, while a file-based approach will not. At the same time, it is completely safe: no real person ever receives anything, no matter how buggy your code is, because the fake mailbox delivers to nobody.

Setting one up is straightforward. You run the fake mailbox program, which usually provides a web page on your local machine showing received messages. You point your application's mail settings at it, typically a local address and a port, instead of your real mail server. Then you use your application normally: sign up, request a password reset, trigger a notification. Each message appears in the fake mailbox within seconds. You can open it, check that the subject and body are right, verify that links work, and confirm that the HTML version looks sensible. It turns email testing from a chore involving your own inbox into a quick, visual check.

Fake mailboxes also help with a subtle problem: testing with realistic data. When you test with your own address, you tend to use your own name, your own data, and your own habits. A fake mailbox lets you create test users freely, with different names, including names with unusual characters, and see how your templates handle them. Does a name with an apostrophe break your HTML? Does a very long name overflow your layout? These are the details that embarrass an application in production, and a fake mailbox makes them easy to catch in development, before any real user sees them.

Finally, a fake mailbox teaches a broader lesson about development tools: the best tools make the safe thing also the easy thing. When sending to a fake mailbox is as easy as sending to a real server, you will test email often, and you will catch problems early. When testing email is hard or risky, you will avoid it, and problems will reach production. Invest a few minutes in setting up a fake mailbox, and it will repay you many times over, in confidence and in avoided apologies.

# Point development mail at a local fake mailbox, not a real server.
settings = {"development": {"host": "localhost", "port": 1025},
            "production": {"host": "smtp.example.com", "port": 587}}
env = "development"
print("Mail server for", env, ":", settings[env])

Section 40: Testing Background Jobs

Background jobs need testing just as much as web requests do, but they are tested differently, because they do not have a user, a browser, or an immediate response to check. A job test usually works like this: create a job, run its handler directly, and check the result. Did the email get queued correctly? Did the report contain the right numbers? Did the job mark itself done? Did a failing job retry the right number of times? These are all questions you can answer with small, focused tests that run the handler function with known inputs and check its outputs and side effects.

The first kind of job test checks the handler in isolation. Take the function that handles a send_email job, give it a fake job, and run it with a fake mail sender that records what it was asked to send instead of actually sending. Then check that the recorded message has the right recipient, subject, and body. This kind of test is fast and reliable, because it does not need a database, a mail server, or a worker. It tests the logic of the job, which is where most bugs live. If the logic is right, the plumbing around it, the queue and the worker, is much less likely to surprise you.

The second kind of test checks the queue and worker together, at a small scale. Queue a job in a test database, run one pass of the worker loop, and check that the job is now marked done. Then queue a job that fails, run the worker, and check that it is marked for retry, with its attempt count increased. These tests catch plumbing bugs: jobs that are claimed twice, jobs that are marked done before they finish, jobs that disappear when the worker crashes. They are slower than handler tests, because they use a real database, but they test the parts that handler tests cannot reach.

The third kind of test checks timing and scheduling. A reminder job scheduled for tomorrow should not run today. A daily job should run once per day, not twice. These tests often use a fake clock, a way to pretend the current time is something else, so you can test tomorrow's behaviour without waiting until tomorrow. Time-based bugs are notoriously hard to catch by manual testing, because they only appear on particular days or at particular hours. Automated tests with a fake clock catch them in seconds.

Finally, test the failure paths, not just the happy ones. What happens when the mail server is down? When the database is locked? When a job's payload is missing a field? A job system that has only been tested with perfect inputs will fail in confusing ways when reality arrives. A job system tested with failures will fail gracefully, retry sensibly, and land in the dead letter queue with a clear error. Testing failures is not pessimism. It is the recognition that background jobs live in an unreliable world, and that reliability is a feature you build, not a feature you hope for.

# Test a job handler with a fake sender: no real mail leaves the test.
sent = []

def fake_send(to, subject):
    sent.append((to, subject))

def handle_send_email(job):
    fake_send(job["to"], job["subject"])
    return "done"

print(handle_send_email({"to": "ada@example.com", "subject": "Welcome"}))
print("Recorded sends:", sent)

Section 41: Rate Limits on Sending Email

Mail providers limit how much mail you can send, and those limits are not there to annoy you. They exist because unlimited sending is exactly what spammers want, and providers must protect their users and their own reputation. A rate limit might say you can send a hundred messages per hour, or ten thousand per day, depending on your account and your history. New senders get low limits, which rise as they prove themselves with wanted mail and few complaints. Understanding these limits, and designing around them, is part of sending email professionally.

The first consequence of rate limits is that bulk sending must be paced. If you have ten thousand summaries to send and a limit of one thousand per hour, the honest answer is that sending will take ten hours, not ten minutes. A queue makes this easy: workers send at a steady rate, and if the provider reports that the limit is reached, the workers pause and retry later. Trying to dodge a limit by using several accounts, or by switching providers rapidly, is a bad idea. Providers notice, and the penalty for evading limits is usually much worse than the limit itself, including losing the account entirely.

The second consequence is that your code must handle the provider's answer when a limit is hit. Providers usually return a specific error, often a temporary failure code, meaning "slow down and try later". Your worker should treat that as a retryable failure, with backoff, not as a permanent failure. It should also log the event, so you can see how often limits are hit. Hitting a limit occasionally is normal during a large send. Hitting it constantly means your sending rate is too high for your account, and you need to slow down, spread the send over more time, or ask the provider for a higher limit, which is a legitimate request for an established sender with a good record.

The third consequence is about priorities again. When sending capacity is limited, what should go first? Password resets and security alerts should jump ahead of weekly summaries and marketing messages, because a delayed summary is an inconvenience, while a delayed password reset blocks a user from their account. A priority queue, combined with rate limiting, gives you exactly this behaviour: urgent jobs first, routine jobs when capacity allows. Without priorities, a large routine send can delay urgent mail for hours, and users will notice.

Finally, rate limits are a reason to keep your lists clean and your mail wanted. Every message sent to a bouncing address, or to someone who marks it as spam, wastes part of your limit and damages your reputation, which can lower your limit further. A clean list, permission-based sending, and honest content are not just good manners. They are the way to earn higher limits and keep them. In email, as in many parts of back-end work, good behaviour is rewarded with more freedom, and bad behaviour is punished with less.

# Pace bulk sending: respect the provider's hourly limit.
LIMIT_PER_HOUR = 1000
to_send = 2500
hours = (to_send + LIMIT_PER_HOUR - 1) // LIMIT_PER_HOUR
print(f"Sending {to_send} messages at {LIMIT_PER_HOUR}/hour takes {hours} hours")

Section 42: Unsubscribe Links and Respect

Every marketing or bulk email should include a clear way for the recipient to stop receiving it. That link, usually at the bottom of the message, is called an unsubscribe link, and it is one of the most important features of a responsible email system. It matters legally in many places, where sending bulk mail without an unsubscribe option can break the law. It matters practically, because a user who cannot unsubscribe will mark your mail as spam instead, which damages your reputation far more than a single lost subscriber. And it matters ethically, because nobody should be forced to receive mail they do not want.

An unsubscribe link should be easy to find, easy to use, and immediate in effect. Easy to find means visible, not hidden in tiny grey text at the very bottom of a very long message, though the footer is the conventional place. Easy to use means one click, without requiring the user to log in, because a user who wants to leave may have forgotten their password, and a login wall turns a simple request into a frustrating one. Immediate means that once clicked, the user stops receiving that kind of mail. A system that says "you will stop receiving mail within ten days" is a system that will receive ten more days of complaints.

There is an important distinction between unsubscribing from one kind of mail and from all mail. A user who unsubscribes from a weekly summary may still want security alerts and password resets, and those should continue, because they protect the account. A good unsubscribe page therefore offers choices: stop this category, or stop everything except essential account mail. That choice respects the user's intent. A system that treats every unsubscribe as "delete the account" or "stop everything including security mail" is overreacting, and it can leave users unprotected.

Behind the link is a simple mechanism. The link contains a unique token for the user and the category, just like a confirmation link. When clicked, your server marks that preference as disabled in the database. Future sends check preferences before sending, as you learned earlier, so the user simply stops receiving that category. The mechanism is not complicated. What matters is that it works reliably, every time, without exception. An unsubscribe that silently fails is worse than no unsubscribe at all, because the user believes they opted out while the mail keeps coming.

Finally, treat unsubscribes as information, not as insults. If many people unsubscribe after a particular message, that message, or that frequency, was wrong. If unsubscribes spike after you start a new kind of mail, that kind of mail was not wanted. Listen to the signal, adjust your content and frequency, and your remaining subscribers will be people who genuinely want your mail. A smaller, willing audience is worth far more than a large, resentful one, both for deliverability and for the health of your application.

# An unsubscribe link marks a preference off; future sends check it first.
preferences = {"ada@example.com": {"weekly_summary": True}}

def unsubscribe(email, category):
    preferences[email][category] = False
    return f"{email} will no longer receive {category}"

print(unsubscribe("ada@example.com", "weekly_summary"))

Section 43: Storing Notification History

A notification history is a record of what your application told each user, and when. It is closely related to the in-app notifications table you met earlier, but it is broader: it covers email, push, and SMS as well as in-app messages, and it is kept for longer, because its purpose is accountability and debugging, not just display. When a user says "I never got that message", the history is where you look. When a support team asks what a user was told, the history answers. When you need to prove that a security alert was sent, the history proves it. Building it is a small amount of work with a large payoff.

The design is a table, much like the notifications table, with a row per sent message. Each row holds the user, the channel, the kind of message, the time it was sent, and its status: sent, delivered, bounced, or failed. It should not hold the full message body by default, especially if the body contains personal data, because a history table that copies every message becomes a large store of sensitive data. Instead, hold a reference to the kind of message and its key facts, such as a task id or an order id, so the message can be reconstructed if needed without storing a copy of everything.

History also needs a retention policy, like the cleanup policies you learned about. Keeping every notification forever is wasteful and risky: it grows the database and accumulates personal data with no purpose. A sensible policy might keep detailed history for ninety days, then summarise or delete it, while keeping security-related history, such as password change alerts, for longer, because those have accountability value. Whatever you choose, write it down, apply it automatically with a cleanup job, and be able to explain it. A policy you cannot explain is a policy you cannot defend.

There is a privacy angle here too. Notification history is personal data about a user: it reveals what your application told them, which can reveal what they did. A user who asks for their data, or asks for it to be deleted, is also asking about this history. Your system should be able to find a user's history, show it to them if appropriate, and delete it when required. Designing the history table with the user as a clear key makes all of that straightforward. A history scattered across logs, mail provider dashboards, and SMS records is much harder to manage, which is another reason to keep your own central record.

Finally, history makes your notification system measurable. How many messages did you send last month? Which channel is used most? Which kind of message bounces most often? Those answers come from the history table with simple queries, and they guide decisions about channels, content, and frequency. A notification system without history is flying blind. A notification system with history can improve itself, because it can see what it actually did, not just what it intended to do.

import sqlite3

# One row per sent notification: who, which channel, what kind, when, result.
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("""CREATE TABLE notification_history
              (user TEXT, channel TEXT, kind TEXT, sent_at TEXT, status TEXT)""")
db.execute("INSERT INTO notification_history VALUES ('ada', 'email', 'welcome', '2026-10-31', 'sent')")
rows = db.execute("SELECT * FROM notification_history WHERE user='ada'").fetchall()
print(rows)

Section 44: Webhooks as Notifications to Other Servers

So far, notifications have meant messages to people. But servers also notify each other, and the most common way they do it is called a webhook. A webhook is a message one server sends to another server when something happens, by making a web request to an address the other server provided. For example, when a payment succeeds, a payment service can send a webhook to your server saying "payment 123 succeeded". Your server receives that request, records the payment, and acts on it. Webhooks are notifications between machines, and they are everywhere in modern back-end work, even though users never see them.

The basic shape is simple. The receiving server publishes a special endpoint, a URL designed to accept incoming notifications. The sending server, when an event happens, makes a request, usually a POST with JSON, to that URL. The receiving server reads the event, checks that it is genuine, and acts on it. That check matters enormously, because anyone who knows the URL could send fake events. A common protection is a signature: the sender signs the message with a shared secret, and the receiver verifies the signature before trusting the event. Without that check, an attacker could fake a "payment succeeded" event and get goods for free. Signature verification is not optional decoration. It is the core security of webhooks.

Webhooks also have the same reliability concerns as other background work. The receiving server might be down when the event happens, so the sender should retry, with backoff, until it succeeds or gives up. The receiving server should handle duplicate deliveries, because a retry can deliver the same event twice, and processing it twice could double-charge or double-count. That is the idempotency lesson again, applied to incoming events: use the event's unique id to check whether it was already processed, and skip duplicates. Webhooks are a place where all the lessons of this chapter come together.

There is also a design question: should your server act on a webhook immediately, inside the request, or queue it as a job? The safe answer is usually to queue it. Accept the webhook quickly, return a success response, and process the event in the background. That way, a slow or failing process does not make the sender think delivery failed, which would cause unnecessary retries. It also means your webhook endpoint stays fast and reliable, even when the work behind it is slow. The pattern should feel familiar by now: answer fast, do the work in the background.

Finally, webhooks teach you to think about events as a language between systems. A well-designed webhook sends clear, versioned events with stable fields, so receivers can rely on them. A poorly designed one sends vague events that change without warning, breaking every receiver. If you ever build a service that sends webhooks, treat its events as a promise to other developers, and keep that promise carefully. Your own server will be on the receiving end of someone else's webhooks soon enough, and you will appreciate the difference.

import hmac, hashlib, json

# Verify a webhook signature before trusting the event.
SECRET = b"shared-secret"
payload = json.dumps({"event": "payment_succeeded", "id": "pay_123"}).encode()
signature = hmac.new(SECRET, payload, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
check = hmac.new(SECRET, payload, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
print("Signature valid:", hmac.compare_digest(signature, check))

Section 45: Keeping Secrets Out of Email

Email is a convenient channel, and convenience tempts developers to put things in email that should never be there. Passwords, secret keys, internal addresses, database credentials, and private personal data have all, at various times, been emailed by applications that should have known better. The rule is simple: email is not a secure channel, and it is not a private one. Messages can be forwarded, stored for years, read on shared screens, and kept in inboxes long after they are relevant. Anything you would not want a stranger to read should not be in an email.

The most common mistake is emailing a password. Sometimes this happens when an account is created: the system generates a password and emails it to the user. That feels helpful, but it means the password now lives in an inbox, possibly forever, and anyone with access to that inbox can use it. The better approach is to let the user choose their own password during signup, or to send a time-limited link that lets them set one. A link expires. A password in an inbox does not, unless the user changes it, which many will not. Never email a password, not even a temporary one, if a link can do the job instead.

The second mistake is emailing secrets meant for developers or systems: API keys, tokens, internal server addresses, or configuration details. These sometimes end up in emails through error reports or debug output that gets mailed to a team. Error emails are useful, but they must be scrubbed of secrets before sending. A stack trace that includes a database password is a leak, not a debugging aid. The habit to build is to treat every outgoing message, whether to a user or to your own team, as something that might be forwarded, stored, or seen by the wrong person, and to include only what is needed.

The third mistake is emailing more personal data than necessary. A notification that says "your appointment is tomorrow at ten" is useful. A notification that includes a full medical record, a home address, and a phone number is oversharing. The user already has that information in your application, behind a login. The email only needs to nudge them to look. Keep emails short, keep them free of sensitive detail, and link back to the application for anything private. That habit protects users if their inbox is ever compromised, because the damage is limited to what the emails themselves contain.

Finally, remember that email content is hard to take back. You cannot unsend a message that has already been delivered and read, and you cannot control where it is forwarded. That permanence is the reason for caution. Before any email goes out, ask: if this message were published publicly tomorrow, what would it reveal? If the answer is anything you would regret, remove it before sending. Caution at send time is cheap. Regret after sending is expensive.

# Safe: a time-limited link. Unsafe: the password itself.
print("Safe:   Set your password: https://taskday.example/set-password?token=abc123")
print("Unsafe: Your password is hunter2 (do NOT do this)")

Section 46: Personal Data in Notifications

Notifications often contain personal data, because they are about a person's life: their tasks, their orders, their messages, their appointments. That makes notification design a privacy design problem, not just a messaging problem. Every notification you send is a small disclosure of information about a user, and you should think about who might see it beyond the intended recipient. A push notification appears on a lock screen, visible to anyone glancing at the phone. An email preview appears in an inbox list, visible to anyone looking over a shoulder. A text message appears similarly. Designing notifications with that visibility in mind is part of respecting your users.

The first rule is to include only what the message needs. A push notification that says "You have a new message from Ada" is useful and discreet. A push notification that includes the full text of a private message is a disclosure waiting to happen, on a lock screen, in a meeting, or on a shared device. The user can open the app to read the full message, behind their lock screen and login. The notification's job is to tell them something happened, not to reproduce the private content. Short, vague-on-detail notifications are not lazy. They are protective.

The second rule is to respect the user's context and settings. Some users are comfortable with detailed previews. Others are not. Where possible, let users choose how much detail notifications show, especially on lock screens. And remember that notification preferences are themselves sensitive: the fact that a user receives a particular kind of notification can reveal something about them, such as a health condition or a financial situation. Do not expose a user's notification settings, or their notification history, to anyone but that user, and do not use notification data for purposes the user did not agree to, such as advertising.

The third rule is about data minimisation in storage and logs. Notification history, job payloads, and logs should hold the minimum data needed to do their job. A job payload that says "notify user 42 about task 99" is better than one that copies the task's full private content, because the payload will sit in the queue, in logs, and possibly in a dead letter queue, long after the notification is sent. Each copy is another place the data can leak. Minimising data in notifications is not just tidiness. It reduces the damage if any part of the system is compromised.

The fourth rule is about legal and ethical responsibility. Many places have laws about personal data, and notifications are squarely within them. You should know the basics of the rules that apply to your users, such as the need for consent, the right to access and delete data, and the duty to keep data secure. You do not need to become a lawyer, but you do need to design with these duties in mind, and to ask for expert advice when a feature handles especially sensitive data. A developer who thinks about privacy at design time builds systems that are easier to defend, easier to explain, and more trustworthy to users.

# Discreet notification: says something happened, not the private content.
push = {"title": "New message", "body": "You have a new message from Ada."}

Section 47: Monitoring the Mail Queue

A mail queue that nobody watches will eventually fail quietly, and users will notice before you do. Monitoring is the practice of watching your system's important numbers and alerting a human when they look wrong. For email, the numbers that matter are the queue depth, the age of the oldest job, the success rate, the bounce rate, and the complaint rate. Watching these five numbers gives you an early warning for almost every email problem, from a stopped worker to a damaged reputation, and it turns email from a hope into a managed system.

Queue depth and age, which you met earlier, tell you whether mail is flowing. A sudden rise in depth means workers are falling behind or have stopped. A rising age means users are waiting longer than usual. Both deserve an alert at a threshold you choose, such as more than a hundred jobs waiting, or any job older than an hour. The threshold matters less than the habit: pick a number, alert on it, and adjust it as you learn what normal looks like for your application. An alert that never fires is too loose. An alert that fires every day is too tight, and will be ignored, which is worse than no alert.

Success, bounce, and complaint rates tell you about quality. Success rate is the share of messages accepted for delivery. Bounce rate is the share that bounced, split into hard and soft. Complaint rate is the share marked as spam. A rising hard bounce rate means your address list is going stale. A rising complaint rate means your mail is unwanted, or your content has changed for the worse. Both are reputation warnings, and both should trigger a review before providers start filtering you. These rates are usually available from your mail provider's dashboard, and for larger systems they should be pulled into your own monitoring too, so they sit alongside your other numbers.

Monitoring also means checking the content of what you send, not just the counts. Occasionally, send yourself a copy of each kind of email your system produces, and check that it looks right, that links work, and that nothing embarrassing has crept in, such as a test value or a placeholder. Automated checks can help: a test that sends each template to a fake mailbox and checks for leftover placeholders catches a whole class of mistakes. Monitoring is not only about numbers. It is about regularly looking at what your system actually does, with fresh eyes.

Finally, monitoring needs an owner and a response. An alert that nobody acts on is noise. Decide who receives email alerts, what they should do first, and how quickly. Write that down, even briefly, so that a 3 a.m. alert does not become a puzzle. And review your alerts periodically: remove ones that never matter, tighten ones that matter too late, and add ones for problems you have actually experienced. Monitoring improves by learning from real incidents, not by guessing every possible problem in advance.

# Simple mail health check: alert when the numbers look wrong.
waiting = 250
oldest_age_minutes = 90
bounce_rate = 0.08
if waiting > 100 or oldest_age_minutes > 60 or bounce_rate > 0.05:
    print("ALERT: mail queue needs attention")
else:
    print("Mail queue healthy")

Section 48: A Small Notification System Design

You have now met all the pieces of a notification system. This section puts them together into a small, complete design that you could actually build, using only the tools from this chapter and earlier ones. The design is deliberately modest: it handles email and in-app notifications, respects preferences, uses a background queue, and stays honest about what it does not do. Modest designs are valuable, because they can be built, tested, and understood, and then grown. A grand design that never gets built helps nobody.

Start with the data. You need a users table, which you already have. You need a notifications table, with one row per in-app notification, as designed earlier. You need a notification history table, recording what was sent, on which channel, and with what result. You need a preferences table, linking users to categories and channels. And you need a jobs table, holding background work. Five tables, each with a clear job, each understandable on its own. That is the whole data model. Resist the temptation to add more tables before the system works, because every extra table is extra complexity you must maintain.

Next, the flow. When something happens in your application, such as a task being assigned, your code does one thing: it records the event. A small function, which you might call notify, takes the user, the event type, and the relevant facts, and does three things. First, it inserts an in-app notification row. Second, it checks the user's preferences for each channel. Third, for each enabled channel, it queues a job: a send_email job, or a push job, with the facts it needs. The function does not send anything itself. It records and queues, and returns quickly. That separation is the heart of the design, and it is why the system stays fast even when notifications are numerous.

Next, the workers. One worker process, or a small pool, reads the jobs table and handles each job by type. The email handler builds the message from a template and sends it, with retries and logging. The push handler sends the message through the platform, handling dead tokens. Failed jobs go to the dead letter queue after their retries. A scheduled job runs daily, building digests for users who chose them, and another runs weekly, cleaning up old notifications and history. That is the whole worker layer: handlers, retries, dead letters, and scheduled jobs, all patterns from this chapter.

Finally, the user-facing parts. A notifications page lists the user's in-app notifications, with unread counts and a mark-all-as-read button. A preferences page lets them choose categories, channels, and digest frequency, with a link from every email. And every email includes an unsubscribe link for its category. Those three pages, plus the tables and workers, are a complete notification system. It does not do SMS, it does not do complex targeting, and it does not need to. It does the common things well, and it can grow later, one channel at a time, without being rebuilt.

# The notify function: record in-app, check preferences, queue the rest.
def notify(user, event, facts, preferences):
    print(f"In-app notification recorded for {user}: {event}")
    for channel, enabled in preferences.items():
        if enabled:
            print(f"Queued {channel} job for {user}: {facts}")

notify("ben", "task_assigned", "task 12", {"email": True, "push": False})

Section 49: Common Mistakes with Email and Jobs

Every topic has its classic mistakes, the ones that beginners make again and again, and email and background jobs are no exception. This section collects the most common ones, not to embarrass anyone, but because recognising a mistake in a list is much cheaper than making it in production. Read through them, check your own designs against them, and you will avoid a large share of the trouble that these systems can cause.

The first mistake is sending email directly inside a request. It feels natural: the user signed up, so send the welcome email right there. But it makes requests slow, it turns mail failures into user-facing errors, and it scales badly. The fix, by now familiar, is to queue the email and send it in the background. If you remember only one lesson from this chapter, this should be a strong candidate, because it affects nearly every feature that sends mail.

The second mistake is putting all recipients in one message. It leaks addresses, it looks unprofessional, and it can break privacy rules. The fix is one message per person, or a mail service's bulk feature. The third mistake is forgetting the unsubscribe link, or making it hard to use. The fix is a visible, one-click unsubscribe for every bulk category. The fourth mistake is hard-coding credentials in source code. The fix is environment variables, which you will study in a later chapter, and never committing secrets to a code repository.

The fifth mistake is retrying everything forever, or never retrying at all. Both are wrong. Temporary failures deserve patient retries with backoff and a limit. Permanent failures deserve to be recorded and moved aside. The sixth mistake is swallowing errors silently: a worker that catches every exception and logs nothing is a worker that fails invisibly. Log every outcome, and alert on repeated failures. The seventh mistake is testing email with real users, or with production settings in development. Use a fake mailbox, separate settings, and separate credentials, and make it impossible for development to reach real inboxes.

The eighth mistake is ignoring bounces, complaints, and queue growth. These are your early warning system, and ignoring them is like ignoring a smoke alarm. Check them regularly, act on them promptly, and set alerts. The ninth mistake is making jobs too large or too vague: a job that does everything for a user is hard to retry, hard to test, and hard to understand when it fails. Keep jobs small, specific, and idempotent where possible. The tenth mistake is sending notifications nobody asked for, on every channel, about everything. Respect preferences, choose channels deliberately, and remember that silence is sometimes the right notification.

None of these mistakes comes from bad intentions. They come from rushing, from copying an example without understanding it, or from building a feature and moving on before its failure modes are considered. The antidote is the habit this chapter has tried to build: think about what happens when things go wrong, design for that from the start, and check your system regularly. A developer who does that will still make mistakes, but they will be new and interesting ones, not the classics listed here.

for m in mistakes:
    print("-", m)

Section 50: Putting Email, Notifications and Jobs Together

This chapter began with a simple observation: servers send email. It ends with a bigger picture: email, notifications, and background jobs are not three separate features. They are one system for communicating with users and doing work on their behalf, reliably, at the right time, through the right channel. A welcome email, a push notification, a weekly digest, and a nightly cleanup are all expressions of the same ideas: record the work, queue it, do it in the background, handle failures gracefully, and respect the user. Seeing that unity is what turns a collection of techniques into a way of thinking.

Let us trace one complete story through the whole system. A user, Ada, assigns a task to Ben, with a deadline of Friday. The request records the assignment and returns quickly. The notify function inserts an in-app notification for Ben, checks his preferences, and queues an email job and, because Ben allows it, a push job. Workers send both, logging each outcome. A reminder job is also queued, scheduled for Thursday, one day before the deadline. On Thursday, the worker sends the reminder. On Friday, Ben completes the task, and Ada gets a notification that it is done. Over the weekend, a cleanup job removes old notifications. Every step used a pattern from this chapter, and no step blocked a user, spammed anyone, or lost a message silently.

Notice what the user experienced in that story: timely, relevant messages, through channels they chose, with no duplicates, no delays they noticed, and no noise. That experience is the goal. The machinery behind it, queues, workers, retries, priorities, dead letters, preferences, and logs, exists to produce that experience reliably, at scale, and in the face of failures. When you build notification features, judge them by that experience, not by how clever the machinery is. Simple machinery that produces a good experience beats clever machinery that confuses users.

There are things this chapter did not cover, and you should know their names so you can learn them next. Dedicated queue services, which replace the database table at larger scale, are covered in the chapter on queues and workers. Real-time notifications, where messages appear instantly without refreshing, are covered in the chapter on WebSockets. Advanced deliverability, including dedicated sending domains and reputation management at scale, is a deep topic of its own. You do not need those yet. The foundation you have now, a database queue, a worker, templates, preferences, and honest logging, will carry you a long way, and will make those advanced topics much easier when you reach them.

Your next step is practice. Build the small notification system from section 48, with a real database, a real worker script, and a fake mailbox. Send yourself welcome emails, assign tasks between test users, watch jobs move from pending to done, break the mail server on purpose, and watch retries and dead letters do their work. There is no substitute for watching a system you built handle failure gracefully. When you can do that, you have understood this chapter, not just read it. And that understanding will serve every back-end feature you build from here on, because nearly all of them, sooner or later, need to tell someone something, or do something later, without making a user wait.
