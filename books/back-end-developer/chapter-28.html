Chapter 28: Logging and Monitoring a Server

Section 1: Why Servers Need to Remember What Happened

A server runs day and night, handling requests while nobody watches it. When something goes wrong at three in the morning, there is no one standing beside it to see what happened. The user who reports a problem the next day can only say what they saw: a page failed, an email never arrived, a task disappeared. They cannot say why. That is why servers need to remember what happened, in the form of logs and measurements. Without a memory, debugging is guesswork. With one, it is investigation: you can look at what the server recorded, follow the trail, and find the cause.

Think of it like a shopkeeper's notebook. A good shopkeeper writes down what was sold, what was returned, and what went wrong, because without notes, they cannot answer questions later: why is stock missing, why did a customer complain, why was Tuesday slow? A server's logs are that notebook, kept automatically, at a scale no human could match. Every request, every error, every slow operation can be recorded, and those records become the evidence you use when something breaks. A server without logs is a shop without a notebook: it may run fine for a while, but the first serious problem becomes a mystery.

Memory also matters for questions that are not about failures. How many users visited yesterday? Which pages are slowest? Which features are used most? Did the new release make things faster or slower? Those questions are answered by the same records: logs and metrics. A team that keeps good records can make decisions based on evidence, such as which feature to improve, or whether a change helped. A team without records makes decisions based on opinions and anecdotes, which are often wrong. Logging and monitoring are therefore not just debugging tools. They are the foundation of running a service responsibly.

There is another reason servers need memory: accountability. When a security incident happens, or a user disputes a charge, or a regulator asks what happened, the records are the answer. A log showing that a password was changed from a particular address at a particular time settles questions that arguments cannot. A record showing that a notification was sent proves the system did its job. Records do not just help developers. They protect users, by making the system's behaviour checkable, and they protect developers, by showing what actually happened rather than what someone remembers happening.

Finally, remember that a server's memory is only useful if someone can read it. Logs dumped into a file nobody ever opens are barely better than no logs. Measurements nobody looks at are decoration. The habit this chapter builds is not just to record, but to record well, to keep records findable, and to look at them regularly, not only during crises. A server that remembers, and a team that reads what it remembers, is a server that can be trusted to keep running, and to get better over time.

# A server's memory starts with a simple record of what happened.
events = ["request received", "task created", "email queued", "response sent"]
for event in events:
    print("Logged:", event)

Section 2: What Is a Log?

A log is a record of events, written as they happen, usually as lines of text, each describing one thing that occurred. A web server's log might record that a request arrived, what page was asked for, how long the response took, and whether it succeeded. An application's log might record that a user signed up, that an email was queued, or that a database query failed. Each line is small, but together, thousands of lines tell the story of what your system did, in order, over time. That story is what you read when you need to understand behaviour, diagnose a problem, or prove what happened.

A typical log line has a few standard parts. It has a timestamp, saying when the event happened. It has a level, saying how important the event is, such as information, warning, or error. It has a source, saying which part of the system wrote it. And it has a message, describing the event, often with useful details, such as a user id, a request path, or an error description. Those parts are not decoration. The timestamp lets you order events and correlate them with user reports. The level lets you filter for what matters. The source tells you where to look. The message tells you what happened. A log line missing its timestamp is nearly useless, because you cannot place it in time, and a log line with a vague message is nearly useless, because you cannot tell what actually occurred.

Logs are written to different places depending on the system. In development, they often go to the terminal, where you can watch them live. In production, they usually go to files on the server, or to a central service that collects logs from many servers in one place. Files are simple and work well for a single server. A central service is better for many servers, because it lets you search across all of them at once. The destination matters less than the habit: logs must go somewhere durable, somewhere you can find them later, not just to a terminal window that closes when you log out.

Logs are also different from other kinds of output. A print statement used for debugging during development is a kind of log, but it is usually temporary, unstructured, and removed before release. A proper log is permanent, structured enough to search, and kept in production, because production is where the interesting events happen: real users, real data, real failures. Debugging prints help you while you build. Logs help you while the system runs, which is most of its life. Treating logs as a first-class part of your application, not as leftover debugging, is a mark of a professional developer.

One more distinction matters: logs record events, not just errors. Beginners often log only when something goes wrong, which means their logs are silent during normal operation and cryptic during failures, because there is no context around the error. Good logging records the normal flow too: requests, signups, jobs started and finished. Then, when an error occurs, the surrounding lines show what led up to it, which is often the key to understanding it.

import logging

# A proper log line: timestamp, level, source, message.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO,
                    format="%(asctime)s %(levelname)s %(name)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday")
log.info("User ada created task 12")

Section 3: Logs, Metrics and Traces in Plain Words

Logging and monitoring involve three kinds of information, often called the three pillars of observability, a grand term for a simple idea: being able to understand what your system is doing from the outside. The three pillars are logs, metrics, and traces. They complement each other, and a mature system uses all three, but they answer different questions, and confusing them leads to using the wrong tool for a job. This section draws the lines clearly, so you can reach for the right one each time.

Logs, which you met in the previous section, are records of individual events. They answer the question "what happened, exactly?" A log line says a specific request failed with a specific error at a specific time. Logs are detailed and specific, which makes them perfect for diagnosing a particular incident, but poor for seeing trends, because reading thousands of lines to spot a pattern is slow. Logs are the microscope: excellent for examining one thing closely, impractical for surveying a landscape.

Metrics are numbers measured over time. They answer the question "how is the system doing, overall?" Examples include requests per second, average response time, error rate, CPU usage, and queue depth. Metrics are usually shown as graphs, so you can see trends: response times rising over the week, errors spiking after a deployment, traffic growing each month. Metrics are the dashboard: excellent for spotting that something is wrong and when it changed, but they do not say why. A graph showing an error spike tells you when the problem started, but you need logs to find which error, and which users were affected.

Traces follow a single request as it travels through your system, especially when the system has several parts, such as a web server, a database, and an outside service. A trace records each step the request took, how long each step lasted, and where time was spent. Traces answer the question "where did this request spend its time, and where did it fail?" If a request took ten seconds, a trace shows whether the database, the outside service, or your own code was responsible. Traces are the journey map: they show the path and the delays along it, which is invaluable when a slow request involves several systems.

The three pillars work together in a natural flow. A metric alerts you that error rates rose at 2 p.m. A trace shows that the failing requests all spent their time waiting on one database query. Logs show the exact error that query produced, and which users were affected. Each pillar narrows the search. A system with only logs can diagnose, but slowly. A system with only metrics can notice, but not explain. A system with all three can notice, explain, and fix, which is the goal.

For a small application, you do not need elaborate tools for all three. Logs from Python's logging module, plus a few metrics such as request counts and response times, will take you a long way. Traces become important as systems grow and split into parts. The key lesson is to understand what each kind of information is for, and to make sure you can answer all three questions: what happened, how is the system doing, and where did this request spend its time?

# The three pillars answer three different questions.
pillars = {"logs": "what happened, exactly?",
           "metrics": "how is the system doing overall?",
           "traces": "where did this request spend its time?"}
for name, question in pillars.items():
    print(name, "->", question)

Section 4: Log Levels in Plain Words

Not every event deserves the same attention. A routine request is not as important as a failed payment, and a failed payment is not as important as a server running out of disk space. Log levels are a way to rank events by importance, so that you can filter logs to the level you need: show me only errors, or show me everything, depending on the situation. Levels are a simple idea, but using them consistently is a discipline that makes logs vastly more useful, because it turns a flood of lines into a filterable record.

The standard levels, from least to most severe, are DEBUG, INFO, WARNING, ERROR, and CRITICAL, with Python's logging module providing all five. DEBUG is for detailed information useful while developing: variable values, step-by-step flow, the kind of detail you need when hunting a bug but never want in production. INFO is for normal, expected events: a server started, a user signed up, a job finished. INFO is the everyday narrative of your system, and it should be readable as a story of what happened. WARNING is for something unexpected that did not break anything, but might indicate a problem: a retry after a temporary failure, a deprecated feature being used, a disk getting full. Warnings deserve attention, but not panic.

ERROR is for something that failed and needs fixing: a request that returned an error, a job that failed permanently, a database connection that dropped. Errors are the lines you investigate, and they should contain enough detail to act on: what failed, for whom, and why. CRITICAL is for something that threatens the whole system: the database is unreachable, the disk is full, the application cannot start. Critical events usually mean the service is down or about to be, and they should trigger an alert to a human immediately. The difference between ERROR and CRITICAL is scope: an error affects one request or job, while a critical event affects the system itself.

Choosing levels correctly matters, because filtering depends on them. If everything is logged as ERROR, then errors lose meaning, and real problems hide in a flood of routine messages. That is called crying wolf, and it is a common beginner mistake: logging every minor issue as an error because it felt important at the time. If real errors are logged as INFO, they hide in the everyday narrative, and nobody notices them until a user complains. The discipline is to ask, for each event, "if I saw a thousand of these, would I want to be alerted?" If yes, it is an error or critical. If no, but it is still worth noting, it is a warning or info. If it is only useful while debugging, it is debug.

Levels also control volume. In production, you typically show INFO and above, hiding DEBUG, because debug lines are numerous and only useful during development. In development, you show DEBUG, because you need the detail. That single setting, the minimum level displayed, lets the same code produce a detailed log while developing and a manageable log in production, without changing the logging calls. It is a simple, powerful mechanism, and it is why levels are worth using from the very first log line you write.

import logging

# Levels rank importance: debug for development, critical for system danger.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(levelname)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday")
log.debug("Detailed step (hidden at INFO level)")
log.info("User ada signed up")
log.warning("Mail server was slow, retried")
log.error("Failed to send email to ben@example.com")

Section 5: What Makes a Good Log Line

A good log line is one that lets a future reader, often yourself, weeks later, at an awkward hour, understand what happened and what to do next, without needing to read the code. That is a high standard, and most log lines fail it, because they were written quickly, in the moment, with context the writer had but the reader does not. Learning to write good log lines is a craft, and it pays off every time you debug, because the difference between a good log line and a bad one is the difference between fixing a problem in minutes and spending hours guessing.

The first rule is to be specific. "Error occurred" is a bad log line. It says nothing about what failed, for whom, or why. "Failed to send welcome email to ada@example.com: connection refused by smtp.example.com" is a good log line. It names the action, the affected user, and the reason, so the reader knows immediately whether the problem is in their code, in the mail server, or in the address. Specificity means including the key facts: who, what, where, and why, in plain words, without jargon that only the writer understands. If a log line requires you to look up what it means, it has failed.

The second rule is to include identifiers. A log line about a request should include a request id, or at least the user and the path. A log line about a job should include the job id and type. A log line about a database error should include the query or the table, if safe to do so. Identifiers let you connect related lines together: all lines for one request, all lines for one job, all errors for one user. Without identifiers, logs are a pile of disconnected statements. With them, logs become a story you can follow, which is exactly what you need during an incident.

The third rule is to say what happened, not just that something happened, and to say it in a consistent style. Use the same wording for the same event everywhere, so that searching works: if one part of the code logs "email sent" and another logs "mail delivered", searching for either will miss half the events. Pick one phrasing and use it everywhere. Consistency also applies to format: if most lines put the user first, do that everywhere, so the eye can scan quickly. A consistent log is a searchable log, and a searchable log is a useful log.

The fourth rule is to avoid noise. Do not log the same routine event at a high level on every request, because it will drown the important lines. Do not log huge data structures, because they make lines unreadable and fill storage. Do not log the same error repeatedly in a tight loop without limiting it, because a thousand identical lines hide the one line that is different. Good logging is selective: it records what a future reader needs, and it leaves out what they do not. That selectivity is a judgement that improves with experience, and with reading your own logs after incidents and noticing what was missing, or what was in the way.

# Bad: vague. Good: specific, with identifiers and a reason.
print("Bad:  Error occurred")
print("Good: Failed to send welcome email to ada@example.com: "
      "connection refused by smtp.example.com (job 482)")

Section 6: Structured Logging: Logs as Data

Traditional logs are lines of human-readable text, designed to be read by a person. Structured logs are records designed to be read by both people and machines, usually written as JSON, where each field is a separate, named piece of data: timestamp, level, message, user id, request id, duration, and so on. The difference matters because as systems grow, logs are searched, filtered, and analysed by tools, not just read by eye, and a structured format makes those tools far more powerful. A text line requires a tool to guess where the user id is. A JSON record tells it exactly.

Consider a question you might ask during an incident: show me all errors for user Ada in the last hour. With text logs, a tool must search for the word "error" and the string "Ada", hoping both appear in the same line, in a consistent format, which they may not. With structured logs, the tool filters on level equals error and user equals Ada, precisely and reliably, regardless of how the message is worded. That precision is the main benefit of structured logging. It turns logs from text to be searched into data to be queried, which is a much stronger foundation for debugging at scale.

Structured logging also makes aggregation easy. Want to know the average response time per endpoint? With structured logs that include endpoint and duration fields, a tool can compute it directly. Want to count errors by type? Group by the error field. Want to find the slowest requests? Sort by duration. Those analyses are painful with text logs, because they require parsing each line and hoping the format is consistent. With structured logs, they are simple queries. That is why larger systems adopt structured logging early: it pays off as soon as you start asking questions that span many log lines, rather than reading one line at a time.

For a small application, structured logging can be as simple as logging JSON instead of text, using Python's json module to format each record. You do not need a special library to start. The key is consistency: every record should have the same core fields, with the same names, so that tools can rely on them. Extra fields, specific to an event, can be added freely, but the core fields, timestamp, level, message, and identifiers, should never vary. That consistency is what makes structured logs useful, and it is a discipline worth building from the first structured log you write.

There is a trade-off: structured logs are harder for a human to read at a glance than a well-written text line. A JSON record is verbose and ugly compared to a sentence. That is why many teams use text logs in development, where a human reads them live, and structured logs in production, where tools analyse them. Or they use tools that render structured logs as readable lines for humans while keeping the data for machines. Either way, the lesson is to choose the format for the audience: humans while developing, machines at scale, and to keep the core fields consistent in both. A log format that serves its audience is a log format that gets used.

import json, logging

# Structured log: fields a tool can filter and aggregate reliably.
record = {"level": "error", "message": "Failed to send email",
          "user": "ada@example.com", "job_id": 482,
          "reason": "connection refused"}
print(json.dumps(record))

Section 7: Logging in Python with the logging Module

Python's standard library includes a logging module, which is the right tool for logging in Python applications, and it is worth learning properly, because it solves problems that print statements do not. Print statements go to one place, the terminal, with no levels, no timestamps, and no control. The logging module gives you levels, so you can filter; formatters, so you can control what each line looks like; handlers, so you can send logs to the terminal, to files, or to other destinations, all at once; and loggers, so different parts of your application can log under different names. Those features are the difference between debugging output and a logging system.

The basic setup is simple. You get a logger for your application, usually named after your module, and you call its methods: debug, info, warning, error, and critical. You configure the root logger once, at startup, with a level and a format, and optionally with handlers for files. After that, every log call in your code produces a properly formatted line, with a timestamp and a level, in every destination you configured. That central configuration is a major advantage: changing the log level, or adding a file handler, is a one-line change at startup, not a change to every logging call in your code.

Loggers form a hierarchy based on their names, separated by dots. A logger named "taskday.email" is a child of "taskday", and it inherits configuration from its parent unless overridden. That hierarchy lets you control logging per part of your application: you can set the email module to DEBUG while the rest stays at INFO, which is useful when debugging one feature without drowning in detail from others. It also means you should name loggers after your modules, not use a single global logger for everything, because per-module control is one of the module's most useful features, and it costs nothing to adopt.

Handlers and formatters deserve a brief explanation, because they are the parts beginners often miss. A handler is a destination: the console, a file, or a network service. You can attach several handlers to a logger, so the same log line goes to both the terminal and a file, for example. A formatter controls how a line looks: which fields appear, in what order, and in what format. A development formatter might be human-readable text. A production formatter might be JSON. Separating the destination and the format from the logging calls means you can change where logs go, and how they look, without touching the code that produces them. That separation is good design, and it is built into the module.

Finally, a warning about a common mistake: do not use the root logger directly in library code, and do not call basicConfig in multiple places, because the first call wins and later calls are ignored, which confuses beginners. Configure logging once, at your application's entry point, and use named loggers everywhere else. And never log with string formatting that builds the message before checking the level, because that wastes work for disabled levels. Use the logging module's built-in formatting, passing arguments separately, so the message is only built if it will actually be logged. Those small habits make the difference between logging that helps and logging that hinders.

import logging

# Configure once at startup; use named loggers everywhere else.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO,
                    format="%(asctime)s %(levelname)s %(name)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday.email")
log.info("Email job 482 queued for ada@example.com")
log.warning("Mail server slow: retrying in 30s")

Section 8: Logging to Files

In production, logs usually go to files, because files persist after the terminal closes, they can be searched and archived, and they survive restarts. Logging to a file in Python is straightforward: you add a file handler to your logger, pointing at a path, and log lines are written there as well as, or instead of, to the terminal. That simplicity hides a few decisions that matter, and this section covers them, because file logging done badly can cause problems of its own, from filling disks to losing logs at the worst moment.

The first decision is where the file lives. It should be in a directory your application can write to, with enough disk space, and it should be a place your team knows to look. A log file buried in a temporary directory, or in a directory that is wiped on restart, is a log file that will not be there when you need it. A standard location, such as a logs directory next to your application, or a system log directory, is better, because everyone knows where to look, and because backup and monitoring tools can be pointed at it. Document the location, so a new team member can find logs without asking.

The second decision is what goes in the file versus the terminal. A common pattern is to log INFO and above to a file, for the permanent record, and to log everything, including DEBUG, to the terminal in development, where a human is watching. In production, the terminal may not exist, or may not be saved, so the file becomes the primary record, and it should contain enough detail to debug, which usually means INFO and above, with ERROR and CRITICAL always included. The file is the record you will read during an incident, so it should be complete enough to reconstruct what happened, without being so verbose that it is unusable.

The third decision is about buffering and flushing. For performance, file writes are often buffered, meaning lines are held in memory and written in batches, rather than one at a time. Buffering is faster, but it means that if the application crashes, the most recent lines, which are often the most important ones, describing the crash, may be lost, because they were still in the buffer. For critical logs, flushing after each line, or using unbuffered writes, ensures lines are saved immediately, at a small performance cost. That trade, speed versus durability, should be made deliberately: for most applications, flushing error and critical lines immediately is worth the cost, while info lines can be buffered.

The fourth decision is about permissions and safety. Log files can contain sensitive information, even if you try not to log secrets, because they record user activity, addresses, and behaviour. Log files should therefore have restricted permissions, readable only by those who need them, and they should not be served by your web server or included in public files. A log file accidentally exposed on the web is a serious incident, and it happens more often than it should, usually because logs were written into a web-accessible directory. Keep logs outside the web root, with tight permissions, and treat them as sensitive data, because they are.

import logging

# Log to a file for the permanent record, as well as the terminal.
handler = logging.FileHandler("taskday.log")
handler.setFormatter(logging.Formatter(
    "%(asctime)s %(levelname)s %(name)s: %(message)s"))
log = logging.getLogger("taskday")
log.addHandler(handler)
log.info("Server started, logging to taskday.log")

Section 9: Log Rotation: Keeping Logs from Filling the Disk

A server that logs continuously will eventually fill its disk, if logs are never removed. That sounds obvious, but it is a common cause of outages, because it happens slowly, over weeks or months, and then suddenly the disk is full, the application cannot write, the database cannot grow, and everything fails at once. Log rotation is the solution: the practice of regularly archiving old logs and deleting very old ones, so that log storage stays bounded. Rotation is a maintenance habit, not a feature, and it is essential for any server that runs for more than a few days.

Rotation works like this. Logs are written to a current file, such as app.log. At regular intervals, usually daily, or when the file reaches a size limit, the current file is renamed, for example to app.log.1, and a new, empty app.log is started. Older files are renamed in turn, app.log.1 becomes app.log.2, and so on, up to a limit, such as seven or thirty files. Files older than the limit are deleted, or compressed and archived elsewhere. That cycle keeps a window of recent logs, usually days or weeks, available for debugging, while preventing unbounded growth. The exact window depends on your needs and disk space, but the principle is the same: keep recent logs, discard old ones, automatically.

Python's logging module includes handlers that do rotation for you. A RotatingFileHandler rotates when the file reaches a size limit, keeping a set number of backups. A TimedRotatingFileHandler rotates on a schedule, such as daily or weekly, which is often more useful, because it aligns log files with calendar days, making it easy to find "yesterday's logs". Both handlers handle the renaming and deletion automatically, so your application code does not need to manage files itself. Using a built-in handler is better than writing your own rotation, because the built-ins handle edge cases, such as rotation during a write, correctly.

There are also system-level tools for rotation, which are common on servers, and which can rotate logs for any application, not just Python. Those tools read a configuration file listing log paths and rotation rules, and they run on a schedule. Whether you use a Python handler or a system tool, the important thing is that rotation is configured and verified, not just intended. Check that rotation actually happens: look at the log directory after a day, confirm old files are renamed and very old ones are gone, and confirm the application keeps logging to the new file without a restart. A rotation setup that has never been tested is a setup that may fail silently.

Finally, think about what to do with rotated logs. Recent logs stay on the server, for quick debugging. Older logs with long-term value, such as security or audit logs, should be archived to cheaper storage before deletion, because once deleted, they are gone. Logs with no long-term value can simply be deleted. Write the policy down, configure rotation to match it, and review it periodically, because disks fill up when policies are forgotten.

import logging
from logging.handlers import RotatingFileHandler

# Rotate at 10 MB, keeping 5 backups: storage stays bounded.
handler = RotatingFileHandler("taskday.log", maxBytes=10_000_000, backupCount=5)
log = logging.getLogger("taskday")
log.addHandler(handler)
log.info("Logging with rotation: old files are archived, not kept forever")

Section 10: What Not to Log: Secrets and Personal Data

Logs are powerful, and that power makes them dangerous if they contain the wrong things. The rule is simple to state and requires discipline to follow: never log secrets, and minimise personal data in logs. Secrets include passwords, API keys, tokens, session identifiers, and private keys. Personal data includes full names, addresses, phone numbers, financial details, and health information, beyond what is strictly necessary for debugging. A log file containing those is a leak waiting to happen, because logs are copied, stored, backed up, and shared more casually than databases, and because they are often readable by more people than the data deserves.

The most common way secrets end up in logs is by accident, through logging a whole object or a whole request. A developer debugging an authentication problem logs the entire request, including the password field. A developer debugging an API call logs the entire response, including a token. A developer logs an exception, and the exception's message includes a database connection string with a password. None of these are malicious. They are shortcuts taken under pressure, and they all create a permanent record of a secret in a place that was never designed to protect it. The fix is to log selectively: log the fields you need, by name, not the whole object, and to review log lines for sensitive fields before they reach production.

Personal data in logs deserves similar care, for both ethical and legal reasons. Many places have laws about personal data, and logs are within their scope. A log that records every user's full activity, with names and addresses, is a store of personal data that must be protected, and that a user might ask to have deleted, which is hard if it is scattered across many log files. Minimising personal data in logs, by using user ids instead of names, and by omitting fields that are not needed for debugging, reduces both the risk and the burden. When personal data is genuinely needed in a log, such as an email address to debug delivery, include only that field, not a full profile.

There are practical techniques that help. One is to redact sensitive fields automatically, replacing passwords and tokens with stars before logging. Some logging setups do this with filters that scan records for known sensitive field names. Another is to use allow-lists: define, for each kind of event, exactly which fields may be logged, and log only those. Both require effort, but they are far cheaper than dealing with a leak. Combine them with a habit: before merging code that adds a log line, ask whether it contains anything you would not want in a public report.

Finally, treat log files as sensitive data in their own right. Restrict who can read them, do not include them in less-protected backups, and do not post them publicly when asking for help without scrubbing them first. A developer who pastes a log into a public forum without checking for secrets has published those secrets. Write logs as if someone you do not fully trust might read them, because eventually, someone will.

# Log the fields you need by name, never a whole request with secrets.
safe_record = {"user_id": 42, "action": "login", "result": "success"}
print("Safe to log:", safe_record)
print("Never log: password, token, or full request body")

Section 11: Logging Requests: Who Asked for What

One of the most useful logs a web server keeps is the request log: a line for each request, recording who asked, what they asked for, and what happened. Request logs are the foundation of understanding traffic, diagnosing problems, and answering questions like "was that page requested at that time?" and "how slow was that endpoint yesterday?" They are also, in many setups, produced automatically by the web server, so a developer gets them without writing code, which makes them an excellent starting point for learning to read logs.

A typical request log line includes the time, the client's address, the method and path, the status code, the response size, and the time taken. For example, a line might say that at a certain time, an address requested a page with GET, received status 200, a response of a certain size, in a certain number of milliseconds. That single line answers many questions. The status code tells you whether the request succeeded or failed. The time taken tells you whether it was slow. The path tells you which part of your application was involved. And the address, while not a reliable identifier for a person, tells you roughly where the request came from, which can help spot patterns, such as many requests from one address in a short time.

Request logs become more useful when you add a few fields of your own. A user id, when the user is logged in, connects requests to people, which helps support and debugging. A request id, covered in a later section, connects a request's log lines across different parts of the system. The endpoint name, as opposed to the raw path, groups similar requests, such as all requests for a user profile regardless of which user, which makes aggregation meaningful. Without grouping, a log of a thousand different user profile URLs looks like a thousand different endpoints, when it is really one endpoint requested a thousand times. Grouping by endpoint pattern is essential for measuring performance per feature.

Request logs also need care around privacy. A path can contain personal data, if your URLs include names, email addresses, or other identifiers as parameters. A query string can contain tokens or search terms that are private. Logging full URLs without thought can therefore leak personal data into logs, which violates the principles of the previous section. The safe practice is to log the endpoint pattern, not the raw path with identifiers, and to avoid logging query strings that contain sensitive data, or to redact known sensitive parameters. Request logs should tell you what kind of request happened, not expose the private details of every request.

Finally, use request logs actively, not just during incidents. Review them regularly: which endpoints are slowest, which return the most errors, which are requested most often? Those answers guide optimisation, bug fixing, and capacity planning. A weekly glance at request log summaries, even a simple one, will surface problems before users report them, such as an endpoint that gradually slowed down, or an error rate that crept up after a change. Request logs are not just a record for emergencies. They are a regular source of insight into how your application is actually used, which is information no amount of guessing can replace.

Section 12: Logging Errors with Context

When something fails, the error log is the first place you look, and its quality determines how fast you can fix the problem. An error log without context is a dead end: it tells you something failed, but not what was happening, for whom, or why. An error log with context tells a story: this request, for this user, doing this action, failed at this step, for this reason, with this data. That story is what turns debugging from guessing into investigation, and it is built by including context deliberately, at the point where the error is logged, not as an afterthought.

The context that matters most depends on the error, but a few pieces are almost always useful. The request id, so you can find all lines for that request. The user id, so you know who was affected and can contact them or check their data. The action being attempted, so you know what the user was trying to do. The error type and message, so you know what went wrong in the system's own terms. And the relevant identifiers, such as a task id, an order id, or a job id, so you can inspect the specific data involved. Together, those pieces let a reader reconstruct the failure without needing to reproduce it, which is often impossible, because the data or the conditions have changed.

A common mistake is to log an error at every level as it bubbles up, producing many duplicate lines for one failure. A database error might be logged by the database layer, then again by the service layer, then again by the request handler. That duplication clutters logs and makes it hard to count real failures, because one incident looks like three. The better practice is to log an error once, at the point where it is handled, with full context, and to let it propagate silently through the layers in between.

Another mistake is to log an error without its cause. In Python, when you catch an exception, logging with exc_info=True, or using logger.exception, includes the full traceback: the chain of calls that led to the error, with file names and line numbers. That traceback is invaluable, because it shows exactly where the failure occurred, not just that it occurred. A log line that says "database error" without a traceback forces you to guess which query, in which function, failed. A log line with a traceback points you to the exact line. Always include the traceback for unexpected errors. For expected errors, such as a user entering a wrong password, a traceback is noise, and a simple line with context is enough.

Finally, think about what happens after the error is logged. An error log is not just a record. It should trigger the right response: an alert if the error is severe or frequent, a retry if the failure is temporary, or a user-facing message that is honest but not technical. Logging an error and then doing nothing, when the error affects users, is a failure of the system, not just of the code. Errors deserve a response proportionate to their impact, and the log is the start of that response, not the end of it.

import logging

# Log errors with context and a traceback, once, where handled.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(levelname)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday")
try:
    raise ConnectionError("database unreachable")
except ConnectionError:
    log.exception("Failed to load tasks for user 42, request abc123")

Section 13: Logging Slow Requests

Slow requests are a special kind of problem, because they do not fail outright, so they do not produce errors, yet they harm users just as surely as failures do. A page that takes ten seconds feels broken, even if it eventually loads, and users who experience slow pages leave, just as users who experience errors do. Logging slow requests is how you find them, because without a record, slowness is invisible: users complain vaguely that "the site is slow", but nobody knows which pages, for whom, or when. A slow-request log turns a vague complaint into specific, actionable data.

The mechanism is simple: for each request, measure how long it took, and if it exceeded a threshold, log it with extra detail. The threshold should be chosen based on what your users tolerate: for a web page, a second might be slow; for a report generation, ten seconds might be normal. A common approach is to log all requests at INFO level with their duration, and to log requests over a threshold at WARNING level, so they stand out. That way, you have a complete record for analysis, and a highlighted subset for immediate attention. The threshold is not a fixed rule. It is a setting to tune as you learn what normal looks like for each endpoint.

What detail should a slow-request log include? The endpoint, the duration, and the breakdown of where time was spent, if available: how long in the database, how long calling outside services, how long in your own code. That breakdown is what turns "this page is slow" into "this page is slow because of this query", which is a fixable problem. Without a breakdown, you know a page is slow but not why, and you must guess. Even a rough breakdown, such as the number of database queries and the total database time, is enormously helpful. It is worth adding that instrumentation early, because it pays off every time a slow request is investigated.

Slow-request logs also reveal patterns over time. A page that is slow only at certain hours might be affected by a scheduled job, or by traffic peaks. A page that gradually slows over weeks might be affected by growing data, such as a table that is not indexed, or a list that grows without pagination. A page that suddenly slows after a deployment points to a change in that deployment. Those patterns are visible in slow logs over time, and they are invisible in a single complaint. Reviewing slow logs regularly, even briefly, catches degradation early, before it becomes a crisis.

Finally, slow-request logging connects to monitoring, which you will learn about later in this chapter. A metric for response times, graphed over time, shows the same trends as slow logs, but at a glance, while slow logs provide the detail for individual cases. Together, they give you both the overview and the specifics. Start with slow logs, because they are easy to add and immediately useful, and add metrics as your system grows. A system that logs its slow requests is a system that takes performance seriously, and users notice the difference, even if they never see a log line.

import time

# Log requests that exceed a threshold, with their duration.
THRESHOLD_MS = 1000

def handle_request(path):
    start = time.time()
    time.sleep(0.01)  # pretend work happens here
    duration_ms = (time.time() - start) * 1000
    if duration_ms > THRESHOLD_MS:
        print(f"WARNING: slow request {path} took {duration_ms:.0f}ms")
    else:
        print(f"INFO: {path} took {duration_ms:.0f}ms")

handle_request("/tasks")

Section 14: Logging Background Jobs and Queues

Background jobs, which you learned about in the chapter on email and jobs, are invisible to users, which makes their logs even more important than request logs. When a user makes a request, they see the result, or the error, immediately. When a background job runs, nobody sees anything, unless the job logs what it did. A job that fails silently is a job whose failure nobody notices until a user complains that an email never arrived, or a report never appeared, days later. Logging every job's lifecycle is how you prevent that silent failure.

The lifecycle to log is simple: when a job is queued, when a worker picks it up, when it finishes, and when it fails, including how many attempts it has had. A single line per stage, with the job id, type, and relevant identifiers, is enough. For example: "job 482, send_email to ada@example.com, queued", then "job 482 started", then "job 482 completed in 1.2 seconds", or "job 482 failed, attempt 3 of 5, connection refused". Those lines let you answer the essential questions: did the job run, did it succeed, how long did it take, and if it failed, why and how many times has it been tried? Without those lines, a job is a black box.

Queue logs also help with operational questions. How many jobs are waiting? How old is the oldest waiting job? Are jobs taking longer than usual? Those questions are answered by combining job lifecycle logs with queue metrics, which you will learn about later. But even without metrics, lifecycle logs let you investigate a specific complaint: a user says a report never arrived, you search for that user's report job, and you find it failed three times with a database error. That investigation takes minutes with good logs, and hours without them, because without logs you must reproduce the problem, which may depend on data or conditions that have changed.

There are also things to log around jobs, not just within them. Log when a worker starts and stops, because a worker that stopped explains a queue that grows. Log when a job is moved to the dead letter queue, because that is a job that needs human attention. Log when a scheduled job runs, and whether it did anything, because a scheduled job that silently stops running is a common failure. Those surrounding logs turn a collection of job lines into a picture of the whole background system: its workers, its queues, and its schedules, all visible.

Finally, apply the same rules to job logs as to other logs: be specific, include identifiers, do not log secrets, and choose levels correctly. A job failure is an error. A job retry is a warning. A job success is info. A job that is permanently failed and moved to dead letters is an error that deserves attention, not just a line in a file. Job logs that follow those rules are a pleasure to read during an incident, because they tell a clear story. Job logs that do not are a puzzle, and puzzles at three in the morning are the last thing anyone needs.

import logging

# A job's lifecycle in log lines: queued, started, finished or failed.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(levelname)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday.jobs")
log.info("Job 482 send_email to ada@example.com queued")
log.info("Job 482 started")
log.info("Job 482 completed in 1.2s")

Section 15: Logging Database Problems

Database problems are among the most serious a back-end system can face, because the database holds the data everything else depends on. When the database is slow, unavailable, or returning errors, the whole application suffers, and users notice quickly. Logging database problems well is therefore a priority, and it requires a little more care than logging ordinary errors, because database errors can be cryptic, and because the details that help diagnose them are specific to databases: which query, which table, how long it took, and what the database actually said.

The first thing to log is slow queries. A query that takes seconds, when it should take milliseconds, is a warning that something is wrong: a missing index, a growing table, or a query that scans too much data. Log slow queries with their text, or a safe summary of it, and their duration, so you can find and fix them. A slow query log is one of the most valuable logs a database system produces, and many databases produce one automatically. If yours does, read it regularly. If it does not, add slow query logging in your application code, by timing queries and logging those over a threshold, just as you log slow requests.

The second thing to log is connection problems. A database connection that fails, times out, or is exhausted, because all connections in the pool are in use, is a serious event that should be logged as an error, with the reason. Connection exhaustion is a common failure mode under load, and its log lines are the first clue: if you see many "connection pool exhausted" errors, you know the problem is capacity or leaking connections, not a single bad query. Without those lines, a database outage looks like a mysterious application failure, and the real cause is hidden.

The third thing to log is errors with their database message, but without sensitive data. Database error messages are often precise: a constraint violation names the constraint, a syntax error points to the query, a deadlock names the tables involved. Those details are gold for debugging, and they should be logged. But queries can contain personal data, such as values being inserted, and those values should not be logged in full. Log the query structure, or the query with values replaced by placeholders, and log the error message, but not the private values. That balance, precise about the error, careful about the data, is the right one for database logging.

Finally, log database lifecycle events: connections opened and closed, migrations run, backups completed, and replication status changes, if applicable. Those events are infrequent, but they matter during incidents: knowing that a migration ran just before errors started points to the migration as the cause. Knowing that a backup failed explains why recovery is difficult. Database logs, like other logs, tell a story, and the story is most useful when it includes both the routine and the exceptional, so that the exceptional can be placed in context.

import time, logging

# Log slow queries with their duration, not with private values.
logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(levelname)s: %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("taskday.db")

def run_query(sql, duration):
    if duration > 1.0:
        log.warning(f"Slow query ({duration:.2f}s): {sql}")

run_query("SELECT * FROM tasks WHERE user_id = ?", 2.3)

Section 16: Timestamps and Time Zones

Every log line needs a timestamp, but a timestamp is only useful if it is accurate, consistent, and unambiguous, and that is harder than it sounds, because servers, users, and developers may be in different time zones, and because clocks can drift. Getting timestamps right is a small detail with large consequences: during an incident, you need to correlate log lines with user reports, with metrics, and with logs from other systems, and if the timestamps do not agree, correlation becomes guesswork. This section covers the practices that make timestamps trustworthy.

The first practice is to log in UTC, which is the universal time standard, not in local time. UTC has no daylight saving changes, no ambiguity, and it is the same everywhere, so a log line from a server in Lagos and a log line from a server in London can be compared directly, without converting time zones. Local time is for display, when a human reads a log, and it should be converted at display time, not at log time. Logging in local time causes confusion twice a year, when clocks change, because the same local hour occurs twice, or not at all, and log lines during those hours cannot be ordered reliably. UTC avoids all of that, and it is the standard practice for server logs.

The second practice is to include enough precision. A timestamp with only seconds is often not enough, because many events happen within the same second, and ordering them matters. Including milliseconds, or even microseconds, lets you order events precisely and measure durations accurately. A timestamp like "2026-10-31T08:00:00.123Z" tells you the date, time, milliseconds, and that it is UTC, indicated by the Z. That format, called ISO 8601, is the standard for log timestamps, because it is unambiguous, sortable as text, and understood by tools worldwide. Use it, and do not invent your own timestamp format, because tools expect the standard.

The third practice is to keep clocks synchronised. Servers use a protocol called NTP, Network Time Protocol, to keep their clocks accurate against reference clocks. If a server's clock drifts by minutes, its log timestamps will be wrong, and correlating its logs with other systems will be misleading. Most servers synchronise automatically, but it is worth verifying, especially after an incident where timestamps did not line up. A clock that is minutes off is a silent bug that undermines every log line the server produces, and it is easily fixed once noticed.

Finally, be careful with timestamps in user-facing messages. A user report saying "it failed at 9 a.m." is in the user's local time, which may differ from the server's UTC logs by an hour or more. When correlating, convert the user's time to UTC, accounting for their time zone, before searching logs, or you will search the wrong hour and conclude, wrongly, that nothing was logged. Timestamp discipline, logging in UTC, with precision, in a standard format, on synchronised clocks, and converting carefully when comparing with human reports, is a small investment that makes every other logging practice work correctly.

from datetime import datetime, timezone

# Log in UTC, in ISO 8601 format, with milliseconds.
now = datetime.now(timezone.utc)
print(now.isoformat(timespec="milliseconds"))

Section 17: Request IDs: Following One Request Through the System

When a single user request touches many parts of your system, its log lines scatter across different components: the web server logs the request, your application logs its steps, the database logs its queries, and a background job logs its follow-up work. Without a way to connect those lines, debugging a single request means searching through thousands of unrelated lines, hoping to find the ones that belong together. A request id solves this. It is a unique identifier assigned to each incoming request, carried through every part of the system that handles it, and included in every log line related to it. With a request id, you can filter logs to one request and see its complete story.

Generating a request id is simple: when a request arrives, your server creates a random, unique string, such as a UUID, and stores it for the duration of the request. Every log call during that request includes the id, usually as a field in a structured log, or as part of the log format. When your code calls another service, or queues a background job, it passes the request id along, so that the other service's logs and the job's logs carry the same id. That propagation is what makes request ids powerful across systems, not just within one process. A request id that stops at the first service boundary is only half useful.

Request ids also help with user support. When a user reports a problem, you can ask for, or find, the request id from their error page or from your logs, and then pull every line for that request in seconds, instead of asking the user for details they may not have, or guessing which lines are theirs. Some applications show a request id on error pages, with a message like "if you contact support, mention this id", which turns a vague complaint into a precise investigation. That small user-facing detail saves significant support time, and it makes users feel that the system is prepared for problems, rather than surprised by them.

There are a few practices that make request ids work well. The id should be unique per request, not per user or per session, because uniqueness per request is what lets you isolate one incident. It should be random, not sequential, so that it cannot be guessed and used to probe other requests. It should be included in every log line, automatically, through your logging setup, rather than manually in each call, because manual inclusion will be forgotten. And it should be passed to downstream services and jobs, as mentioned, so the story continues across boundaries. Those practices turn a simple idea into a reliable tool.

Finally, request ids connect to traces, which you met earlier. A trace is, in essence, a request id plus timing information for each step, collected across services. If you implement request ids well, you have laid the groundwork for tracing, because you already have the identifier that ties steps together. Request ids are therefore not just a debugging convenience. They are a foundational practice for understanding distributed systems, and they are cheap to implement, which makes them one of the highest-value logging habits you can adopt, even in a single-server application.

import uuid

# One unique id per request, carried in every log line for that request.
request_id = str(uuid.uuid4())
print(f"[{request_id}] Request received: GET /tasks")
print(f"[{request_id}] Database query completed in 12ms")
print(f"[{request_id}] Response sent: 200")

Section 18: Correlating Logs Across Services

As applications grow, they split into parts: a web server, a database, a cache, a mail service, a queue, and perhaps several application servers. Each part produces its own logs, and a single user action can generate lines in several of them. Correlating logs across services means bringing those lines together into one story, so you can follow an action from start to finish, even though it touched many systems. Without correlation, each service's logs are an isolated fragment, and debugging a cross-service problem means opening many files and guessing which lines belong together. With correlation, the fragments become a coherent narrative.

The main tool for correlation is the request id from the previous section, propagated across service boundaries. When your web server calls a downstream service, it passes the request id in a header. The downstream service logs with that id, and if it calls further services, it passes the id along again. When a background job is queued, the request id is stored with the job, and the worker logs with it. That chain of propagation means that a single id can retrieve lines from every service involved, if the logs are collected in one place where they can be searched together. Propagation plus central collection is the complete correlation system, and either part alone is insufficient.

Central collection, sometimes called centralised logging, is the second tool. It means sending logs from all services to one store, where they can be searched as a whole, rather than leaving them on individual servers to be searched one by one. For a small system, central collection can be as simple as forwarding logs to one server's files, or to a shared directory. For larger systems, dedicated log services collect, index, and search logs at scale. The scale of the tool matters less than the principle: logs that cannot be searched together cannot be correlated, and correlation is essential once a system has more than one part.

There are challenges to correlation that are worth knowing. Clocks must agree, as covered earlier, or lines from different services will appear out of order. Ids must propagate through every boundary, including queues, scheduled jobs, and outside services, or the chain breaks. And log formats must be consistent enough that a search for an id finds it everywhere, which means agreeing on a field name, such as request_id, across services. Those challenges are organisational as much as technical: they require teams to agree on conventions and to maintain them as services change. That agreement is worth the effort, because without it, debugging a distributed system is painfully slow.

Finally, correlation is not just for incidents. It is also for understanding behaviour: how long does a typical request spend in each service? Which service is the bottleneck? Which downstream calls fail most often? Those questions are answered by correlated logs, or by traces built on the same ids, and they guide optimisation and reliability work. A system whose logs can be correlated is a system whose behaviour can be understood, and understanding is the prerequisite for improvement. Start with request ids and central collection, even in simple form, and correlation will grow naturally as your system grows.

# Correlation: the same request id appears in every service's logs.
request_id = "req-abc-123"
services = ["web server", "app server", "database", "mail worker"]
for service in services:
    print(f"[{request_id}] {service}: logged this request")

Section 19: Reading Logs: Searching and Filtering

Writing good logs is only half the skill. The other half is reading them effectively, because during an incident, you will face thousands or millions of lines, and you need to find the few that matter, quickly, under pressure. Reading logs well is a method, not a talent, and it can be learned: start from what you know, filter ruthlessly, follow identifiers, and expand outward from the interesting lines. That method turns a flood into a trail, and it is worth practising before an incident, not during one.

Start with what you know. A user report gives you a time, a user, an action, or an error message. Use those as your first filters: logs around that time, for that user, containing that error. If you have a request id, start there, because it is the most precise filter available. If you do not, start with time and level: errors around the reported time are the most likely candidates. Resist the temptation to read logs from the beginning, or to dump an entire file to the screen. Filtering first, reading second, is the efficient order, and it is the order experienced debuggers use instinctively.

Next, follow identifiers outward. Once you find an interesting line, note its request id, job id, or user id, and search for all lines with that identifier. That expands your view from one line to the complete story of that request or job, including the steps before the error, which often reveal the cause. Then, look at the lines immediately before and after, in time, because context matters: a database error preceded by a connection warning tells a different story from the same error preceded by a routine request. Context turns isolated lines into a narrative, and narratives are what you reason about.

Use tools for searching, not your eyes alone. Simple tools, like searching text in a file, work for small logs. For larger logs, use tools that understand structure: filter by level, by service, by time range, by field value, in structured logs. Learn the basic search patterns for your setup before an incident, so you are not learning them under pressure. And be careful with case, spelling, and format: a search for "error" will miss "ERROR" if the tool is case-sensitive, and a search for a user name will miss lines that use a user id instead. Knowing your log format, and its variations, prevents missed lines that lead to wrong conclusions.

Finally, keep notes as you read. During a long investigation, it is easy to lose track of what you have checked and what you have found. A short note, even just a list of interesting lines and their times, keeps the investigation organised and makes it easier to hand over to another person, or to write a postmortem later. Reading logs is detective work, and detectives keep notes. The habit of noting what you find, as you find it, turns a frantic search into a methodical investigation, and methodical investigations solve problems faster and with fewer false leads.

# Filtering first, reading second: narrow thousands of lines to the few that matter.
logs = [
    {"level": "info", "user": "ada", "message": "Task created"},
    {"level": "error", "user": "ben", "message": "Email failed"},
    {"level": "info", "user": "ben", "message": "Task created"},
]
errors_for_ben = [l for l in logs if l["level"] == "error" and l["user"] == "ben"]
print(errors_for_ben)

Section 20: Common Log Mistakes

Like every practice, logging has its classic mistakes, and this section collects them so you can avoid the most common ones. They are not exotic. They are the ordinary errors that beginners, and sometimes experienced developers under pressure, make repeatedly, and each one makes logs less useful at the moment they are most needed, which is during an incident. Reading this list is a cheap way to learn from others' incidents instead of your own.

The first mistake is logging too little. An application that logs only errors, with no context, leaves you with no trail to follow when an error occurs, because there are no surrounding lines showing what led up to it. The fix is to log the normal flow at INFO level, so errors arrive with context. The second mistake is logging too much. An application that logs every variable, every loop iteration, and every minor detail at INFO level produces so many lines that important ones drown, storage fills, and searching becomes slow. The fix is levels: detail belongs at DEBUG, not INFO, and DEBUG stays off in production.

The third mistake is vague messages, such as "error" or "failed", without saying what, for whom, or why. The fix is specificity and identifiers, as covered earlier. The fourth mistake is logging secrets or personal data, which turns a debugging aid into a security incident. The fix is selective logging by field, and redaction. The fifth mistake is inconsistent formats, where different parts of the code log in different styles, making searching and parsing unreliable. The fix is a standard format, configured centrally, used everywhere.

The sixth mistake is missing timestamps, or timestamps in local time, or without enough precision, which makes ordering and correlation unreliable. The fix is UTC, ISO format, with milliseconds. The seventh mistake is logging an error in many places for one failure, creating duplicates that inflate error counts and confuse investigations. The fix is to log once, where handled, with full context. The eighth mistake is never reading logs except during a crisis, which means problems that develop slowly, such as rising error rates or growing slowness, go unnoticed until they become emergencies. The fix is regular review, even briefly, of summaries and trends.

The ninth mistake is keeping logs only on the server that produced them, with no central collection, so that debugging a multi-server problem means logging into each server separately. The fix is central collection, even in simple form, as your system grows. The tenth mistake is no rotation, so logs fill the disk and cause an outage, which is an embarrassing way for a logging system to cause the very problem it was meant to help solve. The fix is rotation, configured and verified. None of these mistakes is hard to avoid, once you know them, and avoiding them is what makes the difference between logs that save you and logs that frustrate you.

Section 21: What Is Monitoring?

If logging is your server's memory, monitoring is its watchfulness: the practice of continuously checking whether your system is healthy, and alerting a human when it is not. A monitored system watches its own vital signs, such as whether the server responds, how fast it responds, how many errors occur, and how full its resources are, and it reports those signs on dashboards and through alerts. An unmonitored system, by contrast, can fail silently for hours, with nobody noticing until users complain, by which time the damage, in lost trust and lost work, is much larger than it needed to be.

Monitoring answers a different question from logging. Logging answers "what happened?" Monitoring answers "is everything all right, right now?" That difference shapes how monitoring works. Logs are detailed records you search after something happens. Monitoring is a live view you glance at regularly, plus alerts that find you when something goes wrong. A dashboard showing green health checks and steady response times tells you, at a glance, that the system is fine, without reading a single log line. An alert telling you error rates doubled tells you to investigate, before users notice. Monitoring is proactive. Logging is reactive. Both are needed, and they complement each other.

Monitoring covers many things, and this section gives you the map. At the simplest level, it checks whether the server is up: does it answer requests at all? That is uptime monitoring. At the next level, it checks performance: how fast are responses, and are they getting slower? That is performance monitoring. At the next, it checks resources: how full are the disk, memory, and CPU, and are they approaching limits? That is resource monitoring. And at the deepest level, it checks business health: are users signing up, are emails being sent, are jobs completing? That is the level that matters most to users, because a server can be "up" while the features users care about are broken. Good monitoring covers all these levels, not just the first.

A useful way to think about monitoring is as a set of questions you ask continuously. Is the server responding? Are responses fast enough? Are errors rare enough? Are resources sufficient? Are the important features working? Each question needs a check, a measurement, and a threshold for alerting. A system that asks those questions automatically, around the clock, is a system that catches problems early, often before users do. A system that does not ask them is relying on users to be its monitoring system, which is unfair to users and slow to detect problems.

Finally, monitoring is not about collecting every possible number. It is about collecting the numbers that tell you whether users are having a good experience, and acting on them. A dashboard with a hundred graphs nobody looks at is not monitoring. It is decoration. A dashboard with ten graphs that someone checks daily, plus alerts that fire when those numbers go wrong, is monitoring. Start small, with the checks that matter most, and grow as you learn what your system needs. The rest of this chapter teaches you those checks, one by one, and how to turn them into alerts that help rather than harass.

# Monitoring asks, continuously: is the server up, fast, and error-free?
health = {"responding": True, "avg_response_ms": 120, "error_rate": 0.01}
ok = health["responding"] and health["avg_response_ms"] < 1000 and health["error_rate"] < 0.05
print("System healthy:", ok)

Section 22: Monitoring Versus Logging

Logging and monitoring are often mentioned together, and they are related, but they are not the same, and confusing them leads to using one where the other is needed. This section draws the distinction sharply, because understanding it helps you design both well, and helps you explain to others why you need both, not just one. The core difference is between records and watchfulness: logs record what happened, monitoring watches what is happening, and alerts you when it goes wrong.

Logs are detailed, historical, and searched on demand. When an incident occurs, you search logs to understand it. Logs are therefore optimised for detail and for after-the-fact investigation. They are verbose, because detail is their value, and they are stored for days or weeks, because incidents are often investigated after the fact. Monitoring, by contrast, is summarised, live, and checked continuously. Metrics are numbers, not stories, and they are graphed and alerted on in real time. Monitoring is optimised for noticing, not for explaining. A metric tells you something changed. A log tells you what changed, and why.

That difference leads to different tools and different habits. Log tools are about searching, filtering, and reading: finding lines, following ids, reconstructing stories. Monitoring tools are about graphing, thresholding, and alerting: watching numbers, setting limits, and notifying humans. A team that only has logs will notice problems late, because nobody reads logs continuously, waiting for an error to appear. A team that only has monitoring will notice problems quickly, but will struggle to explain them, because numbers without detail do not say which users, which requests, or which errors are involved. The two together cover both noticing and explaining, which is why mature systems invest in both.

There is also a difference in cost and volume. Logs are high-volume and expensive to store, because they record every event in detail. Metrics are low-volume and cheap to store, because they summarise many events into a few numbers per time period. That is why metrics are kept for months, while logs are often kept for weeks: metrics are compact enough to keep long-term, and their long-term trends, such as traffic growth over a year, are valuable. Logs, being bulky, are kept shorter-term, for incident investigation, and then rotated away. Understanding that trade helps you set retention policies sensibly, rather than keeping everything forever, which is expensive, or deleting everything quickly, which destroys evidence.

In practice, the workflow connects them. Monitoring notices a problem and alerts you. You then turn to logs to diagnose it, using the time, the metric, and the affected service as your starting filters. After fixing it, you return to monitoring to confirm the numbers recovered. That loop, notice with monitoring, diagnose with logs, verify with monitoring, is the standard incident workflow, and it is worth memorising, because it is how most real incidents are handled. A developer who understands both sides of the loop, and how they connect, is a developer who can handle incidents calmly and effectively, rather than flailing between tools.

# Monitoring notices; logging explains. The incident loop uses both.
print("1. Monitoring alerts: error rate doubled at 14:05")
print("2. Logs explain: database connection failures for job 482")
print("3. Monitoring verifies: error rate back to normal after fix")

Section 23: Health Checks: Is the Server Alive?

A health check is the simplest form of monitoring: a small endpoint on your server that answers the question "are you alive and able to work?" Usually, it is a URL like /health, which returns a success response when the server is running, and an error when it is not. An outside monitor calls that endpoint regularly, every minute or so, and if it stops answering, or answers with an error, the monitor alerts a human. Health checks are simple, cheap, and enormously valuable, because they catch the most basic failure, a server that has stopped, quickly and reliably.

A basic health check only confirms the process is running and can answer a request. That is useful, but it is not enough, because a server can answer a health check while its database is down, its disk is full, or its important features are broken. A deeper health check also verifies its dependencies: it tries a simple database query, checks disk space, and confirms that essential services respond. If any dependency fails, the health check reports unhealthy, even though the server process itself is alive. That deeper check catches more failures, but it must be designed carefully: a health check that depends on everything will report unhealthy for minor dependency problems that do not actually affect users, causing false alarms.

There is a balance to strike, and a common solution is two levels of health check. A liveness check, which only says the process is alive, used by systems that restart unhealthy processes automatically. And a readiness check, which says the server is ready to serve traffic, including its dependencies, used by load balancers to decide whether to send traffic to this server. A server that is alive but not ready, because its database is down, should not receive traffic it cannot serve, so the readiness check removes it from rotation until it recovers. That two-level design is standard in larger systems, and it is worth understanding even for a single server, because it separates "am I running?" from "can I do useful work?"

Health check endpoints should be cheap and safe. They should not do heavy work, because they are called frequently, and heavy checks add load. They should not expose sensitive information, because they are often publicly accessible, so they should return a simple status, not details about your infrastructure. And they should not require authentication, or the monitor cannot call them, but that also means they should reveal nothing an attacker could use. A health check that returns "ok" or "fail", and nothing more, is the right level of detail for a public endpoint. Detailed diagnostics belong in logs and private dashboards, not in a public health response.

Finally, health checks need a monitor to call them. A health endpoint nobody calls is just a URL. Set up an external monitor, even a simple one, that calls your health endpoint regularly and alerts you on failure. External matters, because a monitor on the same server will fail when the server fails, and cannot alert you. An outside monitor, on a different machine or service, can see that your server is down even when your server cannot see anything. That outside perspective is what makes health checks a real safety net, rather than a decorative endpoint.

from http.server import BaseHTTPRequestHandler

# A health endpoint: cheap, simple, and honest about readiness.
class Handler(BaseHTTPRequestHandler):
    def do_GET(self):
        if self.path == "/health":
            self.send_response(200)
            self.end_headers()
            self.wfile.write(b"ok")

Section 24: Uptime Monitoring

Uptime is the share of time your service is available and working, usually expressed as a percentage, such as 99.9 percent, which allows about forty-three minutes of downtime per month. Uptime monitoring is the practice of measuring that availability from the outside, by regularly checking whether your service answers, and recording when it does not. It is the most basic promise a service makes to its users, that it will be there when they need it, and measuring it honestly is the first step to keeping that promise.

Uptime is measured by probes: regular requests from a monitor to your service, often to a health endpoint, or to a key page, from one or more locations. Each probe is a success or a failure, and uptime is the share of successes over a period. Probes from multiple locations matter, because a service can be reachable from one place but not another, due to network problems. A single-location monitor might report your service as down when it is really the monitor's network that failed, or report it as up when users in another region cannot reach it. Multiple locations give a truer picture, and they help distinguish a service outage from a network problem.

Uptime percentages sound precise, but they need interpretation. A service with 99.9 percent uptime sounds excellent, but that still allows over eight hours of downtime per year, which users will notice if it happens at a bad time. A service with 99 percent uptime allows over three days per year, which is a very different experience. The number of nines matters, but so does when downtime happens, and how it is communicated. A planned maintenance window, announced in advance, is experienced differently from an unexpected outage, even if both count the same in the percentage. Uptime is a useful summary, but it is not the whole story of reliability.

Uptime monitoring also produces a record that is useful beyond the percentage. It shows when outages happened, how long they lasted, and whether they correlate with deployments, traffic peaks, or dependency failures. That record helps you find patterns: outages every Sunday night might point to a scheduled job, outages after deployments might point to a release process problem. Without a record, outages blur into vague memories. With a record, they become data you can act on, which is the same lesson as logging, applied to availability.

Finally, uptime monitoring should be paired with communication. When your service goes down, users should not have to guess. A status page, covered later in this chapter, tells them what is happening and when it is expected to be fixed. An uptime monitor that alerts only you, while users are left in the dark, is doing half its job. The other half is telling users, honestly and promptly, that there is a problem and that you are working on it. That communication preserves trust during an outage, which is when trust is most at risk, and it is a habit that distinguishes a professional service from an amateur one.

# Uptime: the share of probes that succeeded over a period.
probes = [True] * 999 + [False]
uptime = sum(probes) / len(probes)
print(f"Uptime: {uptime:.1%} ({len(probes) - sum(probes)} failed probe(s))")

Section 25: Metrics: Numbers That Describe Health

Metrics are numbers that describe how your system is behaving, measured regularly over time. Examples include how many requests arrive per second, how long responses take on average, what share of requests fail, how much memory is used, and how many jobs are waiting in a queue. Metrics are the language of monitoring, because they turn vague impressions, like "the site feels slow", into precise statements, like "average response time rose from 200 milliseconds to 800 milliseconds after Tuesday's deployment". Precision like that is what makes problems actionable, because it tells you what changed, by how much, and when.

Metrics come in a few basic types, and knowing them helps you choose the right one. A counter is a number that only goes up, such as total requests served, or total errors. Counters are useful for rates: by comparing a counter at two times, you can compute requests per second, or errors per minute. A gauge is a number that goes up and down, such as current memory usage, or current queue depth. Gauges describe the present state. A histogram, or distribution, describes how values spread out, such as response times: how many were fast, how many were slow, and what the slowest few percent looked like. Histograms matter because averages hide extremes: an average response time of 200 milliseconds sounds fine, but if ten percent of users wait five seconds, those users are having a bad experience that the average conceals.

Collecting metrics requires instrumentation: code that records measurements as your application runs. That might be as simple as incrementing a counter on each request, recording a duration, or reading a system value like memory usage. Many frameworks and libraries provide instrumentation automatically, but even hand-built instrumentation, with a few counters and timers in key places, is valuable. The key places are the boundaries: incoming requests, outgoing calls to outside services, database queries, and queue operations. Measuring at boundaries tells you where time and failures concentrate, which is exactly what you need to know.

Metrics are stored over time and displayed as graphs, which is where their power becomes visible. A graph of response times over a week shows trends, spikes, and daily patterns at a glance, in a way that a table of numbers cannot. A graph of error rates shows when a problem started, which often points to its cause, such as a deployment or a traffic surge. Learning to read metric graphs, to spot a trend versus a spike, a gradual rise versus a sudden jump, is a skill that improves with practice, and it is one of the most useful skills for running a service, because it lets you see problems developing before they become crises.

Finally, metrics must be chosen deliberately. Collecting every possible number creates noise and cost, without insight. Collecting the numbers that reflect user experience, such as response time, error rate, and feature success rate, plus the numbers that reflect resource health, such as CPU, memory, disk, and queue depth, gives you a balanced view. Those two groups, user experience and resource health, are the core of monitoring, and they map to the golden signals covered next. Start with those, and add more only when a specific question requires them.

# Counters, gauges, and distributions: three basic metric types.
metrics = {"requests_total": 12500,      # counter: only goes up
           "queue_depth": 42,            # gauge: goes up and down
           "response_times_ms": [45, 60, 120, 300, 1200]}  # distribution
print("Average response:", sum(metrics["response_times_ms"]) / len(metrics["response_times_ms"]), "ms")
print("Slowest response:", max(metrics["response_times_ms"]), "ms")

Section 26: The Four Golden Signals

When monitoring a service, it is easy to drown in numbers. The four golden signals are a widely used shortlist of the most important metrics, chosen because together they describe user experience and system health with minimal noise. They are latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. If you monitor those four well, you will catch most problems early, and you will have a clear picture of whether users are having a good experience. If you monitor only one, you will miss problems that the others would have caught. Learning the four signals gives you a framework for deciding what to monitor first, in any system.

Latency is how long it takes for a request to be served, usually measured as response time. Users experience latency directly: a slow page feels broken, even if it eventually loads. Latency should be measured as a distribution, not just an average, because the slowest users matter most. A common practice is to track the 95th and 99th percentiles, which are the response times that 95 percent and 99 percent of requests beat, so you can see what your slowest users experience. A rising 99th percentile means a growing minority is suffering, even if the average looks fine, and that minority will complain first.

Traffic is how much demand is placed on your system, such as requests per second, or jobs per minute. Traffic explains many other signals: a latency rise during a traffic spike may be normal load, not a bug, while a latency rise with flat traffic points to a real problem. Traffic also drives capacity planning: if traffic grows steadily, you will need more resources eventually, and traffic graphs show you when. Measuring traffic per endpoint, not just overall, is also valuable, because a surge in one endpoint, such as a search page, affects the system differently from a general surge.

Errors are the rate of failed requests, such as the share returning error status codes, or jobs that fail. Errors are the most direct measure of user harm: every error is a user who did not get what they wanted. Error rate should be tracked overall and per endpoint, because a small endpoint with a high error rate can be hidden in a low overall rate, while still harming its users. A sudden error spike is often the first sign of a deployment problem, a dependency failure, or a data issue, and it deserves immediate investigation, even if latency and traffic look normal.

Saturation is how full your resources are: CPU usage, memory usage, disk usage, queue depth, and connection pool usage, expressed as a share of capacity. Saturation predicts failure: a disk at 95 percent will soon be full, a queue growing without bound will soon delay everything, a connection pool at its limit will soon reject new work. Saturation is the early warning signal, the one that tells you a problem is coming, while the other three tell you a problem is here. Monitoring saturation, with alerts before limits are reached, is what prevents many outages entirely, because it gives you time to act before users are affected.

# The four golden signals, summarised for one service.
signals = {"latency_p95_ms": 320, "traffic_rps": 150,
           "error_rate": 0.02, "disk_used_percent": 68}
for name, value in signals.items():
    print(name, "=", value)

Section 27: Collecting Metrics in Python

Collecting metrics in Python, using only the standard library, is simpler than it sounds, and it teaches the core ideas that larger monitoring systems build on. At its simplest, a metric is a variable that your code updates as it works: a counter incremented on each request, a timer recording how long an operation took, a gauge set to the current queue depth. Those variables can be stored in memory, in a dictionary, and exposed through a simple endpoint, such as /metrics, that returns them as text. That endpoint can then be read by a monitoring tool, or even by a human checking the numbers. That is the whole pattern, in miniature.

A practical approach starts with a small metrics module, much like the cache module you designed earlier. It provides functions to increment a counter, record a duration, and set a gauge, and it stores the values in a thread-safe way, because multiple requests may update metrics concurrently. Python's standard library provides locks for that, and for a learning project, a simple lock around updates is enough. The module should also provide a way to report all metrics, such as a function returning a dictionary, or a formatted string, so that an endpoint can serve them. Keeping metrics code in one module, with a clear interface, makes it easy to use consistently across your application, and easy to replace later with a dedicated monitoring library if you outgrow it.

Where should you record metrics? At the same boundaries where you log: incoming requests, database queries, outside service calls, and queue operations. For a request, record that it happened, its endpoint, its status, and its duration. For a database query, record its duration and whether it succeeded. For a queue, record its depth and the age of the oldest job. Those measurements, taken consistently, give you the golden signals for your system, without needing to instrument every function. Instrument the boundaries first, and add deeper instrumentation only where a boundary measurement shows a problem that needs explaining.

There are also system metrics, such as CPU, memory, and disk usage, which Python can read through the operating system, though the standard library's support is limited compared to dedicated tools. For a learning project, reading simple values, such as disk usage, is enough to understand the idea. In production, dedicated system monitoring tools usually collect those numbers, because they do it reliably and with little overhead. The lesson is not to build a full system monitor in Python, but to understand that application metrics, which only your code can know, and system metrics, which tools can collect, complement each other, and both belong on your dashboards.

Finally, be careful about the cost of metrics. Recording a metric on every request adds a small overhead, and if the recording itself is slow, such as writing to a file on every request, it can become a performance problem. Keep metric updates in memory, fast, and simple, and let a separate process or endpoint read them. That separation, fast updates in the request path, slower reading outside it, is what keeps metrics cheap. A metrics system that slows the application it measures is a system that will be disabled, and a disabled monitoring system helps nobody.

import threading, time

# A tiny metrics module: counters and timers, updated in memory.
lock = threading.Lock()
metrics = {"requests_total": 0, "total_time_ms": 0.0}

def record_request(duration_ms):
    with lock:
        metrics["requests_total"] += 1
        metrics["total_time_ms"] += duration_ms

record_request(45)
record_request(60)
avg = metrics["total_time_ms"] / metrics["requests_total"]
print(f"Requests: {metrics['requests_total']}, average: {avg:.0f}ms")

Section 28: Dashboards in Plain Words

A dashboard is a visual display of your system's health, usually a page with several graphs and numbers, updated regularly, that lets a human see, at a glance, whether everything is all right. Dashboards are the human face of monitoring: metrics are numbers, dashboards turn those numbers into pictures, and pictures are much easier to scan than tables. A good dashboard answers, in seconds, the questions a team asks most often: is the system up, is it fast, are errors low, are resources sufficient? A bad dashboard, cluttered with irrelevant graphs, answers nothing quickly, and is ignored, which defeats its purpose.

Designing a good dashboard starts with the audience and the purpose. A dashboard for an on-call engineer during an incident needs the golden signals, error details, and recent changes, prominently, with little else. A dashboard for a weekly review needs trends over weeks, traffic growth, and slow endpoints, to guide planning. A dashboard for a business audience needs user-facing numbers, such as signups and feature usage, not server internals. One dashboard cannot serve all audiences well, so most teams have several, each focused on one purpose and one audience. Trying to put everything on one page creates a wall of graphs that nobody reads.

A good dashboard also follows a few visual principles. Put the most important numbers at the top, large and clear, so they are seen first. Group related graphs together, such as all latency graphs, or all per-endpoint graphs, so the eye can compare them. Use consistent scales and time ranges, so that a spike in one graph can be compared with a spike in another. And avoid decoration: every graph should answer a question someone actually asks. A graph that nobody can explain the purpose of should be removed. Dashboards, like logs, suffer from noise, and regular pruning keeps them useful.

Time range matters. A dashboard showing the last hour is for incidents: it shows what just changed. A dashboard showing the last week is for trends: it shows gradual changes that an hour view would hide. A dashboard showing the last month is for planning: it shows growth and seasonality. Being able to switch ranges, and to compare with a previous period, such as this week versus last week, is valuable, because it turns a number into a judgement: is this normal, or is this unusual? A response time of 500 milliseconds means little on its own. It means a lot if last week it was 200 milliseconds, and nothing if last week it was also 500.

Finally, dashboards are not a substitute for alerts. A dashboard requires a human to look at it, and humans do not look continuously. Alerts, covered next, find humans when something goes wrong, even if nobody is looking at a dashboard. Dashboards and alerts work together: alerts tell you when to look, dashboards tell you what you are looking at. A team that relies only on dashboards will miss problems that happen between glances. A team that relies only on alerts will lack context when an alert fires. Use both, with dashboards for understanding and alerts for noticing, and your monitoring will be both timely and informative.

# A dashboard in miniature: the golden signals, at a glance.
dashboard = {"uptime": "99.95%", "latency_p95_ms": 320,
             "error_rate": "0.4%", "disk_used": "68%"}
for name, value in dashboard.items():
    print(f"{name}: {value}")

Section 29: Alerts: When to Wake a Human

An alert is a notification that tells a human something needs attention, now. Alerts are the active part of monitoring: while dashboards wait to be looked at, alerts go and find someone, by message, email, or phone call, when a threshold is crossed. Alerting well is a skill, because alerts that fire too often, or for unimportant things, train people to ignore them, which is dangerous, while alerts that fire too rarely, or too late, fail to prevent harm. The goal is alerts that fire only when human action is needed, promptly enough to act, and with enough information to act on.

The first question for any alert is: does this require a human, now? If the answer is no, it should not be an alert. It might be a dashboard graph, a daily report, or a log line, but it should not wake someone or interrupt their work. Examples of things that deserve alerts include: the service is down, error rates have spiked, a critical dependency is failing, disk space will run out within hours, or a security event is detected. Examples that do not deserve alerts include: a single failed request, a minor latency increase within normal variation, or a daily summary. That distinction, action required now versus information for later, is the core of alert design.

The second question is: what is the threshold, and for how long must it be crossed before alerting? A threshold that is too sensitive fires on normal variation, creating noise. A threshold that is too loose fires late, after users have suffered. And a threshold crossed for a second, such as a single slow request, should not alert, while the same threshold crossed for five minutes probably should. That duration condition, sometimes called "for" a period, filters out brief blips and focuses on sustained problems. Choosing thresholds and durations requires knowing what normal looks like for your system, which comes from watching metrics over time, not from guessing on day one.

The third question is: what information should the alert contain? An alert that says "something is wrong" is nearly useless, because the recipient must then investigate from scratch, under pressure. A good alert says what is wrong, which service, which metric, what the value is, what the threshold is, and when it started, plus a link to the relevant dashboard and logs. That information lets the recipient judge severity immediately and start investigating in the right place. It also lets them decide, quickly, whether the alert is a real problem or a false alarm, which is important for maintaining trust in the alerting system.

Finally, alerts need an owner and a response. Every alert should have a person or team responsible for it, and a documented first response: what to check first, what to do if it is real, and when to escalate. An alert with no owner is an alert nobody acts on. An alert with no documented response forces the recipient to improvise under pressure, which leads to mistakes. Writing down the response, even briefly, and reviewing alerts regularly to remove noisy ones and add missing ones, is what turns alerting from a source of stress into a reliable safety net.

# An alert fires only when a threshold is crossed for a sustained period.
error_rate = 0.08
threshold = 0.05
sustained_minutes = 5
if error_rate > threshold and sustained_minutes >= 5:
    print(f"ALERT: error rate {error_rate:.0%} above {threshold:.0%} for 5 minutes")

Section 30: Alert Rules That Work

Good alert rules share a few properties, and learning them helps you write alerts that people trust and act on, rather than alerts that people mute. The first property is that they alert on symptoms, not causes. A symptom is what users experience: the site is slow, requests are failing, a feature is broken. A cause is why: the database is slow, a server is full, a dependency is down. Alerting on symptoms ensures you catch problems users actually feel, even if the cause is unexpected. Alerting only on causes means you might miss a problem whose cause you did not anticipate, while users suffer. Symptom-based alerts, such as high error rate or high latency, are the primary alerts. Cause-based alerts, such as high CPU, are secondary, useful for diagnosis, but less reliable as the first signal.

The second property is that they are specific. An alert should name the service, the endpoint, or the feature affected, not just say "errors are high". Specificity lets the recipient know where to look and how severe the problem is: errors on a minor endpoint are less urgent than errors on the signup page. It also prevents a single alert from covering many unrelated problems, which makes it hard to know when it is resolved. If an alert fires for two different causes, split it into two alerts, each with its own threshold and owner. Specific alerts are actionable. Vague alerts are puzzles.

The third property is that they have sensible thresholds based on real data, not guesses. A threshold set without knowing normal variation will either fire constantly or never fire. The right way is to watch the metric for a while, learn its normal range, including daily patterns and peaks, and set the threshold outside that range, with room for unusual but harmless variation. For example, if error rates normally vary between 0.1 and 0.5 percent, a threshold of 2 percent will catch real problems without firing on normal days. Thresholds should also be reviewed periodically, because normal changes as traffic grows.

The fourth property is that they include a runbook, or at least a first step. A runbook is a short document saying what to check first, what the common causes are, and how to fix them, or when to escalate. An alert with a runbook link lets a responder act quickly, even if they have never seen this alert before, and even if they are tired or stressed. An alert without any guidance forces improvisation, which is slow and error-prone. Runbooks do not need to be long. A few bullet points, covering the most common causes and the first checks, are enough to make a large difference in response time and confidence.

The fifth property is that they are reviewed and pruned. Alert rules accumulate over time, and without review, they become noisy and outdated. A regular monthly review, asking which alerts fired, which were useful, which were false alarms, and which problems had no alert, keeps the set healthy. Remove alerts that always fire falsely, add alerts for problems that were missed, and adjust thresholds that are too tight or too loose. Alerting is a living system, not a one-time setup.

# Symptom-based alert: users experience errors, whatever the cause.
error_rate = 0.06
if error_rate > 0.05:
    print("ALERT: user-facing error rate high; check dashboard and logs")
    print("Runbook: 1) check recent deployments 2) check database 3) escalate if unresolved")

Section 31: Alert Fatigue: Too Many Alarms

Alert fatigue is what happens when a system produces so many alerts, most of them unimportant or false, that the humans responsible stop paying attention to them. It is a well-known and dangerous failure mode, because an ignored alerting system is worse than no alerting system: it gives a false sense of safety, while real problems go unnoticed among the noise. Alert fatigue does not happen because people are lazy. It happens because the alerting system cried wolf too many times, and people, reasonably, learned that alerts usually mean nothing. Once that lesson is learned, it is hard to unlearn, and a real emergency can be missed.

The causes of alert fatigue are worth naming, because each has a fix. The first is thresholds set too low, so normal variation triggers alerts. The fix is to base thresholds on observed normal ranges, as covered earlier. The second is alerting on causes rather than symptoms, so a single incident produces many alerts, one per cause, overwhelming the responder. The fix is symptom-based alerting, with causes as diagnostic information, not as separate alerts. The third is alerting on things nobody can act on, such as a brief spike that resolves itself, or a metric for a system nobody owns. The fix is to ask, for each alert, "can someone act on this, now?" and to remove alerts where the answer is no.

The fourth cause is lack of grouping. When a database fails, every service that depends on it may alert separately, producing dozens of alerts for one incident. The fix is grouping, or deduplication: alerts about the same incident, within a short window, should be combined into one notification, with the details attached. Many alerting tools support grouping, and it dramatically reduces noise during large incidents, when responders are already under pressure and need clarity, not volume. The fifth cause is alerts that never resolve, or that re-fire repeatedly for a known, accepted problem. The fix is to acknowledge and silence known issues deliberately, with a time limit, rather than letting them fire forever, which trains everyone to ignore them.

Recovering from alert fatigue takes deliberate work. First, measure it: count alerts per day, and how many were acted on versus ignored. If most alerts are ignored, the system is already fatigued. Second, hold a review and remove or fix the noisiest alerts, even if that means temporarily having fewer alerts, because a few trusted alerts are worth more than many ignored ones. Third, set a budget: agree on a maximum number of alerts per day that is acceptable, and treat exceeding it as a problem to fix. That budget forces discipline, because every new alert must earn its place by displacing noise.

Finally, remember that alert fatigue is a human problem, not just a technical one. Responders who are woken at night for false alarms become tired, stressed, and less effective, which harms both them and the service. Respecting responders' attention, by alerting only when action is truly needed, is part of running a humane and sustainable operation. A team that protects its responders' attention will have responders who are alert, engaged, and ready when a real incident occurs. A team that wastes attention on noise will not, and the service will suffer for it, sooner or later.

# Alert fatigue in numbers: most alerts ignored means the system is broken.
alerts = {"acted on": 3, "ignored": 27}
total = sum(alerts.values())
print(f"Ignored: {alerts['ignored'] / total:.0%} of alerts -> fatigue, fix the noise")

Section 32: On-Call in Plain Words

On-call is the practice of having a designated person, or team, responsible for responding to alerts outside normal working hours, such as nights and weekends, so that incidents are handled promptly even when the office is empty. It is a common part of running a service that users depend on around the clock, and it is also a significant responsibility, because it asks people to be available, and sometimes to be woken, for problems they did not cause. Understanding on-call, even if you never join a rota yourself, helps you design alerts and systems that respect the people who keep them running.

The basic structure is a rota: a schedule that says who is on-call each week, or each day, rotating among team members so the burden is shared. The on-call person carries a phone or pager, monitors alerts, and is expected to respond within an agreed time, such as fifteen minutes, to acknowledge an alert and begin investigating. If they cannot resolve the problem, they escalate to others, such as a specialist or a manager, according to a defined path. That structure, rota plus response time plus escalation path, is what makes on-call work, rather than being a vague expectation that "someone" will handle it. Vague expectations fail at 3 a.m. Clear structures do not.

On-call works best when alerts are good, which connects back to the previous sections. An on-call person woken for a false alarm, or for a problem with no documented response, will quickly become frustrated and less effective. An on-call person woken rarely, for real problems, with clear runbooks and the tools to fix them, can handle incidents calmly and well. That is why alert quality is not just a technical concern. It is a human concern, directly affecting the wellbeing and effectiveness of the on-call team. Every noisy alert is a tax on a real person, and that tax should be weighed before the alert is created.

There are also practices that make on-call sustainable. Rotations should be fair, with no one person always on-call, and with time off after a difficult week. On-call work should be counted as real work, recognised and compensated, not treated as a free extra. After an incident, the on-call person should not be blamed for the incident, which is a separate question from how they responded, and blameless review, covered in the postmortem section, is essential for a healthy on-call culture. And the number of on-call alerts should be tracked, as a health metric for the system itself: a rising number of night-time alerts is a sign the system needs fixing, not that the on-call person needs to try harder.

For a small application, or a solo developer, on-call may simply mean you, with alerts sent to your phone, and a personal decision about which hours you cover. That is still on-call, and the same principles apply: only alert for real problems, document responses, and protect your own rest, because a tired solo developer makes mistakes just as a tired team does. You might also choose to limit your promise: a small service might honestly say it is best-effort outside working hours, rather than pretending to offer round-the-clock response it cannot sustain. Honest limits are better than broken promises, for users and for the developer alike.

rota = {"week 1": "Ada", "week 2": "Ben", "week 3": "Chi"}
print("On-call this week:", rota["week 1"], "(respond within 15 minutes)")

Section 33: Monitoring the Database

The database is the heart of most back-end systems, and monitoring it deserves special attention, because database problems affect everything, and because they often develop gradually before becoming crises. Database monitoring covers a few key areas: availability, performance, capacity, and errors. Availability asks whether the database answers at all. Performance asks how fast queries run. Capacity asks how full it is, in disk, connections, and other resources. Errors ask how often queries fail, and why. Together, those areas give you a picture of database health that lets you act before users suffer.

Availability monitoring for a database is similar to a health check: a simple query, run regularly, that confirms the database responds. If that query fails, the database is down or unreachable, and an alert should fire immediately, because little else can work without it. That simple check is the most important database monitor, because it catches the worst failure fastest. It should be run from the application's perspective, not just from the database server itself, because a database can be running yet unreachable from the application, due to a network or configuration problem, and only an application-perspective check catches that.

Performance monitoring for a database focuses on slow queries and query volume. A log or metric of query durations, especially the slowest queries, tells you where time is spent and which queries need optimisation, such as adding an index or rewriting a query. Query volume, the number of queries per second, tells you about load, and whether it is growing. A sudden rise in query volume, without a matching rise in user traffic, might indicate a bug, such as a loop that queries repeatedly, or a cache that stopped working, causing every request to hit the database. Those patterns are visible in performance metrics, and they are worth watching, because database performance problems often masquerade as application slowness.

Capacity monitoring for a database covers disk space, connection count, and table sizes. Disk space is critical, because a full disk stops the database entirely, and it should be alerted well before it fills, such as at 80 percent, not at 99 percent. Connection count matters because databases allow a limited number of simultaneous connections, and exhausting them causes new requests to fail, even though the database itself is healthy. Table sizes matter for planning: a table growing rapidly may need archiving, partitioning, or a larger disk, and knowing its growth rate lets you plan rather than react. Capacity metrics are the early warnings that prevent database outages, and they should be on every database dashboard.

Error monitoring for a database tracks failed queries, by type: constraint violations, deadlocks, timeouts, and connection errors. Each type points to a different cause. Constraint violations might indicate application bugs or bad data. Deadlocks might indicate transaction design problems. Timeouts might indicate overload. Tracking errors by type, rather than as a single count, turns a vague "database errors are up" into a specific diagnosis, which is much faster to fix. Database monitoring, like all monitoring, is most useful when it is specific: which database, which query pattern, which error, and how it changed over time.

# Database health at a glance: availability, speed, capacity, errors.
db_health = {"responds": True, "slow_queries": 2,
             "disk_used_percent": 72, "connections_used": 45,
             "connections_max": 100, "errors_last_hour": 3}
print(db_health)

Section 34: Monitoring Disk, Memory and CPU

Every server has finite resources: disk space, memory, and processor capacity, often called CPU. When any of them runs out, the server fails, often suddenly and completely. Monitoring these resources is therefore a basic, non-negotiable part of running a server, and it is also one of the easiest kinds of monitoring to set up, because operating systems report these numbers readily, and many tools collect them automatically. The skill is not in collecting them, but in interpreting them and setting alerts that warn early enough to act.

Disk space is the most urgent resource to watch, because a full disk causes immediate, widespread failure: the application cannot write logs, the database cannot grow, temporary files cannot be created, and many processes crash at once. Disk usage should be monitored as a percentage, with alerts at sensible thresholds, such as 80 percent for a warning, giving time to clean up or expand, and 90 percent for urgent action. It is also useful to monitor disk growth rate, because a disk at 70 percent but growing fast needs attention sooner than a disk at 80 percent that is stable. And you should know what is using the disk: logs, database files, uploads, or caches, so that when space is tight, you know what can be safely cleaned.

Memory, or RAM, is the workspace where running programs hold their data. When memory runs out, programs crash, or the system starts using disk as emergency memory, which is extremely slow, a condition called swapping, which makes everything crawl. Memory usage should be monitored as a percentage, and also per process, so you can see which application is using the most. A gradual memory rise over days, without a matching traffic rise, often indicates a memory leak, where a program allocates memory and never releases it, slowly consuming all available RAM. Memory leaks are a classic slow-burn problem, and monitoring is how you catch them before they crash the server: a graph showing memory rising steadily, day after day, is the signature of a leak.

CPU is the processor's capacity to do work, measured as a percentage of total capacity. High CPU usage means the server is busy, which is normal under load, but sustained high CPU, such as over 90 percent for long periods, means the server cannot keep up, and requests will slow down or queue. CPU should be monitored overall and per process, so you can see whether the load comes from your application, the database, or something else. A sudden CPU spike might indicate a runaway process, an inefficient query, or an attack. A gradual CPU rise might indicate growing traffic, which calls for more capacity. CPU, like other resources, is most informative when compared over time, not as a single snapshot.

Finally, resources interact. A full disk can cause memory problems, because temporary files cannot be written. High memory usage can cause CPU problems, because swapping consumes processor time. A slow disk can make CPU appear idle while requests wait, because processes are blocked on disk, not on computation. That interaction is why dashboards show resources together, and why an investigation checks all of them, not just one.

# Resource health: alert well before any resource reaches 100%.
resources = {"disk_used_percent": 72, "memory_used_percent": 64, "cpu_percent": 45}
for name, value in resources.items():
    status = "ALERT" if value >= 90 else ("watch" if value >= 80 else "ok")
    print(f"{name}: {value}% -> {status}")

Section 35: Monitoring Queues and Workers

Queues and workers, which you learned about in the email and jobs chapter, need monitoring of their own, because they are invisible to users until they fail, and when they fail, the symptoms, such as missing emails or late reports, are confusing and delayed. Queue monitoring focuses on a few numbers that tell you whether background work is flowing healthily: queue depth, oldest job age, processing rate, failure rate, and worker count. Those five numbers, watched together, give you a complete picture of background system health, and they catch most queue problems early.

Queue depth is how many jobs are waiting. A depth of zero means workers are keeping up. A depth that grows steadily means workers are falling behind, because jobs arrive faster than they are processed, or because workers have slowed or stopped. Depth alone does not tell you how bad the problem is, because a depth of a thousand jobs that are all a minute old is a busy system catching up, while a depth of ten jobs that are all a day old is a stuck system. That is why oldest job age matters alongside depth: it tells you how long the longest-waiting user has waited, which is the user experience of the queue.

Processing rate is how many jobs workers complete per minute. Comparing processing rate with arrival rate tells you whether the queue is stable, growing, or shrinking. If arrivals exceed processing, the queue will grow indefinitely, and you need more workers, faster jobs, or fewer jobs. If processing exceeds arrivals, the queue will drain, and workers may be idle, which is fine, but it might indicate over-provisioning. Processing rate also reveals worker health: a sudden drop in processing rate, without a drop in arrivals, means workers slowed or stopped, and it deserves investigation even if depth has not yet grown large.

Failure rate is the share of jobs that fail, and it should be tracked overall and by job type, because different job types fail for different reasons. A rising failure rate for email jobs might indicate a mail server problem. A rising failure rate for report jobs might indicate a data problem. Failure rate should also be split into temporary failures, which retry, and permanent failures, which go to the dead letter queue, because they need different responses. Temporary failures are normal in small numbers. Permanent failures always deserve attention, because they represent work that will never complete without human intervention.

Worker count is how many workers are running. If a worker crashes and does not restart, the count drops, and the remaining workers must handle the load, which may cause depth to grow. Monitoring worker count, and alerting when it drops below the expected number, catches worker failures quickly, before their effects accumulate. It also helps with capacity planning: if worker count is at its maximum and depth still grows, you need faster jobs or a different design, not just more workers. Queue and worker monitoring, like other monitoring, is about watching the flow of work, not just the state of one component, and it is what keeps background systems reliable.

# Queue health: depth, oldest age, processing rate, failures, workers.
queue = {"depth": 120, "oldest_age_minutes": 25, "processed_per_min": 40,
         "failed_last_hour": 5, "workers_running": 2, "workers_expected": 3}
print(queue)
print("ALERT: a worker is missing" if queue["workers_running"] < queue["workers_expected"] else "Workers ok")

Section 36: Monitoring Email and External Services

Your application depends on services it does not control: mail providers, payment services, maps, and other outside systems. When one of those services fails or slows down, your users suffer, even though your own code is correct, and you need monitoring to tell you whether the problem is yours or theirs, and how badly it affects users. Monitoring external services is therefore a distinct skill, with its own checks and its own responses, because you cannot fix the outside service, but you can respond to its failure, by retrying, falling back, or telling users honestly.

The basic checks for an external service are availability, latency, and error rate, measured from your application's perspective. Availability asks whether the service answers your calls at all. Latency asks how long its answers take, because a service that answers slowly can be as harmful as one that does not answer, if your requests wait for it. Error rate asks how often its answers are errors, such as rate limit rejections or server errors. Those three numbers, tracked over time, tell you whether a service is healthy, degrading, or down, and they let you distinguish a service problem from a problem in your own code: if your error rate rises only for calls to one service, that service is the likely cause.

For email specifically, the numbers to watch are the ones from the earlier email chapter: queue depth, delivery rate, bounce rate, and complaint rate, plus the mail provider's own status, if it publishes one. A rising bounce rate might indicate a stale address list. A rising complaint rate might indicate unwanted mail. A delivery rate drop might indicate a reputation problem or a provider outage. Email monitoring should also include end-to-end checks: periodically sending a test message to a monitored mailbox and confirming it arrives, which verifies the whole path, not just the provider's API. That end-to-end check catches problems that API metrics miss, such as authentication failures or filtering changes.

When an external service fails, your response should be planned, not improvised. For temporary failures, retry with backoff, as covered in the jobs chapter, and queue work for later. For prolonged failures, activate a fallback, if one exists, such as an alternative provider, or a degraded feature that does not need the service. And communicate with users when the failure affects them: a message saying "email delivery is delayed due to a provider issue, and we are retrying" is far better than silence, because it tells users the problem is known and being handled. That communication is part of monitoring, because monitoring is not just about numbers. It is about the actions numbers trigger.

Finally, monitor your usage of external services, not just their health. Many services have rate limits, quotas, and costs, and exceeding them can cause failures or unexpected bills. Track how many calls you make, how close you are to limits, and how much you spend, and alert before limits are reached, not after. A service that stops answering because you exceeded its quota looks like an outage, but its cause is your own usage, and it is preventable with usage monitoring. External services are partnerships, and monitoring both their health and your usage is how you manage those partnerships responsibly.

# External service health from your application's perspective.
service = {"name": "mail provider", "available": True,
           "avg_latency_ms": 320, "error_rate": 0.01, "quota_used_percent": 62}
print(service)

Section 37: Error Tracking and Crash Reports

Error tracking is the practice of collecting, grouping, and analysing errors from your application, so that you can see which errors happen most, which affect most users, and which are new, without manually searching logs for each one. While logs record errors as lines, error tracking turns those lines into a structured view: this error, with this message and this traceback, happened this many times, for these users, starting at this time, after this deployment. That grouping is powerful, because it turns a flood of similar log lines into a single issue to fix, prioritised by impact.

The key idea is grouping by similarity. The same bug, happening for many users, produces many log lines that differ only in details, such as user ids or timestamps, but share the same error type, message, and traceback location. Error tracking groups those lines into one issue, counting occurrences and affected users, so you can see at a glance which bug is most urgent. Without grouping, a bug affecting a thousand users looks like a thousand separate problems, and a rare bug affecting one user looks the same as a common one. With grouping, priorities become clear: fix the error affecting the most users first, and investigate new errors, which might indicate a recent regression, promptly.

Error tracking also captures context that plain logs might miss, especially for crashes. When an application crashes, it may not have time to write a complete log, but an error tracking system can capture the crash report, including the traceback, the state of the request, the user's environment, and the version of the code, and send it to a central store before the process dies. That crash report is often the only record of what happened, and it is invaluable for fixing crashes that cannot be reproduced, because they depend on specific data or conditions. For mobile and desktop applications, crash reporting is essential, because the application runs on the user's device, far from your logs. For servers, it complements logs, by grouping and prioritising what logs record.

There are also practices that make error tracking effective. Errors should be grouped, but not over-grouped: two different bugs with similar messages should not be merged into one issue, or the fix for one will be thought to fix both. Errors should include enough context to reproduce them, such as the request id, user id, and relevant data, without including secrets. And error tracking should be reviewed regularly, with old, fixed errors marked resolved, and new errors triaged promptly. An error tracker full of ignored, ancient issues is as useless as a log file nobody reads. Its value comes from the habit of reviewing it and acting on what it shows.

Finally, error tracking connects to alerting. A new error type appearing after a deployment is a strong signal of a regression, and it should alert, even if its total count is still low. A sudden rise in a known error's frequency is also alert-worthy. Those alerts, based on error tracking rather than raw log volume, are precise and actionable, and they catch regressions faster than generic error rate alerts, which might hide a new error in a sea of old ones.

# Error tracking groups identical errors into one issue with a count.
errors = ["database timeout", "database timeout", "invalid email", "database timeout"]
from collections import Counter
print(Counter(errors))
print("Most urgent: fix the error affecting the most users first")

Section 38: Status Pages: Telling Users What Is Wrong

A status page is a public page that tells users whether your service is working, and if not, what is wrong and what you are doing about it. It is one of the most valuable communication tools a service can have, because it answers the question users ask first during an outage: "is it just me, or is it down?" Without a status page, users flood support, post on social media, and guess, which increases confusion and workload during an already stressful time. With a status page, users can check for themselves, see that the problem is known, and wait for updates, which reduces support load and preserves trust.

A good status page has a few essential parts. First, an overall status, such as "all systems operational" or "partial outage", displayed prominently, so users know immediately whether there is a problem. Second, a breakdown by component, such as website, API, email delivery, and database, so users can see whether the part they care about is affected, even if other parts are fine. Third, a history of incidents, showing past outages and their resolutions, which demonstrates honesty and lets users judge reliability over time. And fourth, updates during an incident, posted regularly, saying what is known, what is being done, and when the next update is expected, even if there is no new information, because silence during an outage feels like abandonment.

Status pages should be hosted separately from the service they report on, because a status page on the same server will go down when the server does, which is exactly when users need it most. A separate hosting, on a different service, ensures the status page remains available during an outage, which is its whole purpose. That separation is a design requirement, not a nice extra, and it is worth planning from the start, even for a small service, because an outage is the worst time to discover your status page is also down.

There is also a question of honesty. A status page that always says "all systems operational", even during obvious outages, destroys trust faster than no status page at all, because users can see the service is down, and the page's denial feels like dishonesty. A status page that promptly reports problems, even embarrassing ones, and follows up with honest postmortems, builds trust, because users see a team that notices problems, communicates openly, and learns from them. Honesty during outages is not just ethical. It is practical, because users who trust your communication will wait patiently, while users who do not will leave.

Finally, a status page is also useful during normal operation, not just outages. It shows users that the service is monitored, that its history is transparent, and that someone is responsible for its health. It can also be used to announce planned maintenance in advance, so users are not surprised by downtime. Planned maintenance, announced with a time window and a reason, is experienced very differently from an unexpected outage, and a status page is the natural place for that announcement. A status page, maintained honestly and updated promptly, is a small investment that pays large dividends in user trust, especially during the difficult moments when trust is tested most.

# A status page in miniature: overall status plus per-component status.
status = {"overall": "partial outage",
          "components": {"website": "operational", "api": "operational",
                         "email delivery": "degraded"}}
print(status)

Section 39: Incident Response in Plain Words

An incident is an event that disrupts your service for users, such as an outage, a major slowdown, or a data problem, and incident response is the structured way a team handles it, from detection to resolution to learning. Without structure, incidents become chaotic: people duplicate work, communicate inconsistently, and fix symptoms rather than causes, while users wait. With structure, even a small team can handle incidents calmly and effectively, because everyone knows their role, what to do first, and how to communicate. Incident response is a skill, and it can be practised, even before a real incident occurs.

The first phase is detection and acknowledgement. Monitoring alerts you, or a user reports a problem, and someone acknowledges that an incident is happening, and takes ownership of coordinating the response. That ownership matters: without a coordinator, several people may investigate separately, or nobody may investigate, assuming someone else is. The coordinator does not need to fix the problem themselves. Their job is to organise: confirm the impact, gather the right people, and ensure communication happens. In a small team, the coordinator and the fixer may be the same person, but the roles should still be distinguished, because coordinating and fixing require different attention.

The second phase is investigation and mitigation. Investigation asks what is wrong and why, using logs, metrics, and traces, as covered earlier. Mitigation asks how to restore service quickly, even if the underlying cause is not yet fully understood. Mitigation might mean restarting a service, rolling back a deployment, scaling up resources, or disabling a broken feature. The goal of mitigation is to stop user harm as fast as possible, even with a temporary fix, while investigation continues in parallel. A common mistake is to focus only on the root cause, while users continue to suffer, when a quick mitigation, such as a rollback, would have restored service in minutes. Mitigate first, understand fully second, is a good rule for most incidents.

The third phase is communication. Users should be told, through a status page or other channel, that there is a problem, what is affected, and when to expect the next update. Internal communication should keep the team aligned: what is known, what is being tried, and who is doing what. Communication should be regular, even if there is no new information, because silence breeds speculation. And communication should be honest: do not claim a fix is coming in five minutes if it is not, and do not hide the severity of the problem. Honest, regular updates preserve trust, even during a serious outage.

The fourth phase is resolution and follow-up. Resolution means the service is restored and verified, not just that a fix was attempted. Verification matters: check metrics, check user reports, and confirm the problem is truly gone, not just quieter. Follow-up means writing a postmortem, covered next, and fixing the underlying cause and the process gaps that allowed the incident, so it does not recur. An incident without follow-up is a lesson wasted, and the same problem will return. Incident response is a cycle: detect, mitigate, communicate, resolve, learn, and improve, and each cycle should make the next incident less likely or less severe.

# Incident response in four phases: detect, mitigate, communicate, learn.
phases = ["1. Detect and acknowledge", "2. Investigate and mitigate",
          "3. Communicate regularly", "4. Resolve and learn"]
for phase in phases:
    print(phase)

Section 40: Writing a Postmortem

A postmortem, also called a post-incident review, is a document written after an incident, describing what happened, why, how it was handled, and what will change to prevent a recurrence. It is one of the most valuable learning tools in operations, because it turns a painful experience into lasting improvement, for the whole team, not just the people who were involved. A team that writes postmortems learns from every incident. A team that does not repeats them, because the lessons live only in individual memories, which fade, and in fixes that address symptoms, not causes.

A good postmortem has a standard structure. It starts with a summary: what happened, when, how long it lasted, and who was affected, in plain language, so that anyone, including non-technical readers, can understand the impact. It then gives a timeline: the key events in order, from detection to resolution, with times, so the sequence can be followed. It then analyses the root cause, or causes, distinguishing the trigger, the immediate cause, from the underlying conditions that allowed it, such as a missing alert, a fragile deployment, or a capacity limit. And it ends with action items: specific, owned, dated changes to prevent recurrence, such as adding a monitor, fixing a bug, or improving a runbook. That structure, summary, timeline, causes, actions, is the backbone of a useful postmortem.

The most important cultural rule for postmortems is that they are blameless. The goal is to understand how the system allowed a mistake, not to punish the person who made it. People make mistakes, especially under pressure, and a culture that punishes mistakes drives them underground, where they cannot be learned from. A blameless postmortem asks "how did our processes, tools, and safeguards allow this to happen?" rather than "who caused this?" That question leads to systemic fixes, such as better alerts, safer deployments, or clearer runbooks, which prevent the same mistake by anyone, rather than just warning one person to be more careful. Blamelessness is not about avoiding accountability. It is about placing accountability where it belongs: on the system, not the individual.

Postmortems should also be shared and stored, so the whole team can learn from them, and so future incidents can be compared with past ones. A postmortem filed away and never read is a wasted effort. A postmortem discussed in a team meeting, with its action items tracked to completion, is a living improvement. Action items deserve the same attention as feature work: they should have owners, deadlines, and follow-up, because an action item that is never completed is a promise broken, and the incident it was meant to prevent may recur. Tracking action items to completion is what turns postmortems from documents into improvements.

Finally, write postmortems even for small incidents, and even for near misses, where a problem was caught before users were affected. Small incidents teach the same lessons as large ones, with less pain, and near misses reveal weaknesses before they cause harm. A team that only writes postmortems for major outages misses most learning opportunities. A team that writes them regularly builds a habit of reflection that makes major outages rarer over time.

# A postmortem structure: summary, timeline, causes, action items.
postmortem = {"summary": "Email delivery delayed 2 hours on 30 Oct",
              "timeline": ["14:00 alert fired", "14:20 worker restarted",
                           "16:00 queue drained"],
              "cause": "Worker crashed, no alert on worker count",
              "actions": ["Alert on worker count", "Auto-restart workers"]}
print(postmortem["cause"])

Section 41: Synthetic Monitoring: Pretending to Be a User

Synthetic monitoring means simulating user behaviour with automated scripts, often called synthetic checks or probes, that regularly perform key actions on your service, such as loading the homepage, signing in, or creating a task, and reporting whether those actions succeeded and how long they took. The word synthetic means the traffic is artificial, created by the monitor, not by real users. That artificiality is a strength, not a weakness, because it gives you a consistent, repeatable measurement of user experience, from known locations, at known times, regardless of whether real users happen to be active. A service with no real users at 3 a.m. can still be monitored synthetically at 3 a.m., and a failure will be caught, rather than waiting for the first real user to discover it.

Synthetic checks are usually built around the most important user journeys, sometimes called critical paths. For a task application, those might be: load the homepage, sign in, create a task, and list tasks. Each journey is scripted as a series of requests, with expected results at each step, and the script runs every few minutes from one or more locations. If any step fails, or takes too long, an alert fires. That approach tests the service the way users experience it, end to end, rather than testing individual components in isolation. A service can pass all its component health checks while still failing a user journey, because the failure is in how components interact, and only an end-to-end check catches that.

Synthetic monitoring has limitations worth knowing. It tests scripted journeys, not every possible user action, so a failure in an unscripted feature will not be caught. It generates artificial traffic, which can pollute analytics unless synthetic requests are marked and excluded. And it tests from the monitor's locations, which may differ from users' locations, so it may miss region-specific problems. Those limitations mean it should be combined with real user monitoring, covered next, and with component monitoring.

Designing good synthetic checks follows a few principles. Keep them focused on critical journeys, not on every feature, because each check adds maintenance: when a feature changes, its check must change too. Make them independent of changing test data, by creating their own test accounts, or by using read-only actions where possible. Run them frequently enough to catch problems quickly, but not so frequently that they add significant load. And require a few consecutive failures before alerting, so a single blip does not cause noise.

Finally, synthetic monitoring is also useful for verifying fixes and deployments. After a deployment, synthetic checks confirm that critical journeys still work, giving confidence that the release did not break the basics, before real users encounter it. After an incident, synthetic checks confirm that the fix restored the journey, not just a component. That verification role makes synthetic monitoring a natural part of deployment and incident workflows, not just a background monitor. A service with good synthetic checks has a safety net that catches user-facing failures early, consistently, and regardless of traffic, which is a valuable complement to every other form of monitoring.

# A synthetic check: script a critical journey, expect success at each step.
journey = [("load homepage", True), ("sign in", True), ("create task", True)]
failed = [step for step, ok in journey if not ok]
print("Journey ok" if not failed else f"ALERT: failed steps: {failed}")

Section 42: Real User Monitoring in Plain Words

Real user monitoring, often shortened to RUM, measures the experience of actual users, as they use your service, rather than the experience of automated scripts. It works by collecting small measurements from real browsers or apps, such as how long pages took to load, how long requests took, and whether errors occurred, and sending those measurements back to be aggregated. RUM answers the question synthetic monitoring cannot: what are real users actually experiencing, on their real devices, networks, and locations, with all their variety? That variety is both RUM's strength and its challenge, because real user experience varies enormously, and understanding that variation is the point.

The main advantage of RUM is that it reflects reality, including the long tail of slow devices, slow networks, and distant locations that synthetic monitors, running on fast machines in data centres, never see. A page that loads in half a second for a synthetic monitor in a data centre might take five seconds for a real user on a slow mobile connection, and RUM will show that, while synthetic monitoring will not. RUM also reveals patterns by segment: users in one country slower than users in another, users on one browser slower than users on another, users on mobile slower than users on desktop. Those segments guide optimisation: if mobile users are slowest, mobile performance deserves attention, even if desktop numbers look fine.

RUM also has limitations. It only measures users who actually visit, so it cannot detect a total outage that prevents users from loading anything, because no measurements arrive at all. That is why RUM must be combined with synthetic monitoring and uptime checks, which detect outages even with no real traffic. RUM data is also noisy, because real user conditions vary wildly, and a single slow measurement might reflect the user's network, not your service. Aggregation is therefore essential: look at distributions and percentiles over many users, not at individual measurements, and look for patterns across segments, not for single outliers. Averages alone hide the slowest users, just as with other latency metrics, so percentiles matter here too.

Privacy is an important consideration for RUM. Because it collects data from real users' devices, it must be designed to minimise personal data: collect timings and error counts, not page contents, not personal information, and not anything that could identify an individual without need. Users should be told, in general terms, that performance data is collected, and the data should be aggregated and anonymised promptly. RUM done carelessly can become a privacy problem. RUM done carefully, with minimal data and clear purpose, is a legitimate and valuable tool for improving user experience, and it is standard practice for serious web services.

Finally, RUM and synthetic monitoring complement each other. Synthetic monitoring gives consistent, controlled measurements, available around the clock, that catch outages and regressions quickly. RUM gives real-world measurements, with all their variety, that show what users truly experience and where optimisation will help most. Together, they answer both questions: is the service working, and how well is it working for real people?

# RUM in miniature: real measurements, aggregated by percentile, not averaged away.
measurements = [200, 250, 300, 400, 500, 800, 1500, 5000]
measurements.sort()
p95 = measurements[int(len(measurements) * 0.95) - 1]
print(f"Real users: median {measurements[len(measurements)//2]}ms, p95 {p95}ms")

Section 43: Monitoring Security Events

Security monitoring is the practice of watching for signs of attack, misuse, or compromise, and it deserves a place in this chapter because many security incidents are first visible in logs and metrics, long before their damage is obvious. A login system that suddenly sees many failed attempts from one address, a user account that suddenly downloads far more data than usual, or an endpoint that suddenly receives strange, malformed requests, are all signals that something may be wrong. Monitoring for those signals, and acting on them, is part of defending a service, not just part of running it, and it is a responsibility every back-end developer shares.

The events worth monitoring for security fall into a few categories. Authentication events: failed logins, password reset requests, and new device logins, especially in large numbers or from unusual locations. Authorisation events: attempts to access resources a user should not access, which may indicate probing for weaknesses. Input events: requests with suspicious patterns, such as attempts to inject code, or requests for files that should not exist, which may indicate scanning for vulnerabilities. And data events: unusually large downloads, bulk exports, or access to many accounts in a short time, which may indicate data theft. Each category has a normal baseline, and deviations from that baseline are the signals to watch.

Security monitoring differs from ordinary monitoring in its response. An ordinary alert might lead to a fix or a scaling action. A security alert might lead to blocking an address, disabling an account, or starting an investigation, and those actions have consequences for users, so they must be taken carefully, with evidence, not on a single suspicious line. A single failed login is normal. A thousand failed logins in a minute from one address is an attack pattern, and it justifies blocking. The distinction is in aggregation and thresholds, just as with other alerts, but the stakes are higher, because a wrong block harms a legitimate user, while a missed attack harms many. Security alerts therefore need clear runbooks, with steps for verifying, containing, and escalating, before action is taken.

There is also a legal and ethical dimension. Security logs contain sensitive information about user behaviour, and they must be protected, retained appropriately, and accessed only by those who need them. Monitoring for security should not become surveillance of legitimate users: the goal is to detect attacks, not to track every action of every user in detail. Collect the minimum data needed to detect the patterns above, aggregate it promptly, and delete raw details after a retention period, unless an investigation requires them. That restraint is part of responsible security monitoring, and it aligns with the privacy principles covered earlier in this chapter.

Finally, security monitoring should be connected to the rest of your monitoring. A security incident often causes ordinary symptoms too: error spikes, latency rises, or traffic surges, which ordinary alerts may notice first. Conversely, a security alert may explain an ordinary symptom, such as a traffic surge caused by an attack. Keeping security monitoring integrated, rather than siloed, helps both sides: ordinary monitoring provides context for security alerts, and security monitoring explains ordinary anomalies.

# Security signal: many failed logins from one address in a short time.
failed_logins = {"192.0.2.10": 120, "192.0.2.20": 2}
for address, count in failed_logins.items():
    if count > 50:
        print(f"SECURITY ALERT: {count} failed logins from {address}")

Section 44: Log Retention and Cost

Keeping logs has a cost, in storage, in processing, and in complexity, and that cost grows with volume and retention time. Log retention is the policy that decides how long logs are kept before they are deleted or archived, and setting it well is a balance between usefulness and cost. Keeping logs forever is expensive and risky, because storage grows without bound, searching becomes slow, and old logs accumulate personal data with no purpose. Deleting logs too quickly destroys evidence, because incidents are often investigated days or weeks after they occur, and the logs needed may already be gone. A retention policy is the deliberate choice between those extremes.

Different logs deserve different retention, based on their purpose. Debugging logs, which record routine operations, are usually needed for days or weeks, long enough to investigate recent incidents, but not for months. Audit logs, which record security-sensitive actions such as logins, password changes, and permission changes, may be needed for months or years, for accountability and compliance, and they should be stored more securely and for longer. Metrics, being compact, can be kept for months or years cheaply, and their long-term trends are valuable. Error tracking data, being grouped and summarised, can also be kept longer than raw logs, because it is smaller and more actionable. Matching retention to purpose, rather than using one rule for everything, is the efficient approach.

Cost is not only about disk space. Searching large log stores is slower and more expensive, because every query must scan more data. Processing logs, such as indexing them, consumes resources that grow with volume. Those costs are why larger systems summarise logs over time: detailed logs are kept briefly, then aggregated into summaries, such as daily counts by error type, which are kept longer, while the raw detail is deleted. That tiered approach preserves trends and counts, while discarding bulky detail rarely needed after a few weeks.

Retention also has a privacy dimension. Personal data in logs should not be kept longer than necessary, because every day it is kept is another day it could leak, and because users may have rights to have their data deleted. A retention policy that deletes debugging logs after thirty days, for example, automatically limits the personal data exposure, without requiring manual cleanup. That automatic deletion is a feature, not a loss, because it enforces data minimisation by default. When setting retention, ask not only "how long might we need this?" but also "how long should we keep this personal data?" and let the shorter answer win, unless a legal or audit requirement says otherwise.

Finally, write the retention policy down, configure your systems to enforce it automatically, and review it periodically. A policy that exists only in someone's head will not be followed when that person leaves, or when a new log source is added. Automatic enforcement, through rotation, archiving, and deletion jobs, ensures the policy actually happens, even when nobody is thinking about it. And periodic review ensures the policy still matches reality, as log volumes grow, regulations change, and the service's needs evolve. Retention is a small policy with large consequences for cost, usefulness, and privacy, and it deserves the same deliberate attention as any other part of your monitoring design.

# Tiered retention: detail briefly, summaries longer, audit longest.
retention = {"debugging logs": "30 days", "error summaries": "1 year",
             "audit logs": "2 years", "metrics": "1 year"}
for kind, period in retention.items():
    print(kind, "->", period)

Section 45: Centralised Logging in Plain Words

Centralised logging means collecting logs from all your servers and services into one place, where they can be searched, analysed, and monitored together. For a single-server application, centralised logging may seem unnecessary, because all logs are already in one place. But as soon as you have more than one server, or more than one service, logs scatter, and finding the lines for one incident means logging into each machine separately, searching each file, and piecing the story together by hand. Centralised logging solves that by bringing the pieces together automatically, so one search finds everything, regardless of which server produced it.

The basic architecture is simple: each server runs a small agent, or uses a logging handler, that forwards log lines to a central store, as they are produced, or in small batches. The central store indexes the lines, so they can be searched by time, level, service, request id, or any structured field, and it provides a search interface, dashboards, and alerts based on log patterns. That index is what makes centralised logging powerful: searching a million indexed lines takes seconds, while searching a million unindexed lines, across many files, takes far longer and is far more error-prone. Indexing turns logs from files to be read into data to be queried, which is the same shift as structured logging, at a larger scale.

Centralised logging has costs and risks that should be weighed. It adds a dependency: if the central store is down, logs may be lost, unless agents buffer them locally and retry, which good agents do. It adds cost, because storing and indexing logs at scale is not free, and volume should be managed by logging sensibly, not by logging everything at DEBUG level in production. And it concentrates sensitive data in one place, which must be protected with access controls, encryption, and retention policies, because a breach of the log store exposes the activity of the whole system. Those costs are real, but for a multi-server system, they are usually outweighed by the benefit of being able to investigate incidents quickly and completely, which is hard without centralisation.

For a small system, centralised logging can start simply. Forwarding logs from a few servers to one server's files, with a shared search tool, captures most of the benefit at low cost. As the system grows, a dedicated log service becomes worthwhile. The progression should be driven by need: when searching across servers becomes painful, centralise. When centralised search becomes slow or expensive, tune what you log and how long you keep it. Centralisation is a spectrum, and the right point on it depends on your scale and your pain.

Finally, centralised logging enables the correlation and alerting practices covered earlier. With all logs in one place, a search by request id finds lines from every service. An alert on a log pattern, such as a sudden rise in a specific error, can fire from the central store, without needing to watch each server separately. And dashboards built on central logs show the whole system at once, not just one server's view. Centralised logging is therefore not just a convenience. It is the foundation that makes advanced log practices possible, and it is worth building, in simple form, as soon as your system outgrows a single server.

# Centralised logging: one search finds lines from every service.
central_logs = [
    {"service": "web", "request_id": "req-1", "message": "Request received"},
    {"service": "db", "request_id": "req-1", "message": "Query completed"},
    {"service": "mail", "request_id": "req-1", "message": "Email sent"},
]
story = [l for l in central_logs if l["request_id"] == "req-1"]
print(f"One search found {len(story)} lines across services")

Section 46: Monitoring in Development Versus Production

Monitoring in development and monitoring in production serve different purposes, and confusing them leads to either noisy development or blind production. In development, you are building and debugging, so you need detail: DEBUG logs, verbose output, and the ability to inspect every step. Noise is acceptable, because you are watching closely, and the audience is yourself, or a small team, who understand the context. In production, you are running a service for real users, so you need signal: INFO logs and above, meaningful metrics, and alerts that fire only for real problems. Noise is harmful, because it hides real problems and fatigues responders. The same system, with different settings, serves both purposes, but the settings must differ deliberately.

The most visible difference is log level. In development, the level is usually DEBUG, so you see detailed flow, variable values, and step-by-step behaviour. In production, the level is usually INFO, or sometimes WARNING, so you see the narrative without the detail, and storage and processing costs stay manageable. That difference is controlled by configuration, not by code changes, which is why logging configuration should be external, such as an environment variable, not hard-coded. A system that requires a code change to change its log level is a system that will be run with the wrong level, because changing code to debug production is slow and risky.

Another difference is what is monitored. In development, you might monitor everything, because you are learning how the system behaves and you want maximum visibility. In production, you monitor what matters for users and for health, because monitoring everything creates cost and noise without insight. That does not mean production monitoring is less thorough. It means it is more selective: the golden signals, key business metrics, and critical dependencies, watched closely, rather than every internal detail, watched loosely. Selectivity in production is a discipline, and it comes from learning, in development, which numbers actually predict problems.

A third difference is who sees the data and how quickly they must act. In development, the audience is the developer, who can inspect data at leisure and act when convenient. In production, the audience includes on-call responders, who must act quickly, and users, who see status pages, so data must be presented clearly, with alerts and dashboards designed for quick judgement. That difference shapes tooling: development tools prioritise detail and flexibility, production tools prioritise clarity, reliability, and alerting. Using development tools in production, such as reading raw log files by hand during an incident, is slow and error-prone compared to using production tools, such as centralised search and dashboards.

Finally, be careful about differences between development and production environments that affect monitoring itself. A feature that works in development, where traffic is low and data is small, may behave differently in production, where traffic is high and data is large, and monitoring must catch those differences. That is why production monitoring should include checks that development cannot provide, such as load, real user experience, and external service health. And it is why staging environments, which mimic production, are valuable: they let you test monitoring, alerts, and dashboards before real users depend on them. Monitoring is part of the system, and it deserves testing in realistic conditions, just like any other feature.

# Log level by environment: detailed in development, signal in production.
import os

env = os.environ.get("APP_ENV", "development")
level = "DEBUG" if env == "development" else "INFO"
print(f"Environment: {env}, log level: {level}")

Section 47: A Small Monitoring Setup Design

You have now met the pieces of logging and monitoring. This section puts them together into a small, complete setup design that you could actually build for a modest application, using Python's standard library plus simple tools, and that embodies the lessons of this chapter. The design is deliberately modest: it covers logs, health, metrics, dashboards, and alerts, without requiring expensive services or complex infrastructure. Modest designs are valuable because they can be built, understood, and maintained, and then grown as needs increase. A grand monitoring system that never gets built helps nobody.

Start with logging. Use Python's logging module, configured once at startup, with a named logger per module, a format that includes timestamp in UTC, level, logger name, request id, and message, and two handlers: one writing to the terminal in development, and one writing to a rotating file in production. Set the level by environment: DEBUG in development, INFO in production. Add structured fields for key events, such as user id and job id, where they help searching, and enforce the rule of no secrets and minimal personal data, with a review of new log lines. That logging setup, alone, gives you a searchable, durable record of what your system does, which is the foundation for everything else.

Next, add health and metrics. Provide a /health endpoint that checks the server and its database, returning a simple status. Provide a /metrics endpoint, or a simple metrics module, that reports request counts, error counts, average and 95th percentile response times, queue depth, and oldest job age, updated in memory as requests are handled. Those numbers cover the golden signals for a small system, and they can be read by a human, or by a simple external monitor that polls them regularly. Keep metric updates fast and in memory, as covered earlier, so they do not slow requests.

Next, add external monitoring and alerting. Use an outside service, or a simple script on another machine, to call your /health endpoint every minute, and to check your /metrics endpoint for error rate and queue depth, alerting you by message or email when thresholds are crossed for a sustained period. Keep alerts few and symptom-based: service down, error rate high, queue growing, disk nearly full. Document a first response for each alert, even briefly, so that a future you, woken at night, knows what to check first. That external monitor, plus a few good alerts, is the difference between a system that fails silently and one that asks for help promptly.

Finally, add the human practices. Review logs and metrics weekly, even briefly, looking for trends, slow endpoints, and rising errors. Maintain a simple status page, hosted separately, updated during incidents. Write postmortems for incidents, blamelessly, with action items tracked to completion. And review your alerts monthly, removing noisy ones and adding missing ones. Those practices cost little time, but they are what turn tools into a monitoring culture, where problems are noticed early, understood quickly, and learned from. A small setup, used consistently, will serve a modest application well, and it will teach you what to add next as the application grows.

# A small monitoring setup: logs, health, metrics, and alerts together.
print("Logs: rotating file, INFO in production, request ids included")
print("Health: /health endpoint, polled every minute from outside")
print("Metrics: requests, errors, latency p95, queue depth, disk use")
print("Alerts: few, symptom-based, with documented first responses")

Section 48: Choosing What to Monitor First

When you are starting monitoring from scratch, the number of things you could monitor is overwhelming, and the temptation is either to monitor everything, which creates noise and cost, or to monitor nothing, which leaves you blind. Choosing what to monitor first is therefore a prioritisation skill, and this section gives you a practical order, based on what harms users most and what is cheapest to check. Follow this order, and you will have a useful monitoring setup quickly, without drowning in dashboards and alerts.

First, monitor whether the service is up. A health check, polled from outside, with an alert on failure, is the single most valuable monitor, because it catches total outages, which are the worst user experience, and it is cheap to set up. If you monitor nothing else, monitor uptime. Second, monitor error rate, because errors are direct user harm, and a rising error rate indicates a problem even when the service is technically up. An alert on error rate, with a sensible threshold, catches regressions, dependency failures, and data problems early, before they affect many users.

Third, monitor latency, specifically the 95th percentile response time, because slowness harms users almost as much as errors, and because latency rises often precede failures, as a system struggles under load or with a slow dependency. A latency alert, set outside normal variation, gives you early warning of degradation. Fourth, monitor saturation for your most constrained resource, which is usually disk space, because a full disk causes abrupt, total failure, and because disk alerts are easy and reliable. Add memory and queue depth next, as they are the next most likely to cause sudden problems.

Fifth, monitor your most important user journey, with a synthetic check, such as signing in and creating a task, because a service can be up, with low errors and good latency on average, while a critical feature is broken. That journey check catches feature-level failures that component monitors miss. Sixth, monitor your critical dependencies, such as the database and the mail provider, with availability and error checks, because their failures become your failures, and knowing which dependency failed speeds diagnosis enormously. Those six, uptime, errors, latency, saturation, critical journey, and dependencies, form a solid first monitoring set for almost any application.

After those, add monitoring based on your specific system and its history. If queues matter, monitor queue depth and age. If email matters, monitor delivery and bounce rates. If security matters, monitor authentication failures. Let incidents guide you: after each incident, ask what monitor would have caught it earlier, and add that monitor. That incident-driven growth ensures your monitoring evolves to cover your real risks, not imagined ones, and it keeps the set focused on problems you have actually experienced, which are the problems most likely to recur.

# Monitoring priorities, in order: uptime first, then errors, latency, and so on.
priorities = ["1. Uptime", "2. Error rate", "3. Latency p95",
              "4. Disk and saturation", "5. Critical user journey",
              "6. Critical dependencies"]
for p in priorities:
    print(p)

Section 49: Common Monitoring Mistakes

Like logging, monitoring has its classic mistakes, and this section collects them so you can avoid the most common ones. They are familiar by now, because they echo the logging mistakes, but applied to the live, alerting side of operations, where their consequences are felt more urgently, in the form of missed incidents or exhausted responders. Reading this list is a cheap way to learn from others' outages instead of your own.

The first mistake is monitoring nothing, or monitoring only uptime, and assuming that "up" means "working", while features break silently underneath. The fix is the prioritised set from the previous section, including errors, latency, and critical journeys. The second mistake is monitoring everything, with hundreds of graphs and alerts, so that real problems hide in noise and responders ignore the system. The fix is selectivity: monitor what reflects user experience and system health, and prune the rest. The third mistake is alerting on every minor variation, causing alert fatigue, which we covered in detail. The fix is thresholds based on real data, sustained durations, and symptom-based alerts.

The fourth mistake is alerting without information or ownership, so an alert fires, nobody knows what it means or who should act, and it is ignored. The fix is specific alerts, with context and a runbook, and a named owner for each. The fifth mistake is dashboards nobody looks at, filled with graphs whose purpose nobody can explain. The fix is dashboards designed for an audience and a purpose, reviewed and pruned regularly. The sixth mistake is measuring averages only, hiding the slowest users behind a comfortable mean. The fix is percentiles, especially the 95th and 99th, for latency and other distributions.

The seventh mistake is no external perspective: monitoring only from inside the system, so that when the system fails, monitoring fails with it, and nobody is alerted. The fix is external probes, from a different machine or service, for uptime and health. The eighth mistake is ignoring saturation until it becomes a crisis, such as alerting on disk at 99 percent, when it is too late to act calmly. The fix is early thresholds, such as 80 percent, with growth rate considered. The ninth mistake is never testing monitoring itself: alerts that have never fired, dashboards that have never been used in an incident, and runbooks that have never been followed, which may fail when they are most needed. The fix is to test alerts deliberately, and to use dashboards in practice, not just in theory.

The tenth mistake is treating monitoring as a one-time setup, rather than a living system that must evolve with the application, its traffic, and its incidents. The fix is regular review: of alerts, dashboards, thresholds, and retention, informed by incidents and by changes in the system. None of these mistakes comes from bad intentions. They come from enthusiasm, from copying a setup without understanding it, or from setting up monitoring and moving on. The antidote is the habit this chapter has tried to build: monitor deliberately, alert sparingly and specifically, review regularly, and learn from every incident. A team that does that will have monitoring that helps, rather than monitoring that harasses or deceives.

Build a small logging and monitoring setup in Python using only the standard library: a logging configuration with a rotating file handler, UTC timestamps, and a request id included in every log line; a http.server application with a /health endpoint and a /metrics endpoint reporting request count, error count, average response time, and queue depth; and a simple alert check that prints an alert when the error rate exceeds 5 percent for a sustained period or when disk usage exceeds 80 percent. Demonstrate it with a few successful and failing requests, show the log file contents, show the metrics output, and show which alerts, if any, fired and why.

  3. What are the four golden signals, and why is saturation an early warning while the others describe current problems?
  4. Design an alert for a failing email queue: state the symptom you would alert on, the threshold, the sustained duration, and the first response step.
