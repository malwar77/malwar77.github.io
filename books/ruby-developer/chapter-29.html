Chapter 29: Debugging, Logging and Diagnostics

Section 1: What Debugging Really Means

This section is about what debugging really means. At first the name may sound technical, but the idea underneath it is ordinary and practical, and you have probably already met it in everyday life without calling it by this name. In Ruby, what debugging really means is a tool you reach for when you want your program to behave clearly and predictably, rather than guessing or hoping that the data will always arrive in perfect form. Take a moment to say the idea in your own words before you look at any code, because a clear plain words picture in your head will make every example that follows easier to follow, easier to remember, and easier to adapt when your own program needs something slightly different. As you read, notice how this idea connects to the wider theme of debugging, logging and diagnostics, and ask yourself where it would help in a program you already understand, such as a task list, a note keeper, or a small tool that works with text and files.

Debugging is detective work with a calm method. A bug is simply a difference between what you expected and what the program actually did. Your job is to narrow that difference down until you can point to one line, one value, or one decision that went wrong. Guessing and changing many things at once feels fast, but it often hides the real cause. Changing one thing, checking the result, and writing down what you learned is slower in the moment and much faster overall.

The first step in debugging is to reproduce the problem reliably. Find the smallest input and the smallest set of steps that make the bug appear every time. If you cannot reproduce it, you cannot be sure you have fixed it. Once you can reproduce it, you can experiment safely: try a different value, add a printout, or remove a piece of code, and watch whether the bug stays, moves, or disappears. Each experiment tells you something, even when it fails.

Error messages and stack traces are clues, not insults. An error message usually names the kind of problem, such as a missing method or a nil value, and the stack trace lists the lines that led to it, from the most recent call backwards. Read the first few lines carefully, find the first line that belongs to your own code, and start there. The bug is often one step earlier than the line that crashed, in the value that was passed in or the condition that was chosen.
