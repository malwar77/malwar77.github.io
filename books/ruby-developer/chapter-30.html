Section 1: Thinking Like a Defender

This section is about thinking like a defender. At first the name may sound technical, but the idea underneath it is ordinary and practical, and you have probably already met it in everyday life without calling it by this name. In Ruby, thinking like a defender is a tool you reach for when you want your program to behave clearly and predictably, rather than guessing or hoping that the data will always arrive in perfect form. Take a moment to say the idea in your own words before you look at any code, because a clear plain words picture in your head will make every example that follows easier to follow, easier to remember, and easier to adapt when your own program needs something slightly different. As you read, notice how this idea connects to the wider theme of security basics for ruby programs, and ask yourself where it would help in a program you already understand, such as a task list, a note keeper, or a small tool that works with text and files.

Security for a small program is not about being clever or secretive; it is about being careful with trust. Every program receives input, opens files, talks to other systems, or stores data that someone cares about. A defender asks a simple question at each of those points: what could go wrong if this input, file, or request is not what I expected? Answering that question honestly, and handling the bad case gracefully, is most of practical security.

The most important rule is to never trust input just because it came from a user, a file, or another program. Input can be longer than you expected, a different type than you expected, or written in a form designed to confuse your code. Validation is the habit of checking input against what you actually allow, and rejecting or cleaning anything else, before you use it. Allow lists, which say exactly what is permitted, are usually safer than block lists, which try to guess every bad thing.

A second rule is to keep secrets out of code. Passwords, tokens and private keys do not belong in a source file, a screenshot, or a shared chat message, because code is copied, shared and stored in many places. Put secrets in environment variables or a private configuration file that is never shared, and read them at runtime. If a secret does leak, treat it as compromised: replace it, check what it could have accessed, and learn how it leaked so it does not happen again.
