This habit of tracing is one of the most useful skills you will ever build, and it works for every topic in this book, not just this one.

Now look at the code example below and walk through it line by line. Start at the first line and ask yourself what Ruby knows at that point, then move to the next line and update your picture. The example is deliberately short, because a short example lets you see the whole idea at once. Try to predict the output before you imagine running it, and if your prediction is wrong, work out which line changed your mind. That moment, when prediction meets reality, is where real learning happens.

def greet(name)
  puts "Hello, #{name}!"
end
greet("Ada")

There is one common mistake to watch out for here. Beginners often rush past this idea and assume it works the way they hoped, rather than the way Ruby actually does it. The fix is simple: test with a tiny example, print the result, and compare it with what you expected. If the two do not match, do not just change things at random. Instead, read the code again, slowly, and find the first line where your expectation and Ruby's behaviour part company. That line is your lesson. Testing like this will save you hours of confusion later.
