Section 50: What You Can Do Next with Loops

This section is about what you can do next with loops. In plain words, What you can do next with loops is to combine them with methods, files, and user input, so programs can handle real amounts of data. That idea may sound simple, and it is simple on purpose. Ruby was designed to read a little like ordinary language, so when you understand the idea in words first, the code that follows feels familiar instead of strange. For example, a program might read lines from a file, loop over them, and count how many mention a keyword.

Here is how it works in practice. Loops are a foundation skill, and you will use them in almost every program you write from here on. Think of it like a small decision, or a small job, that Ruby carries out for you in a fixed order. Ruby reads your program from the top, follows each instruction in turn, and does exactly what you wrote, nothing more and nothing less. When you are unsure what a program will do, slow down and trace it by hand, pretending that you are Ruby and following each line yourself. This habit of tracing is one of the most useful skills you will ever build, and it works for every topic in this book, not just this one.

Now look at the code example below and walk through it line by line. Start at the first line and ask yourself what Ruby knows at that point, then move to the next line and update your picture. The example is deliberately short, because a short example lets you see the whole idea at once. Try to predict the output before you imagine running it, and if your prediction is wrong, work out which line changed your mind. lines = ["Ruby is fun", "Loops help"]
