The way through that feeling is to connect the idea to a concrete fictional example you can inspect row by row. A beginner often checks data by eye, but eye-checking fails as soon as a table grows past a few dozen rows.

To understand your many-to-many checklist, start by asking what problem it solves and what would go wrong without it. Imagine a small fictional library, shop, or school database, and picture the exact rows that would be harmed if this idea were missing. That picture matters, because databases fail quietly: a bad row, a broken link, or a missing match rarely announces itself with a loud error. Instead, reports go wrong, counts drift, and people stop trusting the numbers they see. If a query returns nothing, check the key values first, then the spelling of column names, then the filter itself.

Here is how to work with your many-to-many checklist in practice. First, write the table or query in its simplest form, using only the columns the idea truly needs. Next, add one fictional row that should succeed, run the statement, and confirm that the row appears exactly as you expected. Then add a second row that should fail or behave differently, and read the result carefully instead of rushing past it. Study the SQL example below line by line, naming the input, the rule or link, and the result for each part. Document the reason for each rule in a comment or a note, because the reason is often forgotten long before the rule is.

As you practise your many-to-many checklist, watch for the mistakes beginners make most often, such as forgetting which side holds the link, writing a rule that is stricter or looser than intended, or testing only the rows that already succeed. A careful learner tests the boundary as well as the middle, checks row counts after every change, and keeps examples small enough to verify by hand. That discipline is what turns a memorised statement into a reliable skill you can use on a real database later. When a concept feels abstract, attach it to a concrete fictional row you can picture, and follow that row through the example. Keep the wording of your rule close to the wording of the business sentence it came from, so a reader can match them at a glance. If two readers disagree about what a rule means, the rule needs clearer words before it needs more SQL. Small tables, clear names, and one idea at a time make even a difficult design easy to review and easy to fix.
