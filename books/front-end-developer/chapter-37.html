Chapter 37 — Authentication in Front-End

Section 1 — What authentication is

Authentication is the process of checking who someone is before giving them access to something that belongs to them, such as an account, a private page or a personal setting. In everyday life, showing an identity card or unlocking a phone with a passcode are forms of authentication: they answer the question, are you really the person you claim to be. On the web, a login form plays a similar role, asking for evidence, such as a password, that only the account holder should know. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs.

The front-end part of authentication is the interface: the form, the buttons, the messages and the screens that guide a person through proving who they are. The actual checking, however, must happen on the server, because anything that runs in a browser can be inspected and changed by the visitor. A front-end developer therefore builds a clear, honest login experience, and leaves the decision about whether the evidence is correct to the server, which is the only part that can be trusted with that decision. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour.

A login screen that asks for an email address and a password, explains what happens next, and shows a clear error if the details do not match, is the familiar front-end shape of authentication.

Section 2 — Authentication and authorisation

Authentication and authorisation sound similar, but they answer different questions. Authentication asks, who are you, while authorisation asks, what are you allowed to do now that we know who you are. A person can be authenticated as a genuine account holder yet still not be authorised to view another person's private page, to change an administrator's settings, or to delete content they do not own. Confusing the two is a common source of both poor design and serious mistakes. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer.

In the front end, this distinction shows up in what you display: you might show a signed-in visitor their own dashboard, while hiding administrator tools they are not allowed to use. Hiding a tool, however, is only a matter of presentation; it does not enforce the rule. Enforcement must happen on the server, which checks, for every request, both that the visitor is authenticated and that they are authorised for that specific action. The front end's job is to present the right options clearly, not to grant permission by itself. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system.

Section 3 — Why the front end never trusts itself

A browser runs on the visitor's own device, which means the visitor can inspect its code, change its values, bypass its checks and send requests that the interface never offered. That is not a flaw in a particular browser; it is the nature of code that runs on someone else's machine. Any rule that exists only in front-end code, such as a check that a user is signed in, a check that a value is within a limit, or a check that a button is hidden, can therefore be bypassed by a determined visitor in minutes. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs. This does not make front-end work pointless; it clarifies its role. Front-end checks improve usability, by catching ordinary mistakes early and giving immediate feedback, but they are never security. Security decisions, such as whether a person may view, change or delete something, must be made again on the server, where the visitor cannot change the rules. Section 4 — The login flow, step by step

A login flow is the sequence of steps a person follows to prove who they are and reach their account. At concept level, it begins with the person opening a login screen, continues with them providing evidence, such as a password, and ends with the server confirming that evidence and establishing a signed-in state for subsequent requests. The front end's role is to guide the person through those steps clearly, to show progress and errors honestly, and to handle the result the server returns, whether success or failure. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer.

In a typical flow, the person enters their details and submits the form, the browser sends those details securely to the server, the server checks them, and if they match, it establishes a session, which is covered in a later section. The front end then shows the signed-in view, such as a dashboard or a welcome message, and offers a way to sign out. If the details do not match, the front end shows a clear, non-specific error, without revealing whether the email or the password was the wrong part, for reasons covered in the section on error messages. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system.

Section 5 — The logout flow

Logout is the process of ending a signed-in state, so that the account is no longer accessible from that browser or device. It is just as important as login, because a session left open on a shared computer, a borrowed phone or a public device can let the next person use the account as if they were its owner. A clear, reliable logout is therefore a basic courtesy and a basic safety feature, not an optional extra that can be added later. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.

At concept level, logout should tell the server to end the session, so that the session can no longer be used, and then return the visitor to a signed-out view, such as the home page or the login screen, with a clear confirmation. The front end should not merely hide signed-in content while leaving the session alive, because that would look signed out while still being usable. It should also clear any signed-in display, such as a name or avatar, so the next person sees a neutral, signed-out page. If you can describe the flow in plain words before you write code, the code itself becomes much easier to write and to debug. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later.

Section 6 — Sessions in plain words

A session is the server's way of remembering that a visitor who signed in earlier is the same visitor making a new request now, without asking for their password on every single page. When a person signs in successfully, the server creates a session, gives the browser a session identifier, and remembers that identifier alongside the account it belongs to. On each later request, the browser presents the identifier, and the server checks it, so the visitor stays signed in as they move around the site. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.

The front end does not need to understand the session's internal details, and it must never try to create, forge or edit a session identifier itself. Its job is to present the signed-in or signed-out state that the server reports, to handle the case where a session has expired, and to offer sign-in and sign-out at the right moments. Sessions have a lifetime, after which they expire, and they can be ended early by logout, both of which are server decisions that the front end must respect and reflect honestly. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later.

Section 7 — Cookies in plain words

A cookie is a small piece of data that a server asks a browser to store and to send back with later requests to the same site. Cookies are commonly used to carry a session identifier, so the server can recognise a returning visitor, and they can also carry preferences, such as a language choice. The important point for front-end developers is that the safest cookies are set by the server with protections that keep them out of reach of page scripts, which limits what a malicious script could steal if it ever ran on the page. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.

Two protections matter most at concept level. A cookie marked HttpOnly cannot be read by JavaScript on the page, which means page scripts cannot copy it. A cookie marked Secure is only sent over an encrypted connection, and a SameSite setting controls whether the cookie is sent with requests that come from other sites, which helps against certain cross-site tricks. Front-end code should not try to set, read or manage session cookies itself; it should let the server set them, and it should respect the signed-in state the server reports. If you can describe the flow in plain words before you write code, the code itself becomes much easier to write and to debug. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later.

Section 8 — Tokens in plain words

A token is a credential, a kind of digital pass, that proves a visitor has been authenticated, and that a server can check without looking up a password on every request. Tokens are often used by applications that talk to an application programming interface, where the front end sends the token with a request and the server verifies it before answering. At concept level, a token is like a ticket: it proves you were admitted, it has a limited lifetime, and it can be revoked, but anyone who copies it can use it until it expires or is revoked. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs.

That last point is why token handling is so sensitive. A token is a secret, and a secret that leaks can be misused by whoever finds it, even if they never knew the password. Front-end code must therefore never contain tokens, never embed them in a page, and never treat a token as ordinary display data. Where an application uses tokens, the safer designs keep them managed by the server, or in a form the page's scripts cannot read, rather than handing a long-lived token to front-end code to look after. The details belong to the server design; the front-end lesson is that a token is a credential, not a piece of content. Section 9 — Access and refresh tokens

Some systems use two kinds of token together: a short-lived access token, which is presented with each request to prove the visitor is signed in, and a longer-lived refresh token, which is used only to obtain a new access token when the old one expires. The idea is to limit damage: because the access token lives only briefly, a copied access token is useful only for a short time, while the refresh token, which is more powerful, is used rarely and is kept under stricter protection. Together, they balance convenience, because visitors are not asked to sign in constantly, with safety, because no single token lasts forever. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs.

For front-end developers, the concept matters more than the mechanics. The front end should not be responsible for storing, rotating or protecting these tokens as if they were ordinary data, and it should handle expiry by asking the server for a fresh session through the proper flow, rather than by inventing its own token logic. When an access token expires, the honest front-end response is to show a loading or re-authentication state, and to let the server decide whether the session can be renewed or whether the visitor must sign in again. Section 10 — Secrets must never live in front-end code

This is the most important rule in this chapter, stated without qualification: secrets must never live in front-end code. Passwords, tokens, application programming interface keys, private keys and any other credential must not be written into HTML, CSS, JavaScript, or any file that is sent to a browser, because every file sent to a browser can be read by anyone who opens the page and inspects its source. A secret in front-end code is not hidden; it is published, whether you intended to publish it or not. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.

The reason is simple. Front-end code is delivered to the visitor's device, and the visitor can view it, copy it and search it. There is no front-end hiding technique, no obfuscation and no minification that turns a published secret into a safe secret; those techniques only make the secret slightly harder to spot at a glance. If a front-end feature needs a secret, the correct design is to keep the secret on the server, let the server perform the action that needs the secret, and let the front end talk only to the server, never directly with the secret itself. Section 11 — Why browser storage is not safe for tokens

Browser storage, such as localStorage and sessionStorage, is a convenient place for a page to keep ordinary, non-sensitive data, such as a display preference or a draft that is not private. It is not a safe place for tokens, passwords or other credentials, and this chapter states that plainly, because the opposite advice is common and dangerous. Any script running on the page can read browser storage, which means that if a malicious script ever runs on the page, for example through a cross-site scripting flaw covered later, it can copy every token stored there. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent.

The distinction is between data and credentials. A theme preference leaking is an inconvenience; a token leaking is an account takeover, because the token can be used to act as the account holder until it expires or is revoked. For that reason, authentication credentials should be kept in server-managed, script-inaccessible forms, such as HttpOnly cookies set by the server, rather than in storage that page scripts can read. If you see a tutorial that stores a token in browser storage and calls it safe, treat that tutorial as wrong on this point, however helpful it is otherwise. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened.

Section 12 — Password handling in the front end

Passwords are among the most sensitive data a front end ever handles, and the front-end rules for them are strict and simple. A password should be entered in a password field, sent securely to the server over an encrypted connection, and never stored, logged, displayed or embedded in front-end code. The front end should not keep a copy of the password after submission, should not show it in plain text except when the visitor explicitly asks to reveal it, and should not send it to any destination other than the server that will check it. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs.

Good password interfaces help visitors without weakening safety. They use a password field that masks input by default, offer a clearly labelled show-password option for visitors who want to check their typing, support password managers and autofill, and give clear feedback about requirements without revealing anything about other accounts. They never pre-fill a password from front-end code, never store a password in browser storage, and never include a password in a page address, because addresses are logged, shared and bookmarked. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour.

Section 25 — Cross-site request forgery in plain words

Cross-site request forgery, often shortened to CSRF, is an attack in which a malicious site tricks a visitor's browser into sending a request to a site where the visitor is already signed in, as if the visitor had intended it. Because the browser automatically sends the site's cookies with the request, the target site may treat the forged request as genuine, for example changing a setting, posting a message or transferring something, even though the visitor never meant to do it. The attack works not by stealing a password, but by abusing the trust the site places in the visitor's signed-in browser. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks.

Front-end developers need to understand CSRF at concept level, because the protections are a partnership between front end and server. The server issues a unique, unpredictable token for each form or session, the front end includes that token with requests that change something, and the server checks it before acting. The SameSite cookie setting, covered earlier, also helps, by limiting when cookies are sent with cross-site requests. The front-end lesson is to send state-changing requests through proper forms or requests that include the server's protection, and never to perform a sensitive change through a simple link that a malicious page could trigger by loading it as an image or a link. Section 26 — Cross-site scripting and authentication

Cross-site scripting, often shortened to XSS, is an attack in which a malicious script is made to run on a page where it does not belong, for example through a comment, a profile field or a search result that is displayed without proper escaping. That is exactly why tokens and credentials must never be stored where page scripts can read them, as covered earlier: an XSS flaw turns readable storage into stolen credentials. Section 27 — HTTPS and authentication

Authentication over an unencrypted connection is unsafe, because passwords, session identifiers and other credentials travel in a form that anyone positioned between the visitor and the server could read or alter, for example on a public Wi-Fi network. Section 49 — Auth and lockout recovery

Lockout recovery is the process of regaining access when a visitor cannot sign in, whether because they forgot a password, lost a second factor, or were locked out after too many attempts. It is a moment of stress, because the visitor is locked out of something they own, and a poor recovery experience can push them towards unsafe shortcuts, such as sharing credentials or creating a duplicate account with a weak password. A good recovery experience therefore offers clear, legitimate paths, explains each step, and sets honest expectations about time and verification. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks.

The front-end recovery options usually include password reset through a verified email address, recovery codes for two-factor loss, and, where the site provides it, a support or identity-verification process for cases where neither works. The interface should present those options in order, from the simplest to the most involved, explain what each requires, and avoid promising immediate recovery where verification will take time. It must not offer a shortcut that bypasses authentication, such as revealing a password or skipping a second factor on request, because that shortcut would be available to an attacker as well as to the genuine owner. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once.

Section 50 — Putting it together: an authentication checklist

An authentication checklist turns this chapter into a habit, so no login, logout or account feature goes live with a missing protection or a misleading interface. The checklist is not a guarantee of security; security is a property of the whole system, including the server, and no front-end checklist alone can provide it. What the checklist can do is catch the front-end mistakes that are most common and most costly, such as a secret in code, a token in browser storage, a missing logout, or an error message that leaks information, before visitors meet them. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.
