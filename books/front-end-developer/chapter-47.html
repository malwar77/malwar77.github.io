Chapter 47 — Capstone Planning: MyDay Dashboard

Section 1

MyDay Dashboard is a small personal dashboard for tasks and notes, built with only HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no framework and no server. It brings together everything you have learned so far into one useful page that a person can open every day, add a task, write a note, and see at a glance how the day is going. The dashboard has three panels: a tasks panel, a notes panel, and an overview panel that counts tasks and shows progress. Those three panels are the whole project, and keeping them in view at the same time is what makes the dashboard feel calm and practical rather than scattered across many screens. A good capstone plan starts by naming the job in one sentence, because a project without a clear job tends to grow until it cannot be finished. MyDay Dashboard helps one person keep tasks and notes in one place, find them again quickly, and see progress without any extra effort. Every feature in this chapter must serve that job, and every feature that does not serve it should be left out, however interesting it might seem on its own. That discipline is not about limiting ambition; it is about finishing something real, testing it properly, and being able to explain it clearly to someone else. Planning also means deciding what the project will not do, and writing those limits down before any code is written. MyDay Dashboard will not use a framework, will not need a server, will not ask anyone to create an account, and will not share data between devices or browsers. Its data lives only in the browser where it was entered, which is honest, simple, and easy to test, and it is the reason the footer in the finished project says so plainly. When a limit is stated early, later decisions become easier, because any suggestion can be checked against the limit in seconds. This chapter is the plan for the next three chapters, and it previews them in the order you will build them. Chapter 48 covers structure and style: the HTML for the three panels and the CSS that makes them readable, calm, and responsive. Chapter 49 covers behaviour and data: the JavaScript that adds, changes, filters, searches, saves, and counts tasks and notes. Chapter 50 covers finishing, portfolio presentation, and next steps: testing the whole flow, writing a short readme and case study, and choosing what to learn after this project. Nothing in this plan goes beyond what those chapters deliver, so you can trust it as a map. Before you build, read this chapter with a pen in hand and mark every decision you agree with and every decision you would question. A plan you have merely skimmed will not guide you when building gets difficult, but a plan you have argued with, even briefly, becomes a tool you can return to. Keep your written plan beside you through chapters 48, 49 and 50, and check the finished dashboard against it at the end, panel by panel and feature by feature.

Section 2

MyDay Dashboard is for one person at a time, and that person is its only user, its only editor, and its only audience for the data it holds. Picture someone starting the day with a short list of things to do, a few reminders they do not want to lose, and a wish to see progress by evening. That person does not need a team tool, a shared workspace, or a complicated setup process; they need a page that opens quickly, accepts a task in seconds, and remembers what was entered after the page is closed and reopened. Designing for that single person keeps every decision simple, because there is no need to handle permissions, sharing, or conflicting edits from other people. The same person may use the dashboard in very different moments, and the plan must serve all of them. In the morning, they may add several tasks quickly and want the form to stay out of the way, with focus returning so the next task can be typed at once. During the day, they may glance at the overview to see how many tasks are done and how many remain, without reading every item again. In the evening, they may search for a note they wrote earlier, pin the note they need most, and clear tasks that are already finished. A dashboard that only works well for one of those moments, and frustrates the others, has not understood its user. A second audience matters too, even though they will never type a task into the dashboard: the person who reviews the finished project. That reviewer might be a teacher, a future employer, or a fellow learner, and they will judge the project in a few minutes by opening it, trying a task, and reading a short description. For that reviewer, the dashboard must explain itself at once: a clear title, a short purpose line, panels with plain headings, and generic demo data that shows the features without exposing anyone's private thoughts. Private tasks and notes must never appear in screenshots or in a shared demo, because a portfolio piece should show skill, not personal information. A third audience is you, later in the build, when you return to code you wrote days earlier and need to understand it quickly. Clear file names, honest class names, small functions with plain names, and short comments that explain why a decision was made all serve that future version of you. Planning for that reader now, by keeping the structure simple and the naming consistent, saves far more time than it costs, and it is a habit that carries into every project after this one. Finally, the dashboard should work for someone using a small phone screen, a keyboard without a mouse, or a screen reader, because accessibility is not a separate audience but a quality of the whole design. Large enough tap targets, visible labels, real buttons, and a clear heading order help everyone, not only people who rely on assistive technology. When you plan each panel in the sections that follow, keep asking whether a hurried person on a phone, a keyboard-only user, and a first-time visitor could all use it without help.

Section 3

The problem MyDay Dashboard solves is ordinary, which is exactly why it is worth solving well: tasks and notes tend to scatter across many places, and scattered information is easy to lose track of. A task written on paper may be left at home, a note typed into one app may be forgotten in another, and a mental list may simply fade by afternoon. When tasks live in several places, a person cannot see at a glance what remains, what is already done, or which note holds the detail they need. The result is not laziness or poor organisation; it is the predictable cost of having no single, trusted place to look. MyDay Dashboard answers that problem by putting tasks and notes side by side on one page, with a small overview that summarises the whole picture. A task can be added with text and a priority, marked done when it is finished, and removed when it is no longer needed. A note can be added with a title and a body, pinned to the top when it matters most, edited when it changes, and deleted when it is spent. A search box can find a task or a note by its words, and filters can show all tasks, only active tasks, or only done tasks, so the list shown always matches the question the user is asking at that moment. The overview panel is the part that turns a list into a dashboard, because it answers questions the lists alone cannot answer quickly. How many tasks are there in total, how many are done, and how many are still active? What share of the work is complete, shown as a simple progress bar and a percentage? How many tasks sit at each priority level, shown as a small bar display? Those counts are calculated from the same data as the lists, never typed by hand, so they cannot drift out of date when a task is added, completed, or deleted. A user who trusts the counts can trust the dashboard. It is equally important to be honest about what the dashboard does not solve, because that honesty shapes every design choice. It does not sync data between devices, it does not send reminders, and it does not store data anywhere except the browser on the device where it was entered. If a person opens the dashboard in a different browser, their tasks and notes will not be there, and the project must say so plainly rather than letting them discover it with surprise. That limit is acceptable for a personal capstone, and naming it early prevents the project from promising what a front-end-only page cannot deliver. Framing the problem this way gives you a test for every later feature: does this feature make it easier to capture, find, complete, or review a task or a note? A feature that passes that test belongs in the plan, and a feature that fails it, however clever, should be left out or saved for a later project. Use that test whenever a tempting addition appears during chapters 48, 49 and 50, and the dashboard will stay focused, finishable, and genuinely useful.

Section 4

Success for MyDay Dashboard is not a vague feeling that the page looks pleasant; it is a set of behaviours a reviewer can test in a few minutes, without being told how the code works inside. A successful dashboard loads as a single page, shows its title and purpose at once, and presents the tasks panel, the notes panel, and the overview panel in a layout that reads clearly on a phone and on a wider screen. A visitor can add a task, see it appear in the list with its priority shown, add a note, see it appear with its title and body, and reload the page to find both items still there. Those basic behaviours, working reliably, are the core of success. A second measure of success is that changing data updates every panel that depends on it, without any manual refresh or second step. When a task is marked done, it should move between filtered views as expected, the overview counts should change, the progress bar should move, and the bar display by priority should still reflect the full list correctly. When a note is pinned, it should rise to the top of the notes list, and when a note is edited, the updated title and body should be the ones shown and saved. When a task or note is deleted, the list, the counts, and the empty state, if the list is now empty, should all agree at once. A dashboard whose panels disagree with each other cannot be trusted, even if each panel looks fine on its own. A fourth measure is quality across devices and input methods, because a dashboard is used repeatedly and often in a hurry. Panels should stack on a narrow screen, buttons should be large enough to tap comfortably, text should remain readable without zooming, and every action should be reachable with a keyboard alone, using Tab, Enter, and Space. Headings should be in order, labels should be visible and connected to their inputs, and counts that change should be announced politely for screen reader users. An empty list should show a helpful message, such as No tasks yet, with a hint about what to do next, rather than a blank space that looks broken. Finally, success includes finishing and presenting the work, which chapter 50 covers in detail. The three files should be clean, with no leftover debug output and no dead code, and a short readme should explain what the project is and how to run it by opening the main file in a browser. A portfolio entry and a short case study should describe the problem, the approach, the result, and what you would improve next, honestly and without invented figures. A project that works locally but is never tested end to end, cleaned up, or explained to a reviewer has not fully succeeded, and this plan states those measures now so they are not forgotten at the end.

Section 5

MyDay Dashboard has three panels, and understanding each panel's job before you build any of them prevents the common mistake of letting one panel try to do everything. The tasks panel is where work is captured and tracked: it holds the add-task form, the filter bar, the search box, and the list of tasks themselves. Its job is to make adding a task quick, marking it done satisfying, and finding a particular task easy, even when the list grows to a dozen or more items. Everything in the tasks panel serves that job, and nothing in it should distract from it, such as a long explanation or an extra control that is rarely used. The notes panel is where short thoughts, reminders, and reference details are kept, and its job is different from the tasks panel in an important way. Notes are not marked done or filtered by completion; they are kept, pinned, edited, and deleted, and they are shown with pinned notes first so the most important note is always easy to find. A note has a title and a body, which is enough structure to make it searchable and scannable, but not so much structure that writing a note feels like filling in a form. Keeping notes simple is a deliberate choice, because a note that takes too long to write will not be written at all, and the dashboard will lose one of its main uses. Together, the three panels form a complete working loop that a user can repeat every day. Add a task in the tasks panel, write a supporting note in the notes panel, check the overview to see progress, filter or search to focus on what remains, complete tasks one by one, and watch the overview move toward full completion. That loop is the product, and it is small enough to build well in three chapters, yet rich enough to practise structure, style, behaviour, data, testing, and presentation. When you are tempted to add a fourth panel, ask whether it strengthens that loop or merely adds surface area that must be built, styled, tested, and maintained. The order of the panels on the page matters too, and chapter 48 settles it as tasks, notes, and overview, with headings in that order, so a visitor can understand the whole page from its headings alone. On a wide screen, tasks and notes can sit side by side with the overview spanning the full width, while on a narrow screen the panels stack in the same order, so the reading order never changes between devices. Planning that order now means the HTML, the CSS layout, and the screen reader experience all tell the same story, which is what makes a simple page feel carefully designed rather than merely assembled.

Section 6

MyDay Dashboard is a single page, and that choice shapes almost every other decision in this plan, so it deserves a clear explanation before any code is written. A single page means all of the work happens in one view: tasks, notes, and the overview are visible together, without navigation, routing, or duplicated headers to maintain. A user never has to remember which screen holds a note, because the note is on the same page as the task it supports, and a reviewer never has to click through several screens to understand what the project does. For a first capstone, that simplicity is a strength, not a limitation, because it lets you spend your effort on making one page excellent instead of spreading it thinly across many. A single page also keeps the technical scope honest and achievable with only HTML, CSS and JavaScript. There is no framework to learn, no build step to configure, and no server to run, which means the project can be opened by double-clicking its main file in a browser, or by copying three files to a static host for a public link. That low barrier matters for testing too: every change can be checked by reloading one page, and every bug can be traced to one of three files without wondering whether a routing layer or a build tool introduced it. When something breaks, the search area is small, and the fix is usually close to the symptom. A single page does not mean a long, shapeless page, and the plan guards against that risk with labelled sections, a clear heading order, and a layout that groups related controls together. The header holds the title, a short purpose line, and the current date as text, the main area holds the three panels, and the footer holds a short status line and a note about where data is stored. Each panel has its own container, so styling and behaviour can target one panel without disturbing the others, and each list has a planned empty message for when it holds no items. Structure first, style second, behaviour third is the build order, and this chapter keeps that order visible throughout. Finally, a single page makes the finish line clear, which is important for motivation as well as for quality. The project is done when one page lets a user add, complete, delete, filter, search, pin, edit, and reload tasks and notes, with counts and progress that always match the lists. There is no second page waiting to be built, no hidden screen that could quietly remain unfinished, and no navigation state to test separately. That clarity is why this plan insists on one page, and why chapters 48, 49 and 50 can each be checked against a single, visible result.

Section 7

The project folder holds exactly three files, and planning that folder before you write any code prevents a surprising amount of confusion later. The folder can have any sensible name that describes the project, and inside it sit index.html, styles.css, and app.js, side by side, with no subfolders and no extra files to track. That flat layout means every link between files is a short relative path in the same folder, so opening the page locally and copying the folder to a static host both work without adjusting paths. A beginner who can find every file in one glance can also reason about the whole project in one glance, which is a real advantage when debugging. Each file's job should be stated in one sentence and then respected throughout the build. Index.html holds the structure: the header, the three panels, the forms, the filter bar, the search box, the lists, the overview counts, the progress bar, and the footer. Styles.css holds the presentation: colours, type, spacing, layout, cards, buttons, badges, bars, focus styles, and responsive rules, organised in a clear order from general to specific. App.js holds the behaviour and the data: the state, the storage, the rendering, the event handlers, and the startup sequence that loads saved data and draws the page for the first time. If a change belongs in more than one file, pause and ask which file owns the decision, because a change smeared across files is harder to test and harder to explain. Naming inside the files matters as much as naming the files themselves, and chapter 48 plans a simple rule: identifiers mark single elements that JavaScript must find, such as the task list and the task form, while class names describe reusable patterns, such as a card, a button, or a muted text style. A class should describe the job of the element, not only how it happens to look today, so a class that means a task item remains correct even if its colour changes later. Honest names reduce the need for comments, because the code already says what it means, and they make the stylesheet easier to audit, since a reviewer can see which patterns are reused and which are used only once. Finally, keep a working version saved after each step, and test the page after each change, including reloading to confirm that saved tasks and notes are still there. A three-file project invites the habit of changing one file, reloading, and checking the result before moving on, and that habit is worth more than any clever technique in this book. When a change breaks the page, undo the last change, re-read the code around it, and try a narrower fix, because a calm, step-by-step check usually finds the problem faster than rewriting a whole file. The folder plan is small, but the working habits it supports are the real capstone skill.

The title is MyDay Dashboard, which names the project in the same words the user will use to describe it, and the purpose line is a single calm sentence, such as tasks and notes in one calm place, which tells a first-time visitor what the page is for without requiring them to read further. The current date, shown as text, gives the page a sense of today, which suits a dashboard meant to be used daily, and it also helps a reviewer understand at a glance that the page is meant for the present day rather than for an archive. The header uses a real header element, so the top of the page is a landmark that assistive technology can recognise, not merely a styled division that happens to sit at the top. Inside the header, the title is the page's single level-one heading, which means the heading outline of the whole page starts here and then moves to level-two headings for the panels. Plan the header first, keep it short, and let it do the quiet work of orientation well.

Section 9

The main element is a landmark, just as the header is, so assistive technology can jump straight to the main content without passing through the header again on every visit. Inside it, each panel is a section with its own level-two heading and a label that names it for everyone, including screen reader users, so the page can be understood by region as well as by visual layout. A visitor who cannot see the layout can still navigate by headings and landmarks, and that equivalence between visual and structural order is a mark of good front-end work. Each section needs its own container for a practical reason as well as an accessibility reason: styling and behaviour can target one panel without disturbing the others. The tasks section can hold its form, filter bar, search box, and list as separate parts inside it, the notes section can hold its form and list, and the overview section can hold its counts, progress bar, and bar display, each with a clear container. When a later change affects only one panel, such as restyling the filter bar, the developer can work inside that section with confidence, and when a bug appears in one panel, the search for its cause starts in a small, well-defined area. Finally, the main area is where the build order becomes visible, because chapter 48 starts with the header and the main sections as empty containers, then fills each panel in turn, checking the page after each step. That order lets you verify the structure before any style or behaviour is added, by viewing the page with styles switched off and confirming that the outline still makes sense. A page whose outline makes sense without styles is a page whose structure is doing its job, and every later layer, from CSS to JavaScript, builds on that solid foundation rather than compensating for a weak one.

Section 10

The tasks panel is the busiest panel in the dashboard, and its plan groups its parts so each part can be built, styled, and tested on its own. At the top sits a level-two heading, Tasks, which names the panel for sighted users and for assistive technology alike, and which anchors the section in the page outline. Below the heading sits the add-task form, then the filter bar, then the search box, then the list itself, each in its own container, so a change to one part, such as restyling the filters, does not ripple into the form or the list by accident. That grouping is the tasks panel's structure in miniature, and chapter 48 builds it in exactly that order. The filter bar sits below the form, with three buttons, All, Active, and Done, and the active filter is marked so the user can see the current choice at a glance. Filters are buttons, not links, because they change the view without leaving the page, and each button carries its filter value in a way JavaScript can read simply, such as a data attribute in the markup. That small markup decision, planned now, keeps the later JavaScript simple and the intent visible in the HTML, which is a good example of how structure choices made in chapter 48 make behaviour choices in chapter 49 easier. A filter bar that is clear in markup is also clear to a reviewer reading the HTML alone. The search box is a single text input with a visible label, placed above the lists it affects, and one search box serves both tasks and notes in this project, which keeps the interface simple and the behaviour easy to explain. A user who types a word sees matching tasks and matching notes, without having to remember which box belongs to which panel, and the overview counts continue to count all tasks, not only the visible ones, so searching never makes the summary misleading. That rule, that search filters what is shown but not what is counted, is worth stating now, because it is easy to get wrong later, and a dashboard whose counts change with every keystroke would confuse rather than inform. Finally, the task list itself is an unordered list, because it shows a collection of similar items, and each item, when rendered, will hold a checkbox-style button, the task text, a priority badge, and a delete button. The list starts empty in the markup, ready for JavaScript to fill it, and it has a planned empty message, such as No tasks yet, for when it holds no visible items. Planning the empty message as part of the structure, rather than discovering it when the first user sees a blank panel, is a small habit that makes the finished dashboard feel intentional from the very first run, even before any task has been added.

Section 11

The notes panel is quieter than the tasks panel, and that quietness is intentional, because notes are meant to be captured quickly and found again without fuss. Its structure, planned in chapter 48, is a level-two heading, Notes, followed by an add-note form and a list for notes, with a pin control on each note once it is rendered. Notes are shorter than tasks in this project in the sense that they carry less behaviour: a title and a body, plus a pinned flag that controls their order, and no completion state, no priority, and no filter of their own. That restraint keeps the notes panel easy to explain, easy to style, and easy to use, and it prevents the dashboard from becoming two task managers wearing different labels. Each note in the list, when rendered, shows its title, its body, a pin button, an edit button, and a delete button, and the list is an unordered list, just as the task list is, because it too shows a collection of similar items. Sorting happens at render time, with pinned notes first, which keeps the stored order simple and puts the display rule in one place where it can be changed without rewriting the stored data. Pinning is therefore a display preference stored with the note, not a separate list, and unpinning a note simply returns it to its normal position among the unpinned notes. That design is worth naming now, because it explains why pinning feels instant and why it survives a reload: the flag is saved with the note, and the render rule does the rest. Editing a note reuses the same form that adds a note, which is another deliberate simplification that chapter 49 implements carefully. When the edit button is pressed, the form is filled with that note's title and body, the project remembers which note is being edited, and submitting the form updates that note instead of creating a new one. Reusing one form for add and edit keeps the interface small and the code focused, but it also creates a responsibility: the form must make clear, by its button label or a short heading change, whether it is adding or editing, so the user is never surprised by what a submit will do. Planning that clarity now prevents a confusing moment later, when a user edits a note and wonders whether they have just duplicated it. Finally, the notes panel has its own planned empty message, shown when no notes are visible, just as the tasks panel does, and its own behaviour for the last-note case: when the final note is deleted, the empty state appears as a normal render result, not as a special case handled by hand. That consistency, where empty states are simply what rendering produces when a list is empty, is a principle that runs through the whole project, and it is why chapter 49 centralises rendering instead of scattering display logic across many handlers. A notes panel that is simple, honest, and predictable will be used, and a notes panel that is clever but confusing will be ignored, which is the real test of this panel's plan.

Section 12

The overview panel summarises the dashboard, and its plan is to show information, not to collect it, which is why it contains no inputs of its own. At the top sits a level-two heading, Overview, followed by three counts: total tasks, completed tasks, and active tasks, each clearly labelled so a visitor can read them without guessing what a number means. Below the counts sits a progress bar, with a text label giving the percentage, and below that a simple bar display of tasks by priority, with one row per priority level, each row showing a label, a bar, and a count. Those elements together answer the three questions a user asks most often: how much is there, how much is done, and where is the remaining work concentrated? Progress is a simple calculation from the same state, and planning its edge case now prevents a common bug later. Progress means completed tasks divided by total tasks, multiplied by one hundred and rounded, with zero tasks giving zero percent rather than a division error or a misleading full bar. A dashboard with no tasks yet has made no progress, but it has also not failed, and showing zero percent with an empty bar says that plainly, while showing one hundred percent would celebrate work that does not exist. Chapter 49 guards that empty case explicitly, and chapter 48 styles the bar as a track with a filled portion whose width represents the percentage, plus a text label, so the progress is readable even if colour alone is not perceived. The bar display by priority extends the same idea to a different question, and its plan is equally simple. The code counts tasks per priority, finds the largest count, and sizes each bar relative to that largest count, with the count itself shown as text beside the bar, so the display is never colour-only or width-only. The bars are built from plain divisions styled with widths set by JavaScript, which is data rather than design, and is therefore the one place where an inline width is acceptable, unlike general styling, which belongs in the stylesheet. That distinction, between a width that represents data and a style that represents design, is worth stating now, because it explains an exception that might otherwise look like an inconsistency. Finally, the overview panel's position and presentation matter for trust as well as for appearance. On a wide screen it spans the full width, so its counts and bars can be read at a glance without competing with the lists, and on a narrow screen it stacks with the other panels in the same source order, so its information remains easy to find. Its counts sit in a polite live region, planned in chapter 49, so screen reader users hear updated counts without the page interrupting them, and its text labels ensure that no information is conveyed by colour or bar length alone. An overview that is calculated, labelled, and reachable is not decoration; it is the part of the dashboard that lets a user trust the whole page.

Section 13

The footer is a small area with a large job, because it tells the user, honestly and briefly, where their data lives and what the page is currently showing. Chapter 48 plans it as a short status line, such as how many tasks are shown, plus a note that data is stored only in this browser, placed after the main element so it is supporting information rather than main content. That note is not legal fine print to be hidden at the bottom; it is a promise about the project, and placing it in the footer, where it is always present but never in the way, is the right balance between visibility and restraint. A user who understands where their data lives can make sensible decisions about which browser to use and what to expect after switching devices. The footer also closes the page visually and structurally, which matters more than it might seem on a single-page dashboard. Without a footer, a page that ends abruptly after the last panel can feel unfinished, especially on a wide screen where the panels may not fill the height of the window. With a short footer, the page has a clear ending, the eye knows where the content stops, and the status note has a natural home that does not compete with the panels above it. That sense of completeness is part of why chapter 48 treats the footer as a planned region from the start, rather than as an afterthought added when the rest of the page is done. Accessibility and responsiveness apply to the footer just as they apply to the panels, even though the footer is small. Its text must remain readable at the same sizes and contrasts as the rest of the page, it must not require horizontal scrolling on a narrow screen, and it must not contain links or controls that are unreachable by keyboard. If the footer later gains a link, such as a link to a portfolio page in a deployed version, that link must be checked in the same way as every other link in chapter 50, because one broken or unreachable element in a quiet area still counts as a broken page.
