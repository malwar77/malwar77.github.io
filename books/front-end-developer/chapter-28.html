Chapter 28 — Component Thinking

Section 1 — What a component is

A component is a self-contained piece of an interface, such as a button, a card, a navigation bar, or a comment, that can be described, built, and reused as a single unit. It bundles its markup, its styling, and often its behaviour together, so that wherever the component appears, it looks and acts the same way. Thinking in components means seeing a page not as one long document, but as a collection of such units fitted together. In plain terms, this idea exists because real projects grow. A page that starts with a handful of styles can end up with hundreds, and without a clear approach the stylesheet becomes hard to read, hard to change, and easy to break by accident. The idea in this section gives you a rule you can apply consistently, so that a change in one place does not produce a surprise somewhere else on the site.

Components help because interfaces repeat themselves. A site with twenty article cards does not need twenty separately invented designs; it needs one card design used twenty times. That reuse keeps the site consistent, makes changes cheap, and lets a team divide work by component rather than by page. In practice, start small and observe the result before adding more. Build one component or one page section, apply the idea there, open the page in a browser, and resize the window to see how it behaves. Small, observable steps make it obvious which change caused which effect, and that feedback loop is how good front-end judgement is formed.

For example, an article card component might always contain an image, a heading, a short summary, and a link, arranged the same way each time. Change the card once, and every article listing on the site improves together. A common mistake is to copy a solution without understanding what each part does, then to be stuck when it needs adjusting. Avoid that by reading the example line by line and asking what would happen if each part were removed. The lines you cannot explain are the lines most likely to cause trouble later.

A common mistake is calling every element a component. A single heading is not a component; a card, a form field group, or a navigation bar is, because it combines several parts into a unit with a clear purpose. To check your own work, open the page, inspect the relevant element in developer tools, and confirm that the styles you expect are actually applied rather than overridden or ignored. Then change the browser width, reload, and confirm nothing has broken. Write down anything surprising; those notes become your personal checklist for the next project.

Try it now: make a tiny page with two or three elements, apply this idea to them, and compare the result with a version that does not use it. Notice what got easier, what got harder, and what you would want to change next time. That comparison is the real lesson, and it will guide your choices on larger pages.

Section 2 — Seeing a page as components

Seeing a page as components means drawing mental boxes around its parts before writing code: the header is one box, the hero is another, the list of cards is a repeated box, and the footer is another. Each box should have a name, a purpose, and a clear boundary: what belongs inside it, and what belongs outside it. This sketching step, often done on paper or in a design tool, prevents the common beginner pattern of writing markup top to bottom with no plan for reuse. Think of this as a working habit rather than a piece of trivia. You will use it on almost every page you build, and the benefit appears gradually: files stay shorter, decisions become easier to explain to a teammate, and fixing a visual bug takes minutes instead of an afternoon of hunting through unrelated rules.

The benefit appears when a design changes. If the header is a component, changing it once changes it everywhere, and a new page can be assembled from existing components instead of being built from scratch. Pages become compositions, not inventions, and that composition is faster and more reliable. When you apply this idea, keep the surrounding code in mind. A front-end page is a system: markup, styles, and behaviour interact, and a choice that looks good in isolation can create a problem elsewhere, such as overflowing content on a narrow screen or a colour that fails a contrast check. Test the whole page, not just the fragment you edited.

For example, sketch a blog homepage and box the header, the featured article, each article card, the sidebar, and the footer. The article cards are the same component repeated, while the featured article may be a larger variant of the same idea. Another frequent mistake is to mix approaches in the same file without a reason: part of the page follows one convention and part follows another. That inconsistency makes the code harder to scan and harder to maintain, because a reader cannot predict which convention applies where. Pick one approach for a given job and use it consistently.

A common mistake is drawing boxes that are too large, such as one box for a whole page, or too small, such as a box for every word. Aim for boxes that match a recognisable piece of the interface. A good self-check is to explain the idea aloud in one or two sentences without looking at this section. If you can do that and give a correct example, you understand it well enough to use it. If you cannot, re-read the example and try building a tiny version from scratch before moving on.

For practice, take an existing practice page and rework one section using this idea, keeping the visible result the same. If the page looks identical but the code is clearer or shorter, the rework was worthwhile. If the result changed unexpectedly, you have found a gap in your understanding worth closing before you rely on the idea in a bigger project.

Section 3 — One job per component

A component should do one job well: a button triggers an action, a card presents an item, a search box collects a query. When a component tries to do several unrelated jobs, it becomes hard to understand, hard to test, and hard to reuse, because every use carries the baggage of jobs it does not need. This is the single-responsibility idea applied to interfaces, and it is one of the most useful rules in front-end work. The reason this matters for a beginner is that early choices compound. If you adopt a messy approach on a small practice page, the same approach will feel natural on a larger project, where its cost is much higher. Learning the disciplined version now saves you from having to unlearn a habit later, when a real deadline is involved.

One job does not mean one element. A card does several small things, such as showing an image and a title, but they all serve one job: presenting an item. The test is whether you can describe the component's job in one sentence without using the word and to join unrelated purposes. If the sentence needs several unrelated clauses, the component is probably doing too much. A practical test is to change one thing deliberately. Alter a value, remove a class, or rename something, then refresh and watch what breaks. That experiment teaches you what actually depends on the idea, which is far more useful than memorising a definition. Undo the change afterwards and confirm the page returns to normal.

For example, a comment component's job is to present one comment: the author's name, the text, and the date. If it also handles moderation, reporting, and editing, those are separate jobs that deserve separate components or controls. Watch out for overuse. A technique that helps in moderation can hurt when applied everywhere: too many classes, too many wrappers, or too many overrides all add weight and confusion. If a page feels complicated, look for the simplest version that still meets the design, and prefer that version.

A common mistake is letting a component grow by accretion, adding one more feature each time a new requirement appears, until the original job is buried. Split it before it becomes unmanageable. Keep a small practice file where you try each idea in isolation. A tiny page with a single component is a better laboratory than a large project, because there is nothing else to confuse the result. Once the idea works there, carry it into a real page with confidence.

A helpful exercise is to build the same small component twice: once with this approach and once without it. Put the two versions side by side and judge them on readability, ease of change, and behaviour on a narrow screen. There is rarely one winner for every situation, and learning when each approach wins is the skill that matters.

Section 4 — Props: inputs to a component

Props, short for properties, are the inputs a component accepts so that one component definition can produce many different outputs. A card component might accept a title, an image, and a summary as props, and each use supplies different values. The component's structure stays the same, while its content changes, which is exactly how one card design serves twenty different articles. A useful way to judge this idea is to ask what happens when the design changes. Clients and designers change colours, spacing, and layouts often. An approach that survives those changes without a rewrite is a good one; an approach that forces you to edit dozens of files for one small change is a warning sign.

Props make the boundary of a component explicit: to use it, you must know what inputs it needs and what it will do with them. Good components accept only the props they need, give them clear names, and behave sensibly when an optional prop is missing. That explicitness is what lets a teammate use your component without reading its internal code. Do not adopt this idea blindly on every element. Ask whether it earns its place in this particular case: does it make the code clearer, the page faster, or the design more consistent? If the answer is no, plain hand-written code may be the better choice. Good developers choose tools for a reason they can state.

For example, a button component might accept a label and a style variant as props, so <Button label="Save" variant="primary" /> and <Button label="Cancel" variant="secondary" /> produce two buttons from one definition, each with the correct wording and prominence. A subtler mistake is to optimise for the first screen you test and forget the rest. Check a narrow phone width, a large desktop width, and a zoomed-in view. Many front-end bugs only appear at an extreme, and catching them early is much cheaper than explaining them to a user who has already encountered them.

A common mistake is passing too many props, or props that the component merely forwards to another component without using them. That suggests the boundary is in the wrong place and the components need reorganising. To check your own work, open the page, inspect the relevant element in developer tools, and confirm that the styles you expect are actually applied rather than overridden or ignored. Then change the browser width, reload, and confirm nothing has broken. Write down anything surprising; those notes become your personal checklist for the next project.

To make this stick, add the idea to your next practice project in exactly one place, and keep a short note about why you chose it there. Later, when you review the project, that note will tell you whether the choice paid off. Over several projects, those notes become a reliable personal guide to what works for you.

Section 5 — State: what a component remembers

State is the information a component remembers between interactions: whether a menu is open, what a user has typed, which tab is selected, or whether an item is marked as favourite. Unlike props, which come from outside, state lives inside the component and changes in response to user actions. When state changes, the component's appearance should update to match, so the screen always reflects the current state. This idea also affects how other people read your work. A teammate, a future version of yourself, or a reviewer judging a portfolio project should be able to open your code and understand the intention quickly. Clear, consistent choices communicate professionalism even before anyone runs the page.

Understanding state is essential because most interactive behaviour is state plus display. A toggle button is not two different buttons; it is one button whose state is on or off, and whose appearance follows that state. Designing in those terms keeps behaviour predictable: list every state a component can be in, decide what each state looks like, and make sure every action moves the component to the correct next state. Remember accessibility while you work. Visual changes are not only about appearance; they affect whether text remains readable, whether interactive elements remain reachable by keyboard, and whether a screen reader still announces content in a sensible order. Check those things as you go, not as an afterthought at the end.

For example, an accordion section's state might be open or closed, and clicking its heading flips the state, showing or hiding its content. There are only two states to design and test, which is far simpler than treating open and closed as unrelated layouts. Finally, do not confuse looking finished with being finished. A page can look correct in one browser and fail in another, or look correct with short sample text and break with a long real heading. Test with realistic content, realistic text lengths, and at least one other browser before you call the work done.

A common mistake is storing the same information in two places, so that one copy changes and the other does not, and the screen shows a contradiction. Keep each piece of state in exactly one place. A good self-check is to explain the idea aloud in one or two sentences without looking at this section. If you can do that and give a correct example, you understand it well enough to use it. If you cannot, re-read the example and try building a tiny version from scratch before moving on.

Practice deliberately: create a small test page, apply the idea, break it on purpose by changing one value, and then fix it. The break-and-fix cycle teaches you the boundaries of the idea far faster than reading alone, and it builds the calm debugging habit that professional work depends on.

Section 6 — Composition: components inside components

Composition means building larger components from smaller ones, like nesting boxes inside boxes. A page is composed of sections, a section is composed of cards, and a card is composed of an image, a heading, and a button. Each level only needs to understand the level directly below it, which keeps any single component small enough to understand and test on its own. In plain terms, this idea exists because real projects grow. A page that starts with a handful of styles can end up with hundreds, and without a clear approach the stylesheet becomes hard to read, hard to change, and easy to break by accident. Composition is more flexible than inheritance or copying, because the same small components can be combined in different ways for different pages. A button component can appear in a card, a form, a modal, and a navigation bar without being rewritten, and improving the button improves all of those places at once. Section 7 — Variants: one component, many forms

A variant is a recognised form of a component, such as a primary and a secondary button, a large and a small card, or an error and a success alert. Variants share the component's structure and behaviour but differ in appearance or emphasis, and they are chosen by a prop or a modifier class. Naming variants explicitly prevents the slow invention of near-duplicate components that differ only in colour or size. Think of this as a working habit rather than a piece of trivia. You will use it on almost every page you build, and the benefit appears gradually: files stay shorter, decisions become easier to explain to a teammate, and fixing a visual bug takes minutes instead of an afternoon of hunting through unrelated rules.

Variants should be few and meaningful. A button with primary, secondary, and danger variants covers most needs; a button with twelve variants suggests the design system has no hierarchy. Each variant should exist because a real use case needs it, not because it seemed possible. Document the variants and when to use each one, so a team chooses consistently. When you apply this idea, keep the surrounding code in mind. Test the whole page, not just the fragment you edited.

For example, a primary button is the main action on a view, a secondary button is an alternative action, and a danger button is a destructive action. Using those three consistently teaches users what to expect before they read the button's text. Section 8 — Slots and children

Slots, also called children, are places inside a component where the user of the component can supply their own content. A modal component, for example, might define its frame, its close button, and its overlay, but leave the body as a slot that each use fills with different content. This makes the component flexible without making it complicated: the frame is fixed, the content varies. The reason this matters for a beginner is that early choices compound. If you adopt a messy approach on a small practice page, the same approach will feel natural on a larger project, where its cost is much higher. Learning the disciplined version now saves you from having to unlearn a habit later, when a real deadline is involved.

Slots are the answer to a common design tension. If a component accepts only fixed props, it cannot handle content that is itself made of components, such as a card body containing a list and a button. If it accepts arbitrary children, it can, while still guaranteeing the surrounding structure. Learning when to use a prop and when to use a slot is a key component skill: props for simple values, slots for rich, structured content. A practical test is to change one thing deliberately. Alter a value, remove a class, or rename something, then refresh and watch what breaks. That experiment teaches you what actually depends on the idea, which is far more useful than memorising a definition. Undo the change afterwards and confirm the page returns to normal.

For example, a layout component might have a header slot, a main slot, and a footer slot, so different pages supply different headers while sharing the same overall structure. The layout guarantees the arrangement; the slots supply the content. Watch out for overuse. A technique that helps in moderation can hurt when applied everywhere: too many classes, too many wrappers, or too many overrides all add weight and confusion. If a page feels complicated, look for the simplest version that still meets the design, and prefer that version.

A common mistake is making every part of a component a slot, which leaves the component with no identity of its own. Section 9 — Presentational and container components

A useful distinction separates presentational components, which focus on how things look, from container components, which focus on data and behaviour. A presentational component receives data through props and renders it, without knowing where the data came from. A container component fetches or manages data and passes it down to presentational components, without caring much about the exact markup. A useful way to judge this idea is to ask what happens when the design changes. Clients and designers change colours, spacing, and layouts often. An approach that survives those changes without a rewrite is a good one; an approach that forces you to edit dozens of files for one small change is a warning sign.

This separation is not a strict rule, but a habit that keeps code understandable. Presentational components are easy to test and reuse, because they depend only on their props. Container components are easy to reason about, because their job is data and state, not layout details. When a component mixes both concerns heavily, changes to data logic risk breaking the layout, and vice versa. Do not adopt this idea blindly on every element. Ask whether it earns its place in this particular case: does it make the code clearer, the page faster, or the design more consistent? If the answer is no, plain hand-written code may be the better choice. Good developers choose tools for a reason they can state.

For example, a user list container might load a list of users and pass it to a presentational list component, which renders each user as a card. The card knows nothing about loading or errors; the container knows nothing about card spacing. A subtler mistake is to optimise for the first screen you test and forget the rest. Check a narrow phone width, a large desktop width, and a zoomed-in view. Section 10 — Breaking a design into components

Breaking a design into components starts with the visual design, not with code. Look at the design and identify repeated elements first, because repetition is the strongest signal of a component. Then identify large, distinct regions, such as the header and footer, which are components even if they appear once, because they have a clear job and boundary. Then look for elements that change state, such as menus and tabs, because stateful pieces deserve their own components. This idea also affects how other people read your work. A teammate, a future version of yourself, or a reviewer judging a portfolio project should be able to open your code and understand the intention quickly. Clear, consistent choices communicate professionalism even before anyone runs the page.

A practical method is to draw boxes on the design, name each box, and list its props: what varies between uses, and what stays the same. If two boxes share most of their structure but differ in a few values, they are one component with props. If a box contains several distinct boxes, it is a composed component. This exercise, done before coding, prevents a great deal of rework. Remember accessibility while you work. Visual changes are not only about appearance; they affect whether text remains readable, whether interactive elements remain reachable by keyboard, and whether a screen reader still announces content in a sensible order. Check those things as you go, not as an afterthought at the end.

For example, given a news homepage design, you might identify a header, a featured story, a story card used many times, a newsletter signup, and a footer. The story card is the obvious reusable component; the featured story may be a variant of it. Finally, do not confuse looking finished with being finished. A page can look correct in one browser and fail in another, or look correct with short sample text and break with a long real heading. Test with realistic content, realistic text lengths, and at least one other browser before you call the work done.

A common mistake is starting to code before identifying components, and then discovering halfway through that a repeated pattern was built three different ways. Ten minutes of boxing saves hours of refactoring. Section 11 — Component boundaries and APIs

A component's API is the set of props, slots, and events it offers to the rest of the system, and designing that API well is the heart of component thinking. A good API is small, clear, and hard to misuse: prop names say what they mean, required props are few, and the component behaves sensibly when optional props are omitted. A poor API is large, ambiguous, and full of special cases that only its author understands. In plain terms, this idea exists because real projects grow. A page that starts with a handful of styles can end up with hundreds, and without a clear approach the stylesheet becomes hard to read, hard to change, and easy to break by accident. The idea in this section gives you a rule you can apply consistently, so that a change in one place does not produce a surprise somewhere else on the site.

Think of a component API as a promise to future users, including yourself. Changing it later breaks every use, so invest thought early in names and defaults. Ask: what would a new team member expect this prop to be called? What is the most common use, and does it require the fewest props? Can this component be used without reading its source code? If the answers are unsatisfying, revise the API before the component spreads. In practice, start small and observe the result before adding more. Section 12 — Reusability versus over-abstraction

Reusability is valuable, but over-abstraction, extracting a component before a pattern has genuinely repeated, is a real cost. A component extracted too early often has an awkward API, because its designer guessed at what would vary instead of observing it. When the second use arrives, the abstraction may not fit, and the developer faces a choice between contorting the new use or rewriting the component. Both waste the time the abstraction was meant to save. Think of this as a working habit rather than a piece of trivia. You will use it on almost every page you build, and the benefit appears gradually: files stay shorter, decisions become easier to explain to a teammate, and fixing a visual bug takes minutes instead of an afternoon of hunting through unrelated rules.

A sensible rule is to wait until a pattern has appeared two or three times before extracting it, and to extract from real examples rather than from imagination. This is sometimes called the rule of three. It keeps components grounded in actual needs, and it means the first two uses can be simple and slightly duplicated, which is cheaper than a wrong abstraction. When you apply this idea, keep the surrounding code in mind. A front-end page is a system: markup, styles, and behaviour interact, and a choice that looks good in isolation can create a problem elsewhere, such as overflowing content on a narrow screen or a colour that fails a contrast check. Test the whole page, not just the fragment you edited.

For example, if two pages have similar but not identical banners, build both directly first. When a third banner appears, the common structure and the real variations will be obvious, and the extracted component will fit all three. Section 13 — Component naming

Component names matter because they are how a team talks about the interface. A good name says what the component is or does, in words the team already uses: ArticleCard, SearchBox, SiteHeader. A poor name is vague, such as Box or Wrapper, or technical, such as DivContainer, because it tells a reader nothing about the component's purpose. Names should be consistent in style, usually starting with a capital letter and using the project's language for the domain. The reason this matters for a beginner is that early choices compound. If you adopt a messy approach on a small practice page, the same approach will feel natural on a larger project, where its cost is much higher. Learning the disciplined version now saves you from having to unlearn a habit later, when a real deadline is involved.

Naming also reveals design problems. If you cannot name a component clearly, its job may be unclear, and it may be doing too much or too little. Renaming is cheap early and expensive late, so treat a naming struggle as a signal to rethink the boundary, not as a trivial wording problem. Keep a list of component names in the project, so new components do not duplicate an existing name with a different meaning. A practical test is to change one thing deliberately. Then rebuild one repeated pattern, such as a card or a form field, as a single consistent block used everywhere it appears, and test the rebuilt page with long titles, missing images, and an empty list to confirm its states behave sensibly.

Chapter questions

  1. What is the difference between props and state, and why does that distinction matter for debugging?
  1. A component is used in two places with different content. Should that difference be handled with props, with children, or by making two components? Explain your reasoning.
  1. Why is it risky to extract a component before its pattern has repeated, and what rule of thumb helps you decide when to extract?
  1. Two sibling components must stay in sync. Where should their shared state live, and why?
  1. A list reorders incorrectly when sorted. What is the most likely cause, and how would you fix it?
