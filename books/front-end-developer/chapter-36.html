Chapter 36 — SEO and Social Sharing for Front-End

Section 1 — What search engines see

When a search engine visits your page, it does not admire your design the way a human visitor does. It reads the underlying HTML, follows links, and tries to work out what the page is about, which parts are most important, and whether the page is worth showing to someone searching for that topic. A page that hides its real content inside images, or that only reveals its text after complicated scripts run, can therefore be hard for a search engine to understand, even if it looks perfect in a browser. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs.

First the search engine discovers the page, usually by following a link or reading a sitemap. Then it reads and stores the page, a step called indexing, and later it ranks that stored page against other pages when a person searches. Ranking depends on many signals, including relevance, quality, speed, mobile usability and links from other sites, and no single trick controls it. Your job as a front-end developer is to make the first two steps easy and honest, so the page can be found and understood. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour.

Imagine a simple guide page whose heading, first paragraph and links are all plain HTML. A search engine can read the heading, understand the topic, and follow the links without guessing.

<h1>How to prune a rose bush</h1><p>Prune in late winter, cutting dead wood first.</p><a href="/guides/tools">Tools you need</a>

Keep examples short in real work too: a short, clear example is easier to test, easier to reuse and easier for a teammate to understand. The example is deliberately simple, because the idea is the point; once the idea is clear you can adapt it to longer, messier real content.

A common mistake is that the search engine sees only a blank page because the real content is loaded late by script, so put the main content in the HTML itself and treat script-loaded content as an enhancement, not as the only copy. It helps to keep a short note of mistakes you have made with this idea, since the same few mistakes tend to recur across projects. A second pair of eyes helps here: explain what you expected and what you got, and the gap between the two often points straight at the fault.

To check your own work, open the page, view its source, and confirm the heading and first paragraph appear there without running extra steps. A good check is specific: it names what should happen, what you observed, and whether the two match. Repeat the check after any later change, because a change elsewhere can quietly break something that previously worked.

Section 2 — Titles that describe the page

Every page has a title element in its head, and that title is often the first thing a person sees in search results, in a browser tab and in a bookmark list. A good title names the page's topic plainly, in the words a visitor would actually use, and it distinguishes this page from every other page on the site. A vague title such as Home or Page 1 tells nobody anything, while a title that is stuffed with repeated keywords looks untrustworthy and is hard to read. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer.

Write one title per page, put the most important words near the start, and keep it short enough to display fully in most search results, which in practice means roughly a single clear phrase rather than a long sentence. Check that each page on the site has a different title, because duplicate titles make pages hard to tell apart in results and in tabs. The title should match the page's heading in meaning, even if the wording is not identical, so a visitor who clicks through does not feel misled. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system.

For a guide about pruning roses, a clear title names the task and the plant, rather than naming the site first or repeating the word rose many times.

<title>How to prune a rose bush | Garden Guides</title>

The example is deliberately simple, because the idea is the point; once the idea is clear you can adapt it to longer, messier real content. After you run an example, check the result the way a visitor would, not only the way a developer would, because the visitor's view is the one that counts.

A common mistake is writing the same title on every page of a site, so give each page its own title that describes that page alone, and check the tab text as you browse the site. A second pair of eyes helps here: explain what you expected and what you got, and the gap between the two often points straight at the fault. Do not assume the tool is broken; first assume a small, ordinary error in your own code, and check the simplest explanations first.

Section 3 — Meta descriptions that invite a click

A meta description is a short summary of a page, written in the head of the document, which search engines may show underneath the title in results. It does not directly decide where a page ranks, but it strongly affects whether a person chooses to click, because it is the page's chance to explain, in one or two sentences, what the visitor will find and why it is worth their time. A missing, duplicated or misleading description wastes that chance. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks.

Write the description as a genuine summary, not as a list of keywords and not as a promise the page does not keep. Mention the main benefit or answer, use natural language, and keep it to about two sentences so it is not cut off awkwardly in results. Each page deserves its own description, just as each page deserves its own title, because two different pages that share a description will look identical to a searcher even when their contents differ. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once.

A rose-pruning guide might summarise when to prune, what to cut first, and what the reader will be able to do afterwards, in plain sentences.

<meta name="description" content="Learn when to prune roses, which stems to cut first, and how to shape the bush for healthy growth.">

If the example does not behave as expected, resist the urge to add more code; first check the basics, such as spelling, names and whether the file was saved. Read the example line by line and ask what each part is for, rather than memorising the whole block as one shape.

A common mistake is copying the same description onto every page, so write a fresh summary for each page, and read it as if you were deciding whether to click. Testing early, with a small case, catches these mistakes while they are still cheap and easy to fix. Most mistakes with this idea come from rushing past the basics, not from the idea being unusually difficult.

To check your own work, read a page's description in search results, or in the page source, and check that it honestly summarises that page. The goal of checking is confidence you can defend, not a quick glance that lets you move on. Make checking part of finishing, not an afterthought, and do it before you move on to the next idea.

Section 4 — Semantic HTML for search

Semantic HTML means choosing elements for what content actually is, rather than wrapping everything in generic containers. A navigation element says this is navigation, an article element says this is a self-contained piece of writing, a heading says this is a heading, and a list says this is a list. Search engines, screen readers and other tools all use that meaning to understand a page's structure, so semantic markup helps both people and machines make sense of your work. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent.

Start with the page outline: one main landmark for the main content, navigation for the site's menus, a header for introductory material and a footer for closing material. Inside the main content, use article or section elements for distinct blocks, headings in order, and real lists for lists. Avoid using a heading only to make text bigger, and avoid using a division for everything, because both habits throw away meaning that tools rely on. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened.

A blog post marked up as an article, with its heading, paragraphs and list of steps inside it, is far clearer than the same words wrapped in unnamed containers.

<article><h2>Pruning steps</h2><ol><li>Remove dead wood.</li><li>Cut crossing stems.</li></ol></article>

Section 5 — Heading structure as an outline

Headings form the outline of a page, in the same way chapter headings form the outline of a book. The level-one heading names the page's main topic, level-two headings name its major sections, and level-three headings name subsections within those. When the outline is clean, a reader can scan it and grasp the page at a glance, and a search engine can see how topics relate to each other instead of seeing a flat pile of equally important text. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for.

Use one level-one heading per page, then step down one level at a time without skipping, for example from a level-two heading to a level-three heading, never straight to level four. Write headings that describe what follows, using words a searcher might use, rather than clever phrases that hide the topic. If you want a heading to look smaller or larger, change it with CSS; never choose a level for its default size, because size is presentation and level is meaning. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once. If you can describe the flow in plain words before you write code, the code itself becomes much easier to write and to debug.

A pruning guide might use one main heading for the whole guide, a second-level heading for timing, another for tools, and third-level headings for individual steps inside the tools section.

<h1>How to prune a rose bush</h1><h2>When to prune</h2><h2>Tools</h2><h3>Choosing secateurs</h3>

A common mistake is skipping heading levels or using several level-one headings on one page, so outline the page on paper first, then mark it up in order, and read the headings alone to see if they make sense. Most mistakes with this idea come from rushing past the basics, not from the idea being unusually difficult. When you spot a mistake, fix the cause rather than covering the symptom, because a covered symptom tends to return in a different form.

To check your own work, read only the headings of a page, in order, and check that they form a sensible outline of the topic. Make checking part of finishing, not an afterthought, and do it before you move on to the next idea. If you cannot explain the result in your own words, treat that as a signal to re-read the section, not as a failure.

Section 6 — Clean, readable URLs

A URL is part of a page's public face: people copy it, share it, bookmark it and judge it. A clean URL uses plain words, separated by hyphens, that describe the page, such as a guide path naming the topic. A messy URL full of numbers, meaningless codes and long query strings is harder to share, harder to remember and harder to trust, even when the page behind it is excellent. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. In real projects this idea shows up again and again, in small pages and in large applications alike, so learning it well now saves repeated confusion later.

Keep URLs short, lowercase and stable, because a URL that changes breaks bookmarks, links and search results that point to the old address. Use words that match the page's topic, avoid unnecessary parameters for ordinary pages, and do not include dates or version numbers unless the page genuinely needs them. When a page must move, redirect the old address to the new one, which is covered in a later section, rather than leaving the old address to fail. If you can describe the flow in plain words before you write code, the code itself becomes much easier to write and to debug. Notice the order of the steps: each one depends on the one before it, and skipping a step usually creates a fault that looks mysterious later.

Section 7 — Canonical URLs and duplicates

Sometimes the same content can be reached at more than one address, for example with and without a trailing slash, with a tracking parameter, or through a printed version of a page. To a visitor those addresses look like the same page, but to a search engine they can look like several competing pages, which splits attention and can weaken how the content is understood. A canonical link tells search engines which address is the preferred, official version. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks.

Add one canonical link to the head of each page, pointing to the single address you want search engines to treat as the original. The canonical address should be complete, use the secure form of the address, and match the address you actually link to elsewhere. Canonical tags are a strong hint, not an absolute command, so they work best when the pages really are near-duplicates and when your internal links already point to the preferred address. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once.

A guide that can be reached with a tracking parameter still declares its clean address as canonical, so search engines consolidate their understanding around one version.

<link rel="canonical" href="https://example.com/guides/prune-rose-bush">

Section 8 — Sitemaps for discovery

A sitemap is a file, usually in XML format, that lists the important pages of a site so search engines can discover them more reliably. It is especially helpful for large sites, for new sites with few links pointing to them, and for pages that are buried several clicks deep, because it gives search engines a direct list rather than relying entirely on following links from page to page. A sitemap does not guarantee ranking, but it improves the chance that pages are found and considered. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks.

List the pages you want found, with their full addresses, and keep the list current as pages are added, moved or removed. Do not list pages you do not want indexed, such as private or duplicate pages, and do not list addresses that return errors. Place the sitemap at a standard location and mention it in the robots file, which is the next section's topic, and submit it through the search engine's webmaster tools where available. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once.

A small site's sitemap might list its home page, its guide index and each guide, each with its full address, and nothing else.

<url><loc>https://example.com/guides/prune-rose-bush</loc></url>

Section 9 — Robots files and crawling

A robots file, named robots.txt, sits at the root of a site and gives polite instructions to search engine crawlers about which parts of the site they may visit. It is a request, not a lock: well-behaved crawlers respect it, but it does not hide content from anyone who already knows the address, and it must never be used as the only protection for private material. Used correctly, it helps crawlers spend their time on the pages that matter instead of on admin pages, search results or duplicate views. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent.

Write the file plainly, naming the crawler or all crawlers, then the paths to disallow, and include the sitemap address. Keep it short and specific, test it after any change, and never disallow the whole site by accident, which is a surprisingly common and costly mistake. Remember that a disallowed page can still appear in results in some cases if other sites link to it, so for truly private content, use proper access control instead. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system. When something goes wrong, trace the same steps in reverse, asking at each point what you expected and what actually happened.

A site might allow all crawlers on its public guides while asking them not to crawl its internal search pages or its admin area.

Disallow: /admin/
Disallow: /search/
Sitemap: https://example.com/sitemap.xml

Section 10 — Open Graph basics

When someone shares a link on a social platform, the platform does not guess what to show: it reads special meta tags in the page's head, known as Open Graph tags, and uses them to build the preview card with a title, a description and an image. Without those tags, the platform falls back to whatever it can find, which may be an unhelpful title, a fragment of navigation text or the wrong image. With them, you control the first impression your page makes in a feed. It is worth learning properly rather than copying a snippet and hoping, because a copied snippet hides the choices you will need to make when the real content differs. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer.

The four essential Open Graph tags name the type of content, the title, the description and the page's full address. Each page should have its own values, just as each page has its own title and description for search, because a shared preview that describes the home page will mislead anyone who clicks through to a specific article. Use absolute addresses, meaning the full address including the site name, because a relative address may not resolve when the preview is built elsewhere. A useful habit is to test with realistic content, not only with a perfect example, because long titles, empty lists and slow networks reveal the real behaviour. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system.

A shared guide should preview with its own title, its own short description and the guide's full address, not with generic site-wide text.

<meta property="og:title" content="How to prune a rose bush"><meta property="og:type" content="article"><meta property="og:url" content="https://example.com/guides/prune-rose-bush">

Section 11 — Open Graph images

Of all the parts of a social preview, the image is usually what catches the eye first, and a missing, tiny or badly cropped image can make an otherwise good page look unfinished in a feed. The Open Graph image tag points to a single image that represents the page, and platforms crop it to their own card shapes, so the image must still read clearly after cropping. A strong share image is simple, relevant and readable at small sizes, not a dense collage of tiny text. The benefit is practical, not theoretical: a page that gets this right is easier to maintain, easier to test and easier to explain to another developer. Users rarely notice this when it works, which is exactly the point; they only notice it when it is missing, broken or inconsistent.

Choose an image that is wide rather than tall, large enough to look sharp on high-resolution screens, and hosted at a stable, public, absolute address. Avoid text that sits near the edges, because cropping may cut it off, and avoid images that only make sense with a caption, because the caption may not travel with the card. Use the same image logic for every page, but give each important page its own image where the topic differs, rather than reusing one generic picture everywhere. Keep the responsibility clear: the front end presents and requests, and it should not pretend to decide things that belong elsewhere in the system. Section 12 — Social cards for X

In practice, Open Graph tags already cover most of the preview, and the X-specific tags mainly choose the card size and, optionally, a site handle. The important point is not to memorise every platform's tags, but to understand that each platform builds its preview from tags you provide, and that missing tags mean a weaker, guessed preview. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for. Section 13 — Structured data in plain words

Structured data is extra, machine-readable information added to a page that describes what the page is about in a standard vocabulary, such as saying this page is an article, this is its author, and this is when it was published. Human visitors do not see it directly, but search engines can use it to understand the page more precisely and, in some cases, to show richer results, such as extra details beneath a listing. It is not magic and it does not guarantee a richer result, but it removes ambiguity about the page's meaning. Section 50 — Putting it together: a launch checklist

A launch checklist turns everything in this chapter into a repeatable habit, so no page goes live with a missing title, a broken share image or a blocked crawler by accident. The checklist is not a guarantee of ranking or of viral sharing; it is a safety net that catches ordinary mistakes before visitors and search engines meet them. Teams that use a checklist consistently publish fewer broken pages, and they spend less time fixing problems that a two-minute check would have caught. That matters because the people using the page judge it by what they can see, understand and do, not by how clever the code behind it looks. It also matters for trust: when this part behaves predictably, visitors are more willing to stay, return and complete the task they came for.

Before publishing, confirm the title and description are unique and honest, the headings form an outline, the main content is in the HTML, images have alt text and sensible sizes, the Open Graph tags and image are set, the address is clean and canonical, the sitemap and robots file are correct, and the page works on a phone. After publishing, share the page privately, check its preview, check its indexing status in due course, and fix anything the checks reveal. Keep the checklist short enough to actually use, and update it when you learn a new lesson, so it grows with your experience. Work through it slowly, one step at a time, and check what changes at each step instead of trying to fix everything at once. If you can describe the flow in plain words before you write code, the code itself becomes much easier to write and to debug.
